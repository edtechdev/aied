Educators' Guide to Multimodal Learning and Generative AI

Created: 2026-08-07 | Tags: multimodalgenerative-aiai-literacyhigher-edfaculty-developmentmultimodal-learningguide
Varga-Atkins, Saunders, Beckingham, Hartley, Keshishi, Lacković, Li, Lindsay, Wen & Winder (2025) produced a practical, research-informed guide for educators and educational developers on integrating Generative AI into multimodal teaching, learning, and assessment — grounded in a SEDA-funded study combining a literature review, survey, focus groups, and case-study collection.

Overview

Published by the University of Liverpool and funded by the Staff and Educational Development Association (SEDA), this guide adopts a pragmatic "middle way" between uncritical adoption and outright rejection of GenAI. It draws on Vallor's (2024) framing of AI as a "mirror machine" and Cope & Kalantzis's (2024) concept of cyber-social learning — a collaborative partnership between human and machine intelligence, each with distinct but complementary strengths.

Critical Considerations: The Costs of GenAI

Before offering practical strategies, the guide foregrounds three categories of cost:

Environmental Costs

  • Generating multimodal content (images, video, audio) requires significantly more energy than text-only generation (Luccioni et al., 2024)
  • Energy data from GenAI providers is difficult to extract, but the evidence base confirms substantial environmental impact

Costs to Knowledge

  • Over-reliance on GenAI can harm knowledge acquisition and retention by removing the process of sourcing knowledge (Yan et al., 2025)
  • MIT research: students who clarify their own understanding before consulting GenAI experience major benefits (Naughton, 2025)

Costs to Future Jobs

  • Tomlinson et al. (2025) devised an "AI applicability score" showing highest vulnerability in knowledge work, computing, and administrative occupations
  • Implication: educators must foster agency and evaluative judgement, not just prompt-crafting skills

AI Literacy Across Four Scales

The guide maps AI literacy development onto a four-scale framework:

Level Key Strategies
Individual Workshops on creative multimodal tasks, prompt-crafting practice, reflective assignments documenting AI use; educators experiment in low-stakes settings
Module Embed GenAI literacy into learning outcomes; offer optional multimodal tasks with clear rubrics; include creative and reflective components where students critique GenAI outputs
Programme Cross-module policies on GenAI use; workshops and discussion tasks for consistency; align GenAI practices with graduate attributes (criticality, creativity, digital fluency)
Institutional Clear policies with checklists for permissible uses; vetted tools and data privacy protocols; flexible guidance over rigid mandates (e.g., Traffic Light model)

Multimodal Learning Designs with GenAI

The guide provides specific activity designs that move beyond text-to-text interaction:

  • Creating visual content: Using GenAI to generate images, infographics, and visual aids — then critically evaluating outputs for bias and accuracy
  • Integrating GenAI into teaching delivery: Demonstrating GenAI use live in class ("mirror AI use"), showing the process of prompting, critiquing, and refining outputs
  • Co-creating multimodal output: Students collaborate with GenAI to produce videos, presentations, or digital artefacts, then reflect on the cyber-social partnership
  • Just-in-time support: GenAI as a study companion that explains concepts across modalities (text-to-diagram, text-to-audio)

Benefits and Opportunities

From survey and focus-group data with educators:

Benefit Detail
Expanded pedagogy Moving beyond text-centric formats to video, podcasts, infographics, simulations — making learning more inclusive and engaging
Lowered barriers GenAI helps educators without design/media skills to prototype materials and experiment with new formats
Creative ideation AI as a creative partner for brainstorming, divergent thinking, and breaking out of habitual pedagogical patterns
Efficiency Generating quiz questions, reformatting content, creating draft scripts — freeing time for "relationship-rich pedagogy"
Future readiness Equipping students with prompt engineering, critical evaluation, ethical decision-making, and reflective AI use skills

Five Practical Tips for Educators

  1. Save time with smart AI use — use GenAI for drafting, transposition between formats, and content adaptation
  2. Mirror AI use: show your process — demonstrate GenAI use live, including failures and critical evaluation
  3. Use checklists for ethical and sustainable AI use — guide students through ethical considerations before and after GenAI tasks
  4. Design for human-AI collaboration, not substitution — GenAI generates raw material; educators and learners bring vision, critique, and meaning-making
  5. Teach AI literacy as a core skill — embed critical AI evaluation across the curriculum rather than as a standalone topic
