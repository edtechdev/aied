Trust-Utility Gap in Introductory Physics Education

Created: 2026-08-08 | Tags: physics-educationtrust-calibrationai-literacystudent-experiencehigher-edstem-educationsurveystudent-ai-interactiontechnology-adoption
Fouad & Bentley (2026) survey 81 introductory physics-education students and find a striking 50-percentage-point trust-utility gap: 91% use AI for coursework but only 41% trust AI physics explanations — evidence of domain-calibrated skepticism. Students spontaneously identified AI failure modes (visual-spatial reasoning, circuits, abstract reasoning) that align with known benchmark results, and 65% prefer optional over mandatory AI integration.

Context

As AI becomes pervasive in stem-education, understanding student-experience is critical — especially in physics, where the discipline's distinctive epistemic demands (abstract reasoning, visual-spatial interpretation, mathematical formalism) create unique challenges for ai-literacy.

The Trust-Utility Gap

The headline finding: 91% of students use AI for physics coursework, but only 41% trust AI-generated physics explanations. This 50-percentage-point gap represents domain-calibrated skepticism — students are sophisticated users who know where AI fails in physics, not uncritical adopters. They spontaneously identified failure modes:

  • Visual-spatial reasoning: Interpreting diagrams, graphs, and free-body diagrams
  • Circuit analysis: Tracing current through multiloop circuits
  • Abstract physical reasoning: Dimensional analysis, conceptual relationships

Notably, these failure modes align with Kortemeyer's benchmarking results showing that AI performance is weakest on multirepresentational tasks — suggesting students' trust-calibration tracks AI competence boundaries in physics.

Key Findings

  • 50-point trust-utility gap (91% use vs 41% trust); Cohen's κ = 0.78 for thematic coding
  • 65% prefer optional AI integration; high-performing students overrepresented (13.9 pp) — preference may be inflated
  • Verification gap identified: disconnect between self-reported and actual verification behavior
  • Domain-specific skepticism, not naive acceptance — contrasts with general over-reliance findings
  • Implications for teacher-role: optional AI integration may preserve student agency and critical engagement
