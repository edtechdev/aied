> **Group assessment** — graded work produced and submitted by a team rather than an individual, used to develop collaboration, communication, and shared responsibility while also generating a grade for each member. It is the setting where [[assessment|assessment design]] and [[collaborative-learning|collaborative learning]] meet, and it inherits the tensions of both: free-riding and social loafing, unequal contributions, conflict avoidance, and the problem of attributing collective output to individual learning. [[generative-ai|Generative AI]] adds a further layer, because teams must now negotiate whose and what kind of AI [[student-engagement|engagement]] counts as acceptable — a negotiation that [[agency]] [[research-methods-aied|research]] shows can go in opposite directions within the same cohort.

## Questions to Consider

- What is a group assessment actually measuring — the group's output, each member's learning, or their ability to collaborate? If those three can diverge, which one does your grading claim to warrant?
- Think of a group project you have been part of. Who did the work, who decided what counted as good work, and who stayed quiet? How much of that was the task design rather than the people?
- Research on group work finds students often decline to hold free-riders accountable in order to protect relationships. When a group member uses [[generative-ai|GenAI]] to produce their section, is that free-riding — or is the judgment more complicated than the word suggests?
- If every group in a cohort prompts the same [[conversational-ai|chatbot]] with the same brief, what happens to originality and to the diversity of perspectives that group work is supposed to produce?
- A collective permissive climate can make heavy AI use feel safer than in individual work, because the risk is shared. What does that tell you about using group norms as a way to encourage [[ai-literacy|responsible AI use]]?
- If an AI tool mediated your group's discussion — summarising, synthesising, or challenging your ideas — would that deepen the collaboration or replace the difficult parts of it? How would you tell the difference?

## Introduction

Group assessment occupies a distinctive place in [[higher-ed|higher education]] and [[professional-training|professional training]]: it is valued for building collaboration, communication, and a sense of shared responsibility, and it is pedagogically central in professionally oriented programmes such as [[teacher-education|teacher education]]. Its promise is not automatically realised, however. Both [[teacher-role|teachers]] and students report obstacles to effective collaboration, and the graded group product has to carry two different kinds of claim at once — about what the group accomplished and about what each individual learned. AI, and [[generative-ai|generative AI]] in particular, presses on both claims: it changes how work can be divided, how easily contributions can be fused, and how each member's thinking can be distinguished from a tool's output.

## Why group assessment is difficult by design

- **Free-riding and social loafing.** Some members reduce their contribution and the workload is redistributed onto more engaged peers. Task scope, group size, and [[writing-education|composition]] raise the likelihood; accountability mechanisms and group efficacy reduce it.
- **Accountability is relational as well as structural.** Research in Hong Kong, where the study below was conducted, found undergraduates often chose *not* to report free-riding in order to protect interpersonal relationships — completing the work themselves instead of confronting the problem. Students' [[agency]] can therefore take the form of pursuing the grade while avoiding the conflict.
- **The collaboration has to be designed, not assumed.** Where a task can be partitioned into independent subtasks, students take that route, and the group becomes a submission format rather than a joint intellectual endeavour.
- **Process versus product.** Group assessment is strongest when the process is assessed as well as the output, which requires [[peer-review|peer review]] or other visible intermediates rather than a single final artefact.
- **[[equity-in-ai-education|Equity]] and [[inclusive-learning|inclusion]].** Contribution norms, language, and confidence distribute unequally inside groups; assessments that reward the loudest coordinator rather than the collective reasoning reproduce those inequalities.

## What AI changes in group assessment

Research on AI in group work has mostly addressed the technical layer — algorithmic group formation, AI-generated feedback for groups, [[learning-analytics|analytics]] that help teachers spot struggling groups, personalised assessment to lift group performance. A smaller, newer body of work examines what students actually do, and it complicates the assumption that AI simply makes group work easier or more dishonest.

**Three patterns of agency instead of one spectrum.** [[chen-zou-genai-group-assessment-agency-2026|Chen and Zou (2026)]] interviewed 15 focus groups (52 pre-service teachers) about an authentic group presentation in a multicultural education course, and found three distinct patterns rather than a single enthusiasm-to-avoidance axis:

| Pattern | Groups | Practice | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cooperation-oriented agency | 5 | Intensified GenAI use | Coherence, performance, shared norms lowering perceived risk |
| Normative agency | 7 | Deliberately restrained use | Authenticity, [[bias-mitigation|fairness]], originality, diversity of perspectives |
| Non-enacted agency | 3 | Unchanged from individual work | Task partitioned; collective interaction viewed as costly |

- **GenAI as coordination infrastructure.** Students intensified use to bridge a familiar [[collaborative-learning|collaboration]] problem: not knowing what peers' sections contained. Feeding those sections into a chatbot to decode and align them made distributed knowledge mutually intelligible, and one group rebuilt its workflow as "discussion → externalisation to GenAI → collective review → re-discussion." The authors read this as going beyond [[cognitive-offloading|cognitive offloading]] — judgement stayed with students while the tool absorbed coordination work — while warning that the smoother workflow may bypass the disagreement through which cohesion is built.
- **Group norms can invert accountability.** A permissive collective climate lowered the perceived risk of [[ai-misuse-learning-harm|misuse]] ("it is more comfortable because besides me, everyone in my group is using GenAI"), turning shared norms into shared risk management rather than commitment to learning.
- **Restraint is also agentic.** Students who cut their GenAI use did so to protect the task's [[situated-learning|situated]] knowledge ("AI only knows that moment when you type"), to avoid effectively free-riding on their groupmates, to preserve the originality that distinguishes groups from one another, and to keep the diversity of perspectives the group already had. The authors distinguish this normative [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulation]] from mere compliance or risk avoidance, and caution that both look similar from outside.
- **Capability does not become collective on its own.** In three groups, GenAI practice never changed from individual work; individually capable students never pooled what they knew, even when coherence was an explicit assessment criterion.
- **AI as mediator of group process.** Beyond the tool's content role, AI systems increasingly mediate group interaction itself — surfacing assumptions in interdisciplinary teams, tracking dyadic state in pair programming, or producing non-evaluative classroom-level views of discourse quality — which raises the question of whether machine mediation substitutes for or [[scaffolding|scaffolds]] the relational labour group assessment is meant to develop.

## Design implications

- **Make the AI-use negotiation an assessable outcome.** Requiring teams to justify and document how GenAI will and will not be used turns an implicit peer norm into explicit, gradeable reasoning, instead of leaving it to perceived risk.
- **Assess process, not only product.** [[peer-review|Peer review]], intermediate deliverables, and reflective contributions create the interactions through which norms and shared understanding are actually constructed.
- **Do not rely on a coherence criterion alone.** A rubric line demanding integration does not by itself produce collaboration if the task structure still permits divide-and-conquer; the workflow has to require joint work.
- **Recognise that heavier AI use is not automatically less learning.** In the study above, intensified use coexisted with retained student judgement, while not using AI was sometimes caution rather than engagement. Read the pattern, not the volume.
- **Design for how students read the assessment, not only what it says.** The same instruction was read as permission, as a warning, and as evidence that AI was ineffective, and those readings shaped practice more than the policy text.
