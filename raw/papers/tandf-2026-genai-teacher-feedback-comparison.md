# Comparing Generative AI and teacher feedback: student perceptions of usefulness and trustworthiness

Michael Henderson, Margaret Bearman, Jennifer Chung, Tim Fawns, Simon Buckingham Shum, Kelly E. Matthews & Jimena de Mello Heredia (Monash University; Deakin University; University of Technology Sydney; University of Queensland)

Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, 51(5), 863-878, published online 13 May 2025. Open Access (CC BY-NC-ND 4.0). doi:10.1080/02602938.2025.2502582

ABSTRACT

The rapid integration of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) into
educational contexts has presented both opportunities and challenges
for students seeking and using feedback. While AI-generated feedback
can offer increased access, timely responses and personalised insights,
concerns about the quality of AI-generated feedback still persist, including issues of bias, factual inaccuracies, and homogenisation. This study
investigates how students use, value and trust AI-generated feedback
compared to feedback from educators. This paper draws on a large-scale
cross-sectional survey administered across four major Australian universities. A quantitative analysis of 6960 respondents revealed that half of the
students sought feedback from GenAI. Overall, students reported teacher
feedback to be more helpful, and especially trustworthy. This paper also
reports on the thematic analysis of 8642 open-ended responses, providing deeper insights into students’ experiences. This includes findings that
students valued the feedback from GenAI because of its ease of access,
timeliness, volume, understandability and that it was perceived to be less
risky than seeking feedback from teachers. At the same time, students
were concerned about GenAI’s reliability as well as contextual and disciplinary expertise. We argue that GenAI and teacher feedback appear to
serve different needs, and therefore are complementary but not
interchangeable.

Introduction
In recent decades, there has been a paradigm shift in feedback scholarship, with a focus on
feedback as a student-centred process (Carless 2015). Along with this shift has been the recognition that we need to pay attention to not just the nature of the feedback information, but also
how and why students engage with it, including how they make sense of and use this information. The rapid adoption of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) therefore has significant
implications for feedback in higher education. For example, a growing body of research indicates
AI-generated feedback has the potential to provide timely and personalised feedback information

However, it is also well recognised that GenAI’s statistical genesis promotes biases and other
limitations, including the risk of factual inaccuracies, hallucinations, cultural colonisation, and a
tendency towards ‘expected’ ideas and expressions (Creely and Henderson 2025; Gašević, Siemens

better understand how and why students use, value and trust AI-generated feedback compared
to feedback from educators. To address this need, this Australian cross-institutional large-scale
study employs a survey methodology to describe students’ experience of self-generated GenAI
feedback, and how this compares with teacher feedback.

Feedback and AI
Feedback is a contested topic in educational research. Broadly speaking, feedback can be seen
as: information to a learner about their work or performance; or the interactions surrounding this
information; or as the impact that this information has upon the learner. Carless (2015) calls the
latter approach ‘new paradigm’ and this is exemplified by the definition: ‘Feedback is a process
in which learners make sense of information about their performance and use it to enhance the
quality of their work or learning strategies.’ (Henderson, Phillips, et al., 2019, p. 1402). This study
has been informed by this definition, but we also recognise that our respondents most likely
think of feedback as the information they receive from an appraisal of their work. Irrespective of
how feedback is understood, feedback can have a powerful influence on student learning and
experience, but it is also complex and difficult to implement (Henderson, Ryan, et al., 2019;
Leenknecht and Carless 2023).
In part to address these concerns, there is a rising emphasis on how a learner agentically
seeks and uses information about their performance. Thus, the concept of ‘student feedback literacy’ may be particularly relevant to how learners interact with GenAI, as they may frequently
do so without any guidance from a teacher. Carless and Boud (2018) conceptualise feedback
literacy as having three aspects that directly influence the learner’s actions, roughly: understanding feedback processes, making evaluative judgements, and managing ‘feelings, emotions and
attitudes’ (p. 1317). In other words, how the student feels matters. Interestingly, a realist review
of feedback on written tasks suggests that, while negative emotions can reduce motivation, such
effects can be mitigated through building interpersonal trust and relatedness (Ajjawi et al. 2022).
This highlights the potential complexity of GenAI feedback in an already complex space.
While studies that explore student perspectives are well established in the broader feedback
research, they are largely absent from studies examining GenAI feedback (Nazaretsky et al. 2024).
In their systematic review Shi and Aryadoust (2024) note that there is a growing body of research
exploring the effectiveness of GenAI feedback, but there is a need for research that explores
more complex issues around feedback such as anxiety, motivation, self-regulation and factors
that influence perception and use. While less prolific, there are some interesting glimpses of the
ways in which student perspectives are being explored, or at least, being identified as significant
moderators of GenAI feedback experience. For example, Lee and Moore (2024) systematic review
identified several studies that demonstrated GenAI feedback could serve multiple instructional
purposes and roles, including dialogue partner, teaching assistant and emotional support. Another
example is Ziqi et al. (2024) study which highlighted significant barriers to effective uptake of
GenAI feedback by students, including perceptions of vagueness and irrelevance.
There is also a small but interesting number of studies that directly compare GenAI and human
feedback. A number of studies have shown that the use of GenAI feedback had a positive impact
on student performance (Shi and Aryadoust 2024; Wang 2024), and were ‘in some cases even comparable to well-trained human evaluators’ (Nazaretsky et al. 2024, p. 285). In addition to supporting
improved writing, Wang (2024) found that learners who received GenAI feedback rated lowest on
anxiety scales, followed by the teachers’ group. However, there are also studies that are not so

positive. In their systematic review of AI-based written feedback, Shi and Aryadoust (2024) stated:
‘no conclusive evidence was found to support the effectiveness of [Automated Written Feedback]
in comparison to teacher feedback’ (p. 202). Interestingly, they note that this does not necessarily
suggest that GenAI feedback is not useful, but that it may indicate that GenAI and Teacher feedback may serve different needs and therefore direct comparisons may be misleading.
Nazaretsky et al. (2024) study asked students to evaluate feedback in authentic educational
settings both before and after disclosing that the feedback had been created by a teacher or
GenAI. They found that disclosing the identity of the feedback provider affected students’ perceptions, revealing that students preferred human-created feedback and rated AI-generated feedback as being less credible than feedback provided by their teachers. This echoes studies that
show that, when students are given exactly the same feedback will regard it less highly if they
think it is computer-generated, rather than from a human (Lipnevich and Smith 2008).
In light of the challenges with feedback, as well as the somewhat limited and contradictory
studies emerging around GenAI-generated feedback, it is valuable to understand at scale—across
discipline and across university—how university students perceive and interact with AI-generated
feedback. Furthermore, it is useful to compare students’ perceptions of Generative AI feedback
with their perceptions of comments provided by their teachers. For instance, do students treat
AI and teacher-generated feedback with similar caution or criticality? How are they emotionally
impacted, and how do they navigate this? Such comparisons may lead to understanding how,
when and why AI and/or teacher-generated feedback could be used to support student learning.

Method
This paper reports on a subsection of a large-scale, cross-sectional survey that explored university
students’ use and perceived usefulness of AI for learning. In particular, this paper focuses on the survey items that probe for students’ perceptions of AI-generated feedback compared to feedback from
their educators. Specifically, the survey asks students if they have used GenAI for feedback and their
perceptions of the helpfulness and trustworthiness of feedback from both AI and teachers. Additionally,
open-ended questions ask students to describe the differences between the feedback received from
AI and their teachers, including any differences in emotional impact and effects on learning.

Participants
Participation in this study was voluntary and anonymous. Approximately 192,000 students from
four Australian universities (Deakin University, Monash University, University of Queensland,
University of Technology Sydney) were invited to participate in the online survey, and 5% (n =
10,132) of students volunteered. From those, 79% (n = 8028) completed the survey. This paper
specifically draws on data from 6960 students who responded to the survey item regarding using
GenAI for feedback purposes. In brief, 57% were women, 72% were aged between 18 and
24 years, and 58% spoke English as the main language at home. The sample comprised 89% full
time, 58% domestic, 61% undergraduate, and 92% on campus. 36% of students were from
Sciences, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics, 24% from Health, 20% from Humanities and
Social Sciences, and 20% from Business and Law.

Survey
The survey is published under a Creative Commons BY-NC-SA 4.0 license (Chung et al. 2024). The
survey items were developed through multiple rounds of consultation with the literature and
experts. In addition, the survey underwent a process of piloting and refinement to ensure content validity and usability. Staff and students from the participating universities tested the survey,

In this paper, we explore the nine questions specific to feedback (Table 1). All of the students
who responded to these questions had already indicated that they had previously used GenAI
for their university studies in some way. Before answering the questions about feedback, the
students were provided the following description: ‘Feedback is when you get information that you
are able to use to help you improve your work. This includes getting: suggestions for improving your
work; details of the strengths and weaknesses of your work; suggested text edits; and additional ideas.’
This description is consistent with feedback as a student-centred process but framed in a way for
students to quickly understand.

Data collection
Approval was received from the human research ethics committees from all four universities.
Recruitment of students occurred in different ways across the four universities. In two of the
universities, blanket emails were sent to students, at one university a pop-up was used within
the learning management system, and the final university invited unit/subject coordinators to
post the invitation to their students in their online learning management system. An incentive
was offered to students for participation: the chance to win a $350 gift card voucher (one prize
per university). The survey was available between August and October 2024.

Quantitative analysis
Data was cleaned, processed and analysed using SPSS Version 29. The four feedback survey items
were rated on a 5-point scale and treated as continuous data. Two paired samples t-tests were
conducted to compare the means of the two helpfulness items (between GenAI and teacher) and
similarly, the two trustworthiness items. This determined any statistically significant difference
between pairs of ratings in helpfulness (and trustworthiness) of GenAI and teacher feedback.

to a further round of negotiation. In total, the coders met a further six times to compare coding
and difficult responses. If a new code was proposed, this came back to the full team to discuss
and review the data before being added to the code book. Through this process the initial 18

Results
Quantitative results show that student usage of GenAI for feedback was nearly evenly split, with
49.7% (n = 3461) reporting they sought feedback from GenAI, while 50.3% (n = 3499) did not.
Students who used GenAI for feedback were then asked to compare its feedback to that of their
teachers. 96% of the students (n = 3328) answered these questions. A large majority found both
sources somewhat or very helpful, with 83.9% saying this about AI feedback and 82.2% about
teacher feedback. However, perceptions of trustworthiness were notably different—90.5% rated
teacher feedback as somewhat or very trustworthy, compared to just 60.1% for AI feedback.
Table 2 presents the means, standard deviations, and frequency ratings of helpfulness and trustworthiness. Figure 1 visualises the frequencies presented in Table 2, highlighting that although
most students responded positively to both GenAI and teacher feedback, the latter elicited a
notably stronger positive response, especially with regards to trustworthiness.
A paired samples t-test revealed a statistically significant difference between how helpful students rated GenAI feedback and teacher feedback, with a mean difference of 0.14 (SD = 1.15),
t(3327) = −7.17, p < .001, with a small effect size (Cohen’s d = −0.13). Similarly, the second paired
samples t-test revealed a statistically significant difference between how trustworthy students
rated GenAI feedback and teacher feedback, with a mean difference of 0.93 (SD = 1.09), t(3327) =
−49.30, p < .001, with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = −0.89). Students reported teachers providing
more helpful feedback than GenAI, and similarly rated them as more trustworthy than GenAI.

Exploring why students chose not to use GenAI for feedback
Students who indicated that they had not used GenAI for feedback were asked a single
open-ended question (Table 1: Q43). It should be noted that all of these students had previously
indicated that they had used GenAI in some way for their university studies, but that they did
not use it for feedback purposes. The 2906 comments revealed 16 reasons why they did not use
GenAI for feedback purposes. While half of these appear similar to the characteristics identified
by students who had used GenAI for feedback, the relationship is not necessarily the same. For
example, 59 students who used GenAI for feedback purposes valued it because it reduced the
effort required to do a task (e.g. it did it for them). In contrast, 18 students who did not use
GenAI for feedback indicated that they did not use it because it could reduce the effort that they
felt was an important part of the learning process (Table 5).
The most frequently noted reasons for not using GenAI for feedback related to concerns
about the quality of the information (37.5%), and, in particular, its trustworthiness (28.7%).
Interestingly, concerns with quality, and in particular, reliability, were also the most frequently
noted for those students who did use GenAI for feedback. Another strong theme for those not
using GenAI for feedback was that of feedback processes (29.3%). In particular, there was a large
number of students (n = 957, 28.1%) who indicated that they did not use GenAI for feedback
because they did not know it was possible, or did not know how they might be able to do this.

Discussion
Students who used GenAI feedback
Half of our student participants reported using GenAI for feedback. This is promising because it
reveals a large proportion of students actively engaging in feedback seeking behaviours which is
regularly communicated in feedback literature as a valuable condition that supports effective
feedback (Henderson, Molloy, et al., 2019; Winstone et al. 2017). Our findings highlight that GenAI
feedback is already a common student-initiated practice, and as such there is a strong need for
higher education institutions to consider how they might support the students to most effectively engage in this practice.

With half of the student body using GenAI for feedback, there is an urgent need to understand how useful they find it, why they find it helpful, and if they engage with it in critical ways,
such as judging its trustworthiness. In terms of helpfulness, there is a statistically significant difference (small effect size), in which teachers are rated more helpful. However, this does not
mean that students had a negative appraisal of GenAI’s role in feedback. An overwhelming
majority of the sample reported that feedback from both GenAI and teachers was helpful. When
looking closer the strength of their perceptions is noteworthy: 24.4% of students rated GenAI
feedback as very helpful, compared with 41.3% in relation to teacher feedback. We acknowledge
that these are self-reported perceptions of helpfulness, however, our findings indicate that GenAI
feedback, while helpful, was not seen to be as helpful as teacher feedback. This finding alone
supports the notion that GenAI feedback should not be seen as a replacement for teacher
feedback.
The nature of the helpfulness of these two sources of feedback is not necessarily the same
– our data suggests they are valued for different reasons and are likely to serve different purposes. Overall, students indicated that both GenAI and Teacher feedback had utility and specificity. However, there was a strong indication that GenAI feedback was easier to access (ease, speed,
volume), aided sensemaking insofar as it promoted understanding, and was felt to have some
advantages in relation to some aspects of quality of information (understandable and objective).
These findings affirm and further expand on the literature that claims GenAI can be a valuable
feedback source (e.g. Chan and Hu 2023; Lee and Moore 2024; Shi and Aryadoust 2024). This
finding of students regarding GenAI feedback as more objective compared with teacher feedback
is in contrast with Nazaretsky et al. (2024) who found that students had lower evaluations of
objectivity of AI-generated feedback. They conclude that students have a negative bias against
GenAI as source of feedback information, suggesting a fundamental human preference for personal interaction. Our own data problematizes this broad claim since GenAI feedback is clearly
valued by students for a number of reasons, of which objectivity is only a part.
While GenAI feedback is valued by students in some ways, teacher feedback was also reported
to be more relevant, and contextualised. Similar results were reported by Ziqi et al. (2024), and
appear related to an effect noted in Banihashem et al. (2024) study in which they compared
GenAI and peer feedback. They noted that GenAI produced more extensively descriptive feedback including summary statements, whereas peers were better at identifying the specific issues
that needed improving. This highlights the idea, arguably supported by our data, that teachers
are likely to hold deeply contextual knowledge of the assessment, course and learner, and as a
result are able to identify ‘what matters’. Furthermore, we found that students valued teacher
feedback as being more personal, and expert. This may be connected with Nazaretsky et al. (2024)
findings in which humans were rated as more genuine, and more credible than AI.
These relational, contextually-expert and authoritative factors may underlie the striking difference in how students judged the trustworthiness of GenAI feedback to be less than that of
teacher feedback (statistically significant, with large effect size). However, the experience of
teacher feedback is not always positive. An interesting finding in our study was that GenAI feedback was felt to be less risky (in an interpersonal sense) when compared with teacher feedback.
This aligns with Wang’s (2024) study of language learners which found their anxiety around feedback was lower when feedback is from GenAI rather than a teacher. Our study also found that
GenAI feedback was perceived to be more positive in tone (although this positivity was not
always felt to be a good thing, with some students suggesting it felt disingenuous). On the other
hand, teacher feedback was more likely to be noted as having a negative tone (e.g. dismissive,
insulting) and result in negative feelings. Ajjawi et al. (2022) suggest that the negative emotions
associated with feelings of incompetence, can be mediated by relational engagement. This suggests that teachers do not need to say positive things, but the tenor of their communication
matters. This is noteworthy because negative experiences with feedback can have a large impact
on further engagement (Ryan and Henderson 2018).

These comparisons of GenAI and teacher feedback again draw us to the idea that GenAI and
teacher feedback may both be valued because they meet different needs. This directly challenges
the claims that GenAI feedback is an opportunity for ‘liberating teachers from time-consuming
tasks and allowing them to concentrate on higher-level responsibilities’ (Chan and Tsi 2024, p.2).
While we think arguments for replacement are not well founded, we do think that GenAI has a
potentially valuable role as an additional source of feedback.
Building on this, the literature already provides some insights into how GenAI and teacher
feedback may be complementary rather than competing. For example, previous work has identified useful differences between the two, including the specificity of peer feedback in comparison
to the comprehensive nature of GenAI feedback (Banihashem et al. 2024), the ability for human
feedback to provide mutual recognition of each other as humans in a social world (Corbin, Tai,
and Flenady 2025), a higher student agency when working with GenAI feedback (Jensen, Bearman,
Boud, Konradsen 2025) and the more developmental focus of teacher feedback (Jensen et al.
2025). As outlined above, our findings add to this: noting that GenAI feedback is more accessible,
more understandable and may invite engagement since it is perceived to be less risky. In contrast,
teacher feedback may be more credible and insightful given that it is reported to be more relational, contextualised, relevant and reliable. Taking the literature and our findings together, we infer
the following complementary relationship. At best, teacher feedback challenges students with
contextualised areas for improvement that operate beyond the immediate task, with all the
mutuality and recognition of each others’ lifeworlds that productive student-teachers relationships entail. This can be complemented by GenAI feedback, which presents helpful, digestible
information about present student work, which is easy to understand and is almost always accessible, and without any risk of loss of social status.
We note a much smaller proportion regarded GenAI feedback as very trustworthy. There is a
big proviso however, students themselves need to be able to understand if the GenAI’s comments on their work are in fact trustworthy or reliable. This cannot be based solely on an uninformed impression. Indeed, we should not assume all GenAI use is equal. If a student is in an
environment rich with social and material interactions, as Esterhazy (2018) describes in her ethnography of student feedback interactions, then GenAI will be just one part of many feedback
experiences. However, feedback rich environments are not common (Jensen et al. 2025) and as
a result students may be relying heavily on GenAI output. Therefore, we agree with Bearman
et al. (2024) that there is an urgent need to develop students’ evaluative judgement – or capability to judge the work of self and others – particularly around GenAI. This goes hand-in-glove
with other aspects of feedback literacy, including understanding the need for multiple perspectives about a piece of work (Carless and Boud 2018).

Students who did not use GenAI feedback
Half (50.3%) of the students reported that they did not use GenAI for feedback. The analysis of
their reasons provides further insight into student perspectives and key considerations for policy,
support, and formal integration of GenAI feedback in higher education.
However, it should be noted that while these students did not use GenAI for feedback, they
had all used GenAI in some way for their university studies. One reason, reported by 28.1%, was
that they were unaware of the option to use GenAI for feedback purposes, or indicated that they
did not know how to do so (e.g. how to upload or input a whole essay for feedback). This is
concerning since 83.9% of students who did use GenAI for feedback reported it helpful or very
helpful. We do not argue that GenAI feedback must be used. However, our data indicates that
many students found it to be useful, and therefore all students should be supported in developing an understanding of how they might access and use it for this purpose.
Another commonly reported reason for not using GenAI was to do with trustworthiness of
the feedback (28.7%), in particular, the outputs or processes of GenAI were not regarded as

reliable, or not as trustworthy as teachers, self or peers. Interestingly, a similar number of
students who used GenAI feedback were also concerned about its reliability (31.5%). This
echoes the reports of students who are using GenAI for feedback. Across both groups, some
students cite it as a reason for not using GenAI, while others merely note it as a caution.
Upon reflection, we wonder if these percentages are too small. Should more students be
critical of GenAI? Arguably, students should bring to bear their critical judgment of the quality and appropriateness of any feedback regardless of its source. On the other hand, it is
unclear exactly how the students have come to form these evaluations. Perhaps they are less
related to the efficacy of the system or other issues of quality, but more reflective of a general attitude towards credibility of source (Lipnevich and Smith 2008; Nazaretsky et al. 2024).
On the other hand, perhaps the perceptions of trustworthiness are reflecting a judicious
discernment in relation to the reliability of the feedback across all contexts. In other words,
perhaps our data reflects students’ recognition that GenAI can be trusted in some instances
more than others, such as the difference between asking GenAI to provide advice about
paragraph structures compared with seeking guidance that requires a deeply contextualised
understanding of the course content. If this is the case, then the obvious conclusion, as we
have noted previously, is that GenAI feedback and teacher feedback are useful for different
reasons, that is, they are complementary but are not interchangeable. Clearly there is a need
to continue to research students’ perception of trust with respect to feedback as engagement with GenAI continues to develop.

Future work
The insights from students who used and those who didn’t use GenAI for feedback point to
future work on feedback literacy and feedback engagement. Zhan and Yan (2025) have investigated feedback literacy in the context of English language testing. They identify that student
feedback literacy in a time of GenAI includes: the capability to write and finetune feedback
prompts; evaluative judgement about GenAI outputs; ethical decision-making in terms of how to
use the GenAI feedback; emotional reflexivity to calibrate trust levels; and employing metacognitive skills in terms of regulating their own feedback processes with respect to GenAI. From a
conceptual perspective, Zhan et al. (2025) propose that feedback engagement is a means of
reconciling these feedback skills with their environment in terms of eliciting, processing and
enacting feedback. This framing is helpful, as although our work points to why students seek or
elicit feedback from genAI – it is helpful, understandable, less risky, approachable and they were
aware of its capabilities – we have less of a sense of how they process and enact feedback into
their future work. What happens when they try to integrate what they have learnt through GenAI
feedback into future tasks or modes of thinking? Do GenAI and teacher feedback continue to
complement each other? GenAI feedback tends to be available before an assessment is due
rather than after – what difference does this make? Does the bias against automated feedback
(Nazaretsky et al. 2024) mean students process and enact this feedback differently? These all
form areas for future work.

Limitations
This is a large-scale study drawn from four Australian institutions. However, we note that the
response rate of 8,000 given the broad enrolment numbers of over 200,000 students cannot
claim to be representative. The survey was optional and therefore students with stronger views
about GenAI may have been more inclined to answer the survey. We also note that Australian
higher education may differ from other contexts; our demographics however give a sense of the
key features of the respondents. Finally, this survey was completed at a point where very few

Conclusion
This study underlines that GenAI and teacher feedback are perceived and experienced differently
by university students in their day-to-day study. Students regard teacher feedback as relatively
more helpful and more trustworthy. But their comments suggest that GenAI feedback is not
unhelpful nor altogether untrustworthy. GenAI feedback is considerably easier to access, is more
understandable, less negative and aids sense-making (understanding). And while engaging with
GenAI for feedback provides a lesser sense of connection, it also affords a lessened personal risk.
Overall, the findings suggest that these feedback modes are not an either/or; students can and
do engage with both, and there are likely benefits from doing so because they can helpfully
serve different functions. The ways in which students perceive GenAI and teacher feedback provide important insights into not only how we enact GenAI feedback in institutions but also how
we might further understand the value, usefulness, and relational impact of teacher feedback
practice. The challenge for the sector is to explore how teachers can lean into what they do best,
contextualised, expert and challenging advice, in the context of the teacher-learner relationship.
