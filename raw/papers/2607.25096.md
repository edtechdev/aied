Memdora: Designing Cognitively-Grounded Flashcard
Interactions for AI-Powered Spaced Repetition
Ruiyang Zhang

Ryonix Labs Inc.
Montreal, Quebec, Canada
zhangruiyang36@gmail.com

arXiv:2607.25096v1 [cs.HC] 27 Jul 2026

Abstract
Spaced repetition systems (SRS) have demonstrated robust effects
on long-term retention, yet existing tools reduce the flashcard interaction to a single binary gesture: flip and self-rate. This impoverished interaction model fails to leverage decades of cognitive
science evidence on retrieval practice, and requires learners to
context-switch out of their reading flow to create cards manually.
We present Memdora, a cross-platform AI spaced repetition system
that addresses these limitations through four contributions: (1) a
taxonomy of 17 cognitively-grounded interaction types across three
learning categories—Language (6 types), By Heart (1 type with 3 retrieval modes), and Exam (10 types)—each mapped to peer-reviewed
cognitive science evidence displayed on every card; (2) a unified
AI generation pipeline that collapses card creation to a single gesture at the point of reading across web, mobile, and three browser
extensions (Chrome, Edge, Firefox); (3) a collaborative classroom
layer enabling teachers to publish decks, assign them to students,
and track learning outcomes at the individual card level; and (4)
an effort-based behavioral reward system that incentivizes actual
cognitive engagement rather than mere app presence. Memdora
integrates FSRS-6, the current state-of-the-art spaced repetition
algorithm, and is deployed publicly on iOS, Android, Web, and
three browser extensions. We describe the design rationale for each
interaction type, discuss how the system advances beyond prior
AI flashcard systems including SmartFlash and KARL, and outline
implications for educational technology design.

Introduction

The forgetting curve, first described by Ebbinghaus in 1885 [6],
remains one of the most replicated findings in cognitive psychology: without review, humans forget approximately 70% of newly
learned material within 24 hours. Spaced repetition systems (SRS)

address this by scheduling reviews at optimally expanding intervals,
leveraging the spacing effect to dramatically improve long-term
retention [16].
Despite decades of evidence supporting spaced repetition, mainstream SRS tools—most notably Anki, with over 10 million downloads—
have changed little since the introduction of SuperMemo’s SM-2
algorithm in 1987. Critically, these tools reduce the flashcard interaction to a single gesture: flip the card, reveal the answer, self-rate
recall. This binary interaction model has two significant limitations.
First, it ignores decades of cognitive science evidence on retrieval
practice. Research demonstrates that different retrieval formats—
multiple choice, free recall, fill-in-the-blank, error detection, sequential reconstruction—activate different cognitive processes and
produce differential retention benefits [12, 13]. A vocabulary learner
who always flips a card to see a translation is engaging in recognitionbased recall, among the weakest forms of retrieval [12]. More effortful retrieval formats—generating the answer before revealing
it, completing a cloze deletion, reconstructing a passage from first
words—consistently produce superior long-term retention through
the testing effect [16].
Second, existing tools impose significant creation friction. Anki
users must manually type each card, switching context from their
reading material to the application. This preparation burden is
so significant that it deters sustained adoption: a recent study of
AI flashcard systems found that students reported spending more
energy preparing cards than actually studying them [18].
We present Memdora, an AI-powered spaced repetition system
that addresses both limitations through four primary contributions:
(1) A taxonomy of 17 cognitively-grounded interaction types
across three learning categories, each explicitly mapped to
peer-reviewed cognitive science evidence displayed to the
learner on every card.
(2) A unified AI generation pipeline that collapses card creation
to a single gesture at the point of reading—highlighting text
in a browser, pasting an article URL, or uploading a PDF—
across web, mobile, and three browser extensions.
(3) A collaborative classroom layer enabling teachers to publish
and assign decks to students, track mastery at the individual
card level, and receive live progress dashboards.
(4) An effort-based behavioral reward system that incentivizes
actual cognitive engagement (5 minutes of study time, 100 cards
reviewed) rather than mere app presence.
Memdora is deployed publicly on iOS, Android, Web, and browser
extensions for Chrome, Edge, and Firefox. It integrates FSRS-6 [19],
the current state-of-the-art open-source spaced repetition algorithm, which outperforms Anki’s SM-2 in recall prediction accuracy.

2 Related Work
2.1 Spaced Repetition Systems
Spaced repetition builds on the spacing effect—the finding that distributed practice over time produces better long-term retention than
massed practice [16]. The SuperMemo SM-2 algorithm, introduced
in 1987, operationalized spaced repetition as a card scheduling system and became the basis for Anki. SM-2 uses a simple exponential
interval model with a performance-based easiness factor.
FSRS (Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler) [19], currently in version 6, represents a significant algorithmic advance over SM-2. FSRS
uses a differential equation model of memory stability and retrievability, achieving significantly lower mean absolute error in recall
prediction. KARL [9] further advanced scheduling by incorporating
card content through BERT embeddings, demonstrating that semantic relationships between cards can improve scheduling accuracy
on a dataset of 123,143 study logs.
Despite these algorithmic advances, the interaction layer of SRS
tools has remained largely unchanged. Neither Anki, Quizlet, nor
existing research systems have proposed a systematic, cognitivelygrounded taxonomy of flashcard interaction types.

2.2

AI-Generated Flashcards

The problem of flashcard creation friction has recently attracted
research attention. SmartFlash [18] studied AI flashcard generation using LLMs and found that learners valued automation for
reducing preparation burden, but required editable, transparent
AI outputs to maintain cognitive ownership. The study involved
6 students using a research prototype with a single basic card type.
Bachiri et al. [2] demonstrated comparable quality between AIgenerated and human-created flashcards in MOOC contexts. Agnes
and Srinivasan [1] found that AI-generated mnemonic keywords
significantly improved vocabulary retention.
Memdora extends this line of work by: (a) deploying AI generation across five platforms with a single-gesture capture interface,
and (b) generating cards across 17 distinct cognitively-grounded
interaction types rather than a single basic flip format.

2.3

Retrieval Practice and Card Interaction
Design

Retrieval practice—actively retrieving information from memory—
consistently produces superior retention compared to passive rereading, a phenomenon known as the testing effect [16]. Critically, retrieval format matters: multiple-choice questions produce
recognition-based retrieval, while free recall requires generative
retrieval—a more effortful process producing stronger memory
traces [12]. Cloze deletion produces intermediate benefits [4]. Elaborative interrogation—explaining why a fact is true—produces strong
deep encoding [7].
Bjork’s desirable difficulties framework [3] establishes that retrieval conditions that feel harder during study—interleaving, varied
formats, reduced feedback—consistently produce better long-term

retention than easier conditions. This framework motivates Memdora’s varied interaction types and the By Heart category’s three
self-regulated difficulty modes.

2.4

Gamification and Behavioral Retention

Duolingo’s gamification research demonstrates that streaks, leaderboards, and reward systems improve daily retention and engagement [5]. However, critics note that presence-based rewards—rewarding
app opens regardless of learning depth—may produce superficial engagement rather than deep learning [8]. Memdora distinguishes its
reward design by rewarding effort rather than presence: users earn
free AI generation credits for completing 5 continuous minutes of
study and for reviewing 100 cards per day. These thresholds reflect
minimum effective dose principles for spaced repetition practice.

2.5

Collaborative Learning Systems

Google Classroom provides assignment and tracking infrastructure
but no spaced repetition or AI generation. Quizlet offers basic class
features but without FSRS scheduling or a cognitively-grounded
interaction taxonomy. No existing published system combines AI
generation, 17 cognitively-grounded interaction types, FSRS scheduling, and collaborative classroom tracking within a single crossplatform deployment.

3

System Overview

Memdora is a cross-platform spaced repetition system available as
a web application, iOS app, Android app, and browser extensions
for Chrome, Edge, and Firefox. Mobile apps support offline review
with local state persistence and background synchronization. The
system comprises four layers: capture, generation, scheduling, and
review.

3.1

Capture Layer

Users add content to Memdora through four modalities: (a) pasting
an article or YouTube URL, (b) uploading a PDF, (c) entering text
directly in the application, or (d) using the browser extension to
highlight text on any web page. The browser extensions for Chrome,
Edge, and Firefox provide single-gesture capture: the user highlights
text and clicks the extension popup to immediately trigger AI card
generation for the selected content. This collapses the contextswitching cost identified in SmartFlash [18] to near zero—learners
remain on their reading page throughout.

3.2

AI Generation Layer

The AI generation pipeline processes source content using a large
language model with structured output prompting. The system
performs content-type classification to route extracted knowledge
items to the most appropriate interaction type(s): vocabulary items
to Language cards, quotable passages to By Heart cards, and factual or procedural content to Exam cards. For Language cards, the
pipeline generates example sentences in context from the source
material. For Exam cards requiring multiple-choice formats, the
pipeline generates three plausible distractor options drawn semantically from the same deck, maximizing difficulty without introducing
implausible alternatives.

All AI-generated cards are editable before review, consistent with
SmartFlash’s finding that editability is essential for maintaining
learner cognitive ownership [18]. The system displays a confidence
indicator for each generated card and allows one-click regeneration
of individual cards or distractor options.

3.3

Scheduling Layer

All cards are scheduled using FSRS-6 [19], which maintains per-card
estimates of memory stability (𝑆—how slowly a memory decays)
and retrievability (𝑅—the probability of successful recall at a given
time). The system computes optimal review intervals to maintain
target retention (default 90%) with minimum review frequency.
FSRS-6 is implemented as a TypeScript library running client-side
on mobile for offline scheduling, with server-side synchronization
ensuring consistency across platforms.
Every card displays its current retention percentage—the FSRS-6
retrievability estimate—to the learner during review. This transparency makes the scheduling model’s predictions visible, building
learner trust and promoting metacognitive awareness of memory
state.

3.4

Collaborative Features

Deck owners can publish their decks publicly. Followers receive
updates when the owner modifies card content, enabling living
decks that improve over time. The teacher-student classroom layer
allows educators to create a class, assign decks with due dates,
and monitor student progress through an instructor dashboard
displaying per-student and per-card retention rates. This identifies
cards that students are systematically struggling with, enabling
targeted classroom intervention.

4

A Taxonomy of 17 Cognitively-Grounded
Interaction Types

The central design contribution of Memdora is its taxonomy of
17 interaction types organized across three learning categories. Each
type is grounded in peer-reviewed cognitive science research, with
the citation displayed on the card during review. This transparency
operationalizes the principle that educational technology should
make its scientific foundations visible to learners.

4.1

Design Philosophy

The taxonomy is built on three principles from the retrieval practice
literature. First, retrieval effort produces retention: more effortful
retrieval formats produce stronger memory traces [16]. Second,
format should match learning goal: vocabulary acquisition, verbatim memorization, and conceptual understanding require different
retrieval formats [12]. Third, transparency builds metacognition:
displaying the cognitive science rationale for each interaction type
promotes learner trust and metacognitive awareness [18].

4.2

Language Category (6 Types)

The Language category supports vocabulary and phrase acquisition in a target language. Cards are generated from user-provided

source material and display the target word with phonetic transcription, a source-context example sentence, and the current retention
percentage.
Vocabulary. Presents a target-language word with phonetic
transcription and source sentence. The learner self-rates recall
before revealing the translation. Grounded in retrieval practice
research [16].
Cloze. Presents a sentence in the target language with one key
word blanked. The learner supplies the missing word before revealing it. Cloze deletion has been shown to produce stronger retention
than simple translation recall [4].
Phrase. Presents a multi-word expression in context, requiring
production of the meaning of the full phrase. Grounded in phrasal
learning and chunking research [11].
Concept. Requires the learner to explain a concept or grammatical structure before revealing the target explanation. Grounded
in elaborative interrogation research [7], which shows that selfexplaining produces superior retention to passive reading.
Reverse. Presents the definition in the learner’s native language
and requires production of the target-language form. Bidirectional
retrieval strengthens memory encoding compared to unidirectional
recall [15].
Sentence. Presents a context sentence with a key phrase highlighted, requiring the learner to judge usage correctness or produce
an equivalent sentence. Grounded in contextual and sentence-level
learning research [11].

4.3

By Heart Category (1 Type, 3 Retrieval
Modes)

The By Heart category supports verbatim memorization of extended passages—poems, speeches, scripts, quotations, and procedural sequences. Unlike the Language and Exam categories, By
Heart presents a single card type with three user-selectable retrieval
modes of varying difficulty. This self-regulated difficulty selection
is grounded in Bjork’s desirable difficulties framework [3] and selfregulated learning research [14], which demonstrate that learners
benefit from agency over challenge level selection matched to their
current competence.
Strip Peek. All lines of the passage are hidden. The learner taps
any line to reveal it for 2 seconds before it is re-hidden. This forces
maximum active recall before each reveal—the highest-effort mode.
Step Through. Past lines are dimmed; the current target line
is bold. The learner reads past context and attempts to recall the
current line before advancing. This provides partial cueing from
preceding lines.
First Word. Each line is represented by its opening word only.
The learner uses the first word as a retrieval cue to recall the full
line—the lightest cue, providing the most support.
The three modes constitute three points on the cue-target continuum identified in retrieval practice research. By allowing learners
to self-select difficulty on a per-session basis, Memdora operationalizes the desirable difficulties framework within a practical user
interface—to our knowledge, the first SRS system to do so for extended passage memorization.

4.4

Exam Category (10 Types)

The Exam category supports factual and conceptual knowledge
acquisition for examination preparation. The 10 interaction types
cover the primary retrieval formats identified in educational assessment research, each grounded in a specific cognitive science
citation.
Multiple Choice. Presents a question with four options (one correct, three AI-generated distractors from the same deck). Grounded
in recognition-based retrieval research [12].
Q&A. Open-ended question requiring a free-recall response
before revealing the answer. Free recall produces stronger memory
traces than recognition [16].
True/False. Binary judgment of a factual statement, with explanation revealed after response. Grounded in confidence-based
assessment research [12].
Matching. Pairs of related terms or concepts that must be matched.
Tests associative memory formation [15].
Sequence. A set of steps or events that must be placed in correct order. Grounded in serial order and procedural learning research [11].
Formula. Mathematical or chemical formula completion. Grounded
in procedural memory and worked-example research [17].
Scenario. Applied knowledge question requiring judgment in a
realistic context. Grounded in transfer-appropriate processing [13].
Error Spot. Presents a statement or equation containing a deliberate error; the learner identifies and corrects it. Error detection is
a particularly effective retrieval format for procedural content [12].
Comparison. Requires articulating the difference or relationship between two concepts. Grounded in comparative judgment
and relational learning research [7].
Fill the Blank. Sentence with a key term removed. Grounded
in the cloze procedure and generative retrieval research [4].

5 Behavioral Design
5.1 Effort-Based Reward System
Memdora implements a reward system that addresses a known
limitation of gamified learning: presence-based rewards (rewarding
daily app opening) may produce superficial engagement without
corresponding learning depth [8]. Memdora instead rewards cognitive effort through two primary mechanisms.
Study Time Reward. Users who complete 5 continuous minutes of active card review earn bonus AI generation credits. The
5-minute threshold reflects minimum effective session duration for
spaced repetition learning.
Volume Reward. Users who review 100 cards in a day earn
additional credits. This rewards sustained cognitive engagement—a
stronger proxy for learning than session count.
Streak System. A daily streak tracks consecutive days of study
activity, providing continuity motivation through loss aversion [5].
Unlike Duolingo’s streak, which rewards any app interaction, Memdora streaks require actual card review.

5.2

Transparency Features

Every card displays two pieces of metadata: (1) the current retention
percentage computed by FSRS-6, making the scheduling model’s

predictions visible; and (2) a citation to the cognitive science paper
grounding the card’s interaction type. These features serve dual
purposes: building learner trust in the system by making its scientific basis explicit, and promoting metacognitive awareness of why
different interaction formats are being used. This design responds
directly to SmartFlash’s finding that transparency in AI educational
systems is essential for learner acceptance [18].

6

Implementation

Memdora is implemented as a full-stack TypeScript application. The
web frontend uses React with server-side rendering for performance
and SEO. Native mobile apps use React Native (Expo) for iOS and
Android, enabling code sharing across platforms while maintaining
native performance. Browser extensions are implemented using
the WebExtensions API, providing compatibility across Chrome,
Edge, and Firefox from a shared codebase—a design decision that
maximizes capture-point coverage with minimal duplication.
The AI generation pipeline uses a large language model accessed
via API, with typed JSON output schemas corresponding to each
of the 17 interaction types. Content-type classification determines
which interaction type(s) are most appropriate for each extracted
knowledge item from the source material.
FSRS-6 is implemented as a TypeScript library. On mobile, it runs
client-side against a local SQLite database, enabling fully offline
scheduling. On web, scheduling is performed server-side. Background synchronization ensures all platforms share a consistent
scheduling state. The offline-first mobile architecture is particularly important for the language-learning use case, where users
frequently review during commutes without reliable connectivity.
The deck publishing and classroom infrastructure uses a publishsubscribe model. When a deck owner modifies card content, all followers receive an update notification and can pull the new version.
Teacher-student class assignments are tracked with per-student,
per-card scheduling state, enabling the instructor dashboard to compute class-level retention statistics and identify cards that students
are systematically struggling with.

7 Discussion
7.1 Design Implications
The 17-type taxonomy raises an important design question: does interaction diversity improve or impair learning? Bjork’s desirable difficulties research [3] suggests that varied retrieval formats produce
better long-term retention than consistent format use, as variation
prevents format-specific learning strategies. However, excessive
novelty may increase extraneous cognitive load [17]. Memdora’s AI
generation system addresses this tension by selecting interaction
types appropriate to content type—vocabulary always becomes
a Language card, passages become By Heart cards—rather than
randomly varying formats. This content-driven assignment preserves format-meaning coherence while delivering retrieval format
diversity.
The By Heart 3-mode design operationalizes a key insight from
self-regulated learning research: learners benefit from agency over
difficulty selection [14]. The ability to choose between Strip Peek,
Step Through, and First Word modes on a per-session basis allows

learners to calibrate challenge to their current mastery level, consistent with Bjork’s desirable difficulties framework [3]. Displaying all
three options simultaneously makes the difficulty spectrum explicit,
potentially improving learner metacognition about their own recall
ability.
The decision to display cognitive science citations on every card
reflects a design hypothesis: that transparency about why a learning
interaction is designed the way it is will increase learner trust and
motivation. This hypothesis is directly testable and will be a primary
focus of our forthcoming user study.

7.2

Limitations

The primary limitation of this work is the absence of a controlled
user study evaluating the interaction taxonomy’s effectiveness. The
design rationale for each interaction type is grounded in existing
cognitive science research, but the efficacy of these types within
the Memdora system specifically—and in combination with FSRS-6
scheduling—has not been empirically evaluated. We are designing a
longitudinal study comparing retention outcomes across card types
and plan to report results in future work.
The AI generation pipeline may produce cards of variable quality in highly specialized domains where the underlying language
model has limited training coverage. User editing and one-click
regeneration mitigate this, but quality variance remains a known
limitation of LLM-based content generation systems [18].
The classroom features have not yet been evaluated in a real
educational setting. The teacher-student assignment and tracking
system is designed based on established learning analytics principles, but in-the-wild deployment may reveal unmet needs around
teacher workflow, class management, and institutional authentication.

7.3

Future Work

We are designing Memdora Arena, a daily competitive challenge
feature in which all users within a subject category receive the same
AI-curated deck and compete on accuracy and speed against a global
leaderboard. The Arena is designed to leverage social comparison
for motivation while generating shareable result cards (analogous
to Wordle’s emoji grid) to drive organic user acquisition. Future
work will evaluate whether competitive social features enhance
or impede deep learning outcomes compared to individual spaced
repetition practice.
We also plan a formal evaluation of the classroom layer, recruiting teachers across secondary and post-secondary institutions to
assign Memdora decks and measure retention outcomes against
control groups using traditional study methods. The effort-based reward system will be evaluated in a separate study comparing learning depth metrics (retention at 30 days) between effort-rewarded
and presence-rewarded conditions.

8

Conclusion

We presented Memdora, a cross-platform AI spaced repetition
system with four primary design contributions: a taxonomy of
17 cognitively-grounded interaction types spanning Language, By
Heart, and Exam learning categories; a unified AI generation pipeline
enabling single-gesture card creation at the point of reading across

five platforms; a collaborative classroom layer for teacher-student
deck assignment and progress tracking; and an effort-based behavioral reward system that incentivizes cognitive engagement rather
than app presence. The system integrates FSRS-6 for state-of-the-art
scheduling and provides transparency through per-card retention
percentages and cognitive science citations on every card.
The central argument of this paper is that the flashcard interaction has been systematically under-designed. The dominant flipand-rate paradigm, unchanged since SuperMemo’s SM-2 in 1987,
ignores decades of cognitive science evidence on retrieval practice
format effects. By systematically grounding 17 interaction types
in peer-reviewed research and making those citations visible to
learners, Memdora proposes that educational technology can simultaneously be evidence-based and transparent—building learner trust
through the same scientific foundation that motivates its design.
We invite the IUI community to engage with the open questions
this raises: how much interaction diversity is optimal, how learners
self-regulate difficulty, and whether transparency about cognitive
science mechanisms improves educational technology adoption
and learning outcomes.
