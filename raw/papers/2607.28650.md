Unanticipated Effects of Generative AI on Expertise Pathways and Performance
Perception in System Administration
RANA ABOU KHAMIS, School of Information Technology, Carleton University, Canada
HALA ASSAL, Department Systems and Computer Engineering, Carleton University, Canada
ASHRAF MATRAWY, School of Information Technology, Carleton University, Canada
While industry discourse often emphasizes immediate productivity gains and frames Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) primarily
as a tool for automation [1, 3, 4, 11, 14], the integration of GenAI into system administration may involve deeper shifts in professional
practice that are not yet fully understood. Drawing on 14 semi-structured interviews with Information Technology (IT) professionals,
this paper explores the lived reality of embedding GenAI into daily routines of troubleshooting, scripting, and system verification.
Through inductive thematic analysis, we uncover two unanticipated socio-technical findings. First, we describe a “compression of
traditional expertise pathways” where GenAI appears to function as both a mentor-like tutor and a "ladder-shortening" tool. While the
tool can support faster task performance in unfamiliar domains, our findings suggest it may also reduce a practitioner’s exposure to

the foundational, hands-on cycles of building, failing, and debugging that historically served as the training ground for technical
expertise. Second, we describe a “performance perception shift,” where the speed of AI-assisted work begins to reset organizational
and self-expectations for productivity. This shift may create a "two-speed culture" within teams and introduce "productivity guilt,"
as necessary manual work, even when required for safety or validation, is increasingly perceived as slow or a failure of efficiency.
Our results raise broader questions about how GenAI may influence expertise development, how professional value is assessed in
high-stakes technical environments, and the role of human judgment in complex technical environments.

Introduction

System administrators (sysadmins henceforth) are increasingly using GenAI tools in their daily workflows [10, 13], yet
the broader impact of such tools on system administration practices and expertience development is not fully understood.
Prior research on GenAI in professional environments has primarily focused on productivity, efficiency, and automation
benefits [1, 3, 4, 11]. However, research examining the day-to-day impacts of GenAI on system administration workflows
in real operational environments is lacking. Similar to other technical fields, system administration expertise is mainly
developed through repeated hands-on experience with operational tasks, troubleshooting activities, and exposure
to system failures over time [6, 8]. Seemingly mundane and repetitive everyday technical routines (e.g., scripting,
log analysis, and configuration work) serve beyond their immediate purpose as operational functions. These tasks
are integral to the experiential process through which sysadmins develop sound technical judgment and contextual
understanding [5, 7].
Herein, we take the first step towards addressing this research gap, exploring the effects of GenAI adoption beyond
performance and automation. In other professional domains, concerns around AI-assisted learning and expertise have
emerged. However, it remains unclear whether similar concerns exist in the field of system administration, and the
broader implications of GenAI adoption on the security awareness and operational judgment of those dubbed “keepers
of the machines" [9] remains unknown.

In this paper, we bring the community’s attention to two unexpected and concerning themes that have emerged

from our semi-structured interview study with 14 sysadmins and IT professionals. The following two themes were
identified while exploring the integration of GenAI in sysadmins’ everyday workflows.
• First, we identify a “compression of traditional expertise pathway”. Typically, system administrators ascend a
developmental hierarchy, progressing from routine operational tasks towards more complex technical responsibilities. Our findings suggest that GenAI reconfigures this pathway by enabling users to leapfrog foundational
experiential tasks such as line-by-line manual scripting, exhaustive vendor documentation research, and manual
log analysis, which historically served as the essential “training ground” for professional judgment [5, 7].
• Second, we describe a “Performance Perception Distortion”. As the dramatic time compression provided by
GenAI, speed and rapid task completion become increasingly normalized, it influences both organizational and
self evaluation of productivity. Our finding suggests that this shift is characterized by a significant paradox:
while the final output iwe delivered with speed, this speed frequently masks a substantial layer of invisible
labor that is currently unaccounted for professional evaluations.

Methodology

We conducted semi-structured interviews with 14 system administrators and IT professionals who had experience
utilizing GenAI in their work, with individual experience ranging from 2 to 15 years. These 45-minute sessions were
recorded and transcribed using Trint [12]. Following Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis [2], we iteratively
coded the transcripts to identify patterns in how GenAI reshapes daily routines.The study was formally approved by
the Carleton University Research Ethics Board (CUREB 122772). All participants provided informed consent, and we
maintained strict confidentiality by using pseudonyms (e.g., P1, P2) to protect their identities. While our participants
provide "rich practice signals" regarding current AI integration, we frame these findings as exploratory where future
research should examine these developmental dynamics, particularly as they affect novice administrators over time.

Compression of Traditional Expertise Pathways

Historically, expertise in system administration is a slow ascent and a developmental "ladder" where sysadmins move
from routine fixes toward complex architectural governance through years of hands-on struggle and lesson-learned. Our
findings suggest that GenAI may reconfigure and transform the expertise lifecycle through a “compression of traditional
expertise pathways”. This may create a fundamental tension between (1) accelerated performance and skills and
(2) the depth of professional learning required to develop expertise including intuitive reasoning, contextual
understanding, and judgment.
GenAI as Mentorship and Accelerator:
Rather than describing GenAI as a time-saving tool, several participants discussed how GenAI supported them in
performing tasks beyond their usual expertise boundaries and lowers the barrier to entry for highly specialized work as
“cognitive tutor” or a mentor-like scaffold. This trajectory supports horizontal growth, allowing administrators to
navigate technical domains where they lack formal training. For instance, P10, who noted he is “not a programmer,”
used GenAI to build functional bots across “three different languages” he had never studied. This does not necessarily
suggest that he had learned these languages in a traditional way but GenAI gives him the ability to perform a task and
produce outcome. Similarly, P12 was able to generate Power BI dashboards in just “half a day,” a task he felt would
traditionally have required the expertise of a specialized business analyst. In this mode, the tool acts as a "tutor on the

shoulder," providing immediate direction, filling knowledge gaps, and "feeding" the practitioner’s technical knowledge.
GenAI “gives [SAs] an opportunity to learn” (P14) and complete tasks by providing guidance and fill knowledge gap. P7
said AI helped him “understand everything better,” and described it as “like having a teacher, but right next to you”.
Expertise remains necessary for judgment and evaluation:
These accounts suggest a fundamental shift in the sysadmin’s role that while GenAI may accelerate task execution,
participants continued to position human expertise around contextual understanding, verification, and judgment
rather than direct production of output alone. P13 observed that GenAI “does 60 to 70% of a task by providing a "based
structure to get started with", but not a finished outcome , "the rest of the tweaking I have to do on my own”(P13). This
reinforcement of human oversight highlights that, while the execution expertise of the profession is being compressed,
the judgment layer remains slow and experience-based. P14 was equally clear that GenAI “works best as a teammate,
not a replacement,” comparing its role to an "...autopilot for routine functions..." that still requires "a human in the
cockpit" when conditions become complex. Similarly, P7 characterized the tool as “ a very good assistant” but insisted
on maintaining strict accountability by checking AI-generated scripts “line by line”. Ultimately, these accounts suggest
that GenAI does not eliminate the need for professional intuition. Instead, it makes that expertise more valuable by
shifting the sysadmin’s focus toward the high-stakes tasks of auditing, validation, and governance
GenAI as a Substitution for Experience and Learning Gap: Conversely, our data reveals a concern that GenAI
may function as a “ladder-shortening” substitute that allows practitioners to bypass the very experiential cycles including
building, failing, and debugging where historically used to build technical intuition. By handling “does 60 to 70%(P13) of
technical execution, the tool may inadvertently remove the training ground of repetitive basic work that serves as the
base for professional judgment. Senior participants like P1 expressed that an intern or new employee utilizing GenAI
“won’t be able to do a perfect job with it” since “you still require some form of experience” (P1). P6 warned that users may
rely on AI “without having the fundamental understanding to be able to tell if that answer is right or wrong,” even though
the tool may “very confidently give you an answer” (P6). This may creates a "learning debt", where novices might
produce working outputs without the foundational grounding needed to detect high-confidence hallucinations which
P3 noted can occur when the model “gives misinformation or information that’s not accurate” (P3). P2 captured the
behavioral dimension of this debt as a potential addiction to the tool, stating that if a practitioner becomes dependent,
their “brain will not work anymore” (P2). This dependency causes the administrator to “go to asking” the GenAI as soon
as a problem arises rather than thinking independently. A habit P2 identified as particularly “bad” for newer users who
may simply “take it as is” instead of critically evaluating the output. Ultimately, this trajectory suggests that if the tool
makes a mistake, the “addicted” user will follow it directly into that error
The Core Tension: Acceleration vs. Reduced Experiential Learning: The primary contribution of these
findings lies in identifying the tension between these two trajectories. While GenAI dramatically accelerates "execution
expertise," the "judgment expertise" required for safety and accountability still appears to develop through traditional,
experience-based pathways. This creates a new expertise structure where administrators reach high levels of task
performance early in their careers, but may be able to do so without the deep problem-solving skills that are usually
formed through experiential struggle. Ultimately, this shift redefines the administrator’s role from one of manual
execution to one of expert verification, where the primary challenge becomes auditing solutions they may not have had
the foundational practice to build from scratch.

Performance Perception Shift

The second major pattern identified across our interviews relates to how GenAI is not only making sysadmins work
faster, it is changing what sysadmin now expect as normal performance at work. While industry narratives often focus
on the objective speed of GenAI , our findings suggest a deeper socio-technical consequence: the emergence of a new,
AI-augmented "normal." As sysadmins become used to the dramatic time compression afforded by GenAI tools, the
accelerated pace of tasks delivery seems to be recalibrating organizational and self-expectations, which may cause
resetting the baseline for competent performance. However, this shift is characterized by a significant paradox. While
the final output is delivered with rapid pace, it often masks a substantial layer of invisible labor required to make
AI-generated results usable, safe, and contextually accurate.
Acceleration as a New Performance Baseline: There was a noticeable sense of time compression for sysadmins
in their workflow where P1 explained that GenAI had improved “exponentially,” making configuration, reporting, data
analysis, and research “faster, more effectively, quicker” (P1). He added that a report which would usually take “2 to 3
days” could now be done “within 2 to 3 hours” which he described as “70, 80 percent time saving” (P1). P13 provided
the strongest formulation of this shift, observing that complex task previously “was performed within 2 months is now
compressed to 2 weeks” (P13). This compression creates a psychological environment where AI-assisted speed becomes
the implicit standard. P14 described a workflow that once took “3–4 days” of digging through documentation and
captures, but now produces a working code and steps in just “15–30 minutes”. For many, the tool has transitioned from
an optional aid to an essential component of professional existence, with P12 stating quite simply, “I cannot see myself
working without AI”. As these compressed timelines become the new standard, there is an emerging risk that manual
work, even when required for safety or accuracy, begins to be perceived as unreasonably slow.
Hidden prompting and iterative refinement labor: These strong narratives of increased productivity combine
with equally strong claims of hidden effort. P10 explained that AI-generated configurations and scripts rarely work
immediately. Instead, “usually there’s a discourse like three or four prompts to troubleshoot like one thing”, followed by
repeated clarification and correction (P10). He described the process as “almost like annoying” because it involves “keep
reiterating over and over again” (P10). P13 described something similar situation when AI output is not as intended, “I
tweak the prompt or provide attachments to help it understand better,” and “our team tests all output” (P13). P14 added
another form of hidden work when he said “right now I always have to explain the context repeatedly” (P14) to ensure
the model understands the specific details of the environment.
Invisible verification and validation work: This hidden labor extends into the post-use phase, where verification
becomes a recurring and mandatory requirement. Rather than replacing human judgment, the Gen AI tool appears to
transform the sysadmin into an expert verifier who must carefully monitor the GenAI’s output to catch high-confidence
hallucinations. P10 said he would never run a generated script in production without testing it first. Instead, he works
iteratively, reads through the output, tests it in a separate environment, and “babysit it” to ensure it does what the
conversation said it would do (P10). P14 said he does not trust AI “100%,” instead relying on “experience and gut feeling”
because “sometimes the answers are hallucinations” and especially in security, the recommendations can be way off
(P14). P5 characterized the tool as merely a “guide,” saying: “I don’t trust the output. It’s more like a guide for me,” and
emphasized that he still has to go through it with "due diligence” before accepting it (P5). P2 framed the same issue in a
more cautionary way, “You need to be sure that when it gives you something . . . you step back and think it through . . .
because these tools can really lead you into big mistakes” (P2).

What emerges is not a story of faster work but it is a story of altered visibility. The final GenAI output is easy to
see and obtain but the iterative prompting, verification, comparison, correction, and contextual tailoring are much
harder to see. As a result, faster output can become the new benchmark for what counts as good performance, while
the human effort needed to produce trustworthy AI-assisted work risks being discounted and invisible.
Emotional discomfort and self-perception when reverting to non-AI workflows: Most of the participants
talked about how AI-assisted speed made them more efficient, but some also talked about how it changed how they
saw their own success. P2, for example, explained that when switching back to non-AI-supported tasks, “emotionally. . .
you can feel bad” because tasks that take “30 minutes” with AI may require “a day to troubleshoot” without it, leading
to frustration (P2). This means that GenAI might change not only how performance is judged by others, but also
how sysadmins judge their own efficiency. P12 also said “I don’t see myself working without AI,” demonstrating that
AI-assisted productivity was highly associated with his feeling of working effectively. Together these declarations
imply that GenAI could influence not only how others judge performance but also how sysadmins view their own
effectiveness.
Team and Organizational Comparison: Participants also hinted that this calibration might introduces new
tensions within teams, particularly when tool access or project constraints are uneven. Participants hinted that this shift
might creates a two-speed culture where performance is increasingly evaluated against an AI-generated benchmark
rather than the actual complexity of the work. P1 noted that if one team can use AI and another cannot, the second
may appear less effective even when the underlying work is equally demanding, creating “extra pressure” because “it
seems as if he’s not working as effective” as the team using GenAI (P1). Furthermore, P13 observed that once automation
is introduced, the accelerated pace quickly becomes normalized within the team, noting that “everyone [is] happy that
they don’t have to switch back and forth” in repetitive work. These accounts illustrate a risk where faster visible output
becomes the primary metric for "good" work, potentially ignoring the professional judgment and hidden effort required
to maintain system integrity behind the scenes.
Ultimately, these accounts suggest that when the performance baseline is recalibrated based only on the final,
rapid output, this intensive babysitting and verification overhead is ignored. This creates a two-speed culture and
introduces productivity guilt, where the necessary manual diligence required for safety is increasingly perceived as a
frustration or a sign of human inadequacy compared to the new AI-driven standard. In addition, our findings suggest
that GenAI-assisted speed can become an internalized benchmark, making traditional manual work feel less important
by comparison.

Discussion

The compression of traditional expertise pathways identified in this study raises critical questions about the future of
professional technical ecosystems.
As GenAI reduces direct engagement with foundational (seemingly mundane) tasks, such as manual troubleshooting
and routine configuration, it essentially disrupts the experiential training ground where technical intuition is built.
However, this creates a profound tension. When foundational learning steps are bypassed, the long-term effects
of practitioners’ reliance on Artifical Inteligence (AI) remains unclear. Open questions include their effect on how
practitioners develop deep judgment expertise and contextual reasoning required for high-risk decision-making.
Another open question relates to oversight and responsibility when using AI in professional settings. We are yet to
see how AI-supported workflows reshape the progression from the direct production of output to the governance and
auditing of that output, particularly in environments where safety and reliability are non-negotiable. As participants

consistently positioned AI as an assistant rather than a replacement, it will be interesting to examine how human
responsibility and accountability continue to evolve as these cognitive instruments become standard equipment in
technical environments.
Furthermore, our findings regarding performance baseline recalibration suggest that the definition of a standard pace
is shifting. As AI-assisted speed becomes the norm, organizations risk creating a “two-speed culture” characterized by
distorted competence comparisons and “productivity guilt” among those restricted from using AI tools. This may push
those perceived as under-performers towards underground or shadow AI usage, creating security and compliance risks
and can ultimately render organizations’ restrictive AI policies useless. These dynamics highlight how productivity
metrics must become context-aware, accounting for the “invisible labor” of iterative prompting and the mandatory
“babysitting” of outputs required for expert verification, as well as the use of AI tools or lack thereof.

Conclusion

This study identifies two unanticipated socio-technical effects of GenAI on system administration. First, by enabling
users to achieve high-level task performance without traditional developmental cycles, GenAI appears to compress
traditional expertise pathways, increasing access to technical work while potentially creating a long-term “learning
debt”. Second, the tool shifts the performance perception, setting new expectations for speed that may mask the intensive
manual verification and validation work still performed by humans behind the scenes. Ultimately, these shifts suggest
that the future of the profession lies not in the direct production of output, but in the expert governance and auditing of
GenAI-assisted solutions.
