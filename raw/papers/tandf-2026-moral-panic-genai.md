Students now have unparalleled access to technology that has left many in academia
at a loss for how to adapt. However, this is not the first time we have experienced
a disruptive technology–and is unlikely to be the last. From calculators to word
processors, and search engines to e-learning, generations of academics have worried
that these disruptive technologies would lead students to cheat more and get less
out of their education (Alsharif, Knight, and Katz 2024, Marshall et al. 2024). Over
time, however, these moral panics subsided, and those technologies became useful
tools. We all carry calculators in our pockets, use word processors, and have

   Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) systems generate text (or images, code,
etc.), creating new content from their vast training datasets based on user input.
These rely on predictive text algorithms with no actual comprehension of either the
data they have been fed or the user input. While GenAI may be today’s disruptive
technology within higher education (see e.g. Gilmore et al. 2025, Macfarlane 2026;
Sidorkin 2025; Westerman et al. 2026), we argue that by making a conscious effort
to teach students how to use it, embedding it into the curriculum, and embracing
it rather than banning it, we can recognise GenAI as the powerful tool it is.

Generative artificial intelligence disrupts higher education
Suppose we apply strategies from previous moral panics to the current moral panic
surrounding GenAI. We may be able to help calm the storm surrounding its use
by elevating effective examples of incorporating GenAI into education. Relying on
our andragogical skills, we can continue to focus on the essential elements of edu-
cation by scaffolding knowledge, promoting critical thinking, and assessing learning
in this new environment. For example, professors have introduced scaffolded assign-
ments that incorporate large language models (LLMs) into the writing process–
allowing students to work with them to develop ideas, edit the outputs with tracked
changes, share their chat sessions with the instructor, compare their version of
paragraphs with the GenAI’s version, etc. (Kim, Kim, and Heidari 2022; Wise et al.
2024; Napolitano 2025; Boudreaux and Murphy 2025b). Faculty can demonstrate
the limitations of GenAI and incorporate GenAI’s hallucinations into exercises that
challenge students to critically evaluate false information (Fontanillas et al. 2025;
Maldonado-Maldonado 2025; Boudreaux and Murphy 2025a). Take-home tests or
essays may no longer function to assess an individual student’s ability but can still
be useful tools alongside in-person or project-based assessments (Allen and Kizilcec
2024). We, as educators, can even embrace GenAI to bolster our andragogical skills
and use it to help us find creative ways to adapt curricula, classroom activities, or
project guidelines to the changing technological landscape (Chan and Colloton 2024).
   Creativity, equity, and skill development are valid concerns that need to be kept
at the forefront in technology adoption–especially as companies behind GenAI
continually release updated and more powerful variants. To ignore, ban, or refuse
to learn about GenAI will only delay our understanding of this tool—potentially
missing how it may increase motivation and improve academic performance for our
students (Cufuna, Duart, and Rangel de Lázaro 2024).
   Faculty question whether students can effectively learn if they offload tasks to
machines, or whether ethical and intentional curricular design with GenAI can
enhance learning. We designed our study to place GenAI within the context of the
history of disruptive technologies in education. Just as calculators, word processors,
internet search engines, and online learning platforms once provoked heated debates
between professors, researchers, and administrators prior to their absorption into
everyday andragogy, it might help to see GenAI as the currently contested technology
that could become as familiar and relied upon as our favourite search engine. To

track the impact of GenAI, we draw on empirical evidence from three instructional
contexts that differed in the availability of artificial intelligence (AI): 1) a pre-GenAI
setting in 2019, 2) a GenAI-available setting in 2023, before any andragogical adap-
tations, and 3) a GenAI-integrated setting in 2025, where appropriate use of GenAI
was taught and encouraged. By directly comparing these contexts, we aim to observe
changing student performance and how it aligns with the concerns raised by faculty,
administrators, and graduate student teaching assistants (TAs) alike, by asking:
  Research Question: How does the availability of GenAI across different instructional
  conditions relate to student performance and variability of scores with both applied
  and knowledge-based assessments?

Course context
Data Visualisation in the Social Sciences was a conceptual statistics course offered
every academic term at a large, public university in the United States of America.
The learning objectives focused on student data literacy by emphasising the decisions
made when creating visualisations of quantitative data. Students learned to use
standard spreadsheet programs (i.e. Microsoft Excel and Google Sheets) to summarise
and visualise datasets through completing hands-on tutorials, which included screen-
cast videos and step-by-step printed manuals. Students then accessed a novel dataset
that closely resembled the set demonstrated in the tutorial and were challenged to
practice their skills by applying the same steps to organise, summarise, and visualise
the second dataset. Students meet in small groups (i.e. 25) with a TA once each
week and in a large group (i.e. 100 to 200) with the instructor once each week.
   During the TA-led discussion sections, students demonstrated their mastery of
the tutorial by completing an open-note, proctored, 20-question quiz delivered on
the learning management system (LMS). The quizzes included both knowledge and
applied questions. Knowledge questions contained information related to definitions,
theory, and other course related content. To demonstrate their applied skills, students
were asked questions related to real world data sets and required to analyse the
data in line with topics learned in that particular week. They were encouraged to
bring their data file and results from the second dataset and to verify calculations
in Excel as they took the quiz. With access to the LMS and Excel, they also had
access to the internet. Most of the quiz questions were not easily searchable on the
internet, so access to a search engine was not a particular advantage. An academic
integrity pledge was included at the beginning of each quiz, asking students to verify
that these answers were their own work.
   We set the policies for the in-person quizzes to promote academic integrity (e.g.
given with a 25 min time limit in a small group, restricted to the internet protocol
addresses of the computers in the computer lab, proctored both by a TA in the
room and by proctoring software that monitored the computer lab computers, ques-
tions drawn from test banks with multiple versions, questions written to be not
easily searchable online, and questions not returned to students) while allowing
students access to appropriate resources (e.g. data files, Excel, and their summaries).
However, by 2024, technology had evolved to the point where these policies were

no longer sufficient to promote academic integrity in two distinct ways. Of primary
concern was that most of the knowledge questions were easily answered by GenAI–as
evidenced by TAs catching students doing this during the quiz–and copying and
pasting answers from GenAI was not a learning outcome of the course. Also, students
were increasingly bringing additional devices into the computer lab and expecting
to use multiple devices, thereby bypassing the proctoring software. The TAs reported
that they were aware that students were using GenAI in an unethical way to answer
the knowledge questions but were powerless to control this behaviour due to the
‘open resources’ policies necessary for students to complete the applied questions.
   Instructional interventions and the administrative tasks of overhauling assessment
can lag behind the recognition of the need for a change. During the week between
academic terms in 2024, members of the curriculum design and instructional teams
met to brainstorm solutions; however, the instructor of record was unable to imple-
ment global changes before the start of the next term. Instead, one of the authors,
as the lead TA, proposed piloting a new format during one of the seven quizzes as
a feasibility study in preparation for the following academic term. Drawing on
feedback, she decided on a format that would meet the learning objectives and
promote academic integrity, considering the reality of GenAI.

Pilot study
New quiz format
The new format divided the quiz into two parts, creating a closed-note knowledge
quiz and an open-resource applied quiz. We piloted this on the last quiz. As in the
previous quiz format, students prepared for the knowledge portion by working through
the tutorial and completing it in the discussion section. However, rather than being
delivered on a computer, it was delivered on paper. After the discussion section, we
released the second dataset, and the students completed the applied portion within
36 h by organising and summarising that dataset. They were given explicit instructions
on how to use LLMs, as well as prompt strategies for finding Excel formulas, and
were informed about what LLMs were available to them for free (see Hendley et al.
inpress, for a full description of the pedagogical techniques). We emphasised that
LLMs were becoming a common tool and that they should understand how to sum-
marise data in a way that authentically reflects the expectations in the workplace, i.e.
that they could use LLMs, search engines, or any other tool to help them with the
applied quiz. When developing the applied quiz content, the author tested the questions
by pasting them into ChatGPT to ensure it could not provide the answers outright.
Questions were edited from previous academic terms to ensure that answers were not
readily available on document-sharing or tutoring sites. The author found that none
of the questions at the time of the pilot could be answered directly by an LLM.

Student feedback
All students were asked to respond to a post-pilot evaluation. However, of the 149
students, only 28 responded. Without reading too much into the responses, given

the low response rate (~19%), the sentiments were mixed. Of those who responded,
53% (n = 15) of students preferred the new quiz format to the one they had expe-
rienced for the other six quizzes. Students reported a preference for having more
time to work on their applied portion of the quiz, indicating that this reduced their
stress. A representative comment was,
  I really liked this new format because it allowed me to actively do the calculations
  and find the answers rather than doing everything before the lab and then searching
  through everything to find the answer I need. I also thought that doing it this way
  was more enjoyable and less stressful than the original way of doing it. (Anonymous
  Student A)

   Of the students who preferred the original quiz format, the most common
responses indicated that they preferred the efficiency of completing the quiz in
25 min and not needing to work on it after the discussion section (e.g. “I’d rather
just get the quiz over with during lab” [Anonymous Student B])

Discussion
The piloted format of the quiz showed much andragogical promise. Separating the
knowledge and applied portions neatly created two solutions from one problem.
The knowledge portion, which in a lower-division introductory class is appropriately
comprised of questions that can be easily answered by GenAI, can be delivered with
high academic integrity and low opportunity for cheating, on paper. The applied
portion, which requires access to computer tools, can be delivered as a take-home,
open-resource assignment. The students’ opinions were mixed, but not entirely
negative. Given that we piloted the new format as the final quiz, any positive sen-
timent indicated that students potentially overcame any resistance to the change in
format to appreciate the affordances of this new format.

Study 2- course-wide implementation
Following the success of the piloted new quiz format, we converted all the quizzes
to the new format in subsequent academic terms. For two terms, students only
experienced the knowledge portion of the quiz given on paper, the second dataset
available after the discussion section, and the applied portion of the quiz completed
as a take-home assignment. To assess the impact of the new quiz format, we ret-
rospectively compared performance on the GenAI-integrated format to performance
from academic terms before GenAI was available and to those where GenAI was
available and likely being used unethically.
   Previous studies have found that students have used GenAI to enhance their
critical thinking skills (Sullivan et al. 2024), summarise literature specific to their
field of study (Gamage et al. 2023; Almassaad, Alajlan, and Alebaikan 2024), and
help them become more efficient in their daily tasks (Sousa and Cardoso 2025).
These findings suggest that students may benefit from using GenAI when working
on their applied quiz questions. However, other researchers have found that when
students rely on GenAI, their in-class performance suffers (Abbas, Jam, and Khan 2024;

Wecks et al. 2024). Wecks et al. (2024) found that students who used GenAI on
essays did significantly worse on in-class exams than students who did not use
GenAI (after controlling for previous scores, attendance, and engagement). Taken
together, these findings suggest that using GenAI may have beneficial effects in the
short term for assignments, but detrimental effects on long-term memory and actual
comprehension, and lead to lower performance on summative assessments. Therefore,
we hypothesise:
    H1: Students will effectively use GenAI to generate answers to applied data summary
    questions; performing lower on the applied questions in the pre-GenAI condition and
    higher in the GenAI-available and GenAI-integrated conditions.

    H0: Performance on applied questions will be the same across the three conditions of
    GenAI availability.

    H2: Students will effectively (but unethically) utilise GenAI to locate answers to knowledge
    questions; performing better on the knowledge questions in the GenAI-available condition
    compared to the pre-GenAI and GenAI-integrated conditions.

    H0: Performance on knowledge questions will be the same across the three conditions of
    GenAI availability.

   While some researchers have argued that GenAI may reduce equity gaps (James
and Andrews 2024; Weng and Fu 2025), others have found that it has the opposite
effect. Beckman et al. (2025) found that students majoring in science, technology,
engineering, and mathematics and those who were further along in their education
(third- and fourth-year students) were more fluent in GenAI and performed better
than those with less educational experience and those in the humanities. Carvajal,
Franco, and Isaksson (2024) found that women are less likely to adopt GenAI in
general, leaving them at a disadvantage in the labour force. With these findings in
mind, we hypothesise that
    H3: The GenAI-integrated and GenAI-available conditions will have the largest variability
    of quiz scores for the applied questions compared to the pre-GenAI condition.

    H0: Variability of applied quiz scores will be the same across the three conditions of
    GenAI availability.

Method
Data and participants
Data were drawn from the item analyses of aggregate performance on quiz questions
from eight iterations of the course across six years of instruction. Enrolment ranged
from 84 to 202, totalling 921 students. We excluded the academic quarters during
the COVID-19 global pandemic, as neither the content delivery nor the assessment
of learning was comparable to the in-person course format. We excluded the aca-
demic quarters directly following the release of ChatGPT, as awareness and adoption
of GenAI were not instantaneous.
   We focused on Quizzes 3, 4, 5, and 6. We excluded Quizzes 1 and 2 for two
reasons. Firstly, students often require these first two quizzes to understand the

format and expectations. Secondly, enrolment fluctuates during the first few weeks
of the academic term as students add and drop the course. We excluded Quiz 7
because the class included the flexibility for each student to drop their lowest quiz,
so many students did not appear to expend their best effort on Quiz 7. We iden-
tified quiz items from Quizzes 3, 4, 5, and 6 that were repeated across the entire
study period and extracted performance data from the LMS.

Variables
AI-availability
The six years covered the period before ChatGPT was released (i.e. March 2019 through
March 2020; pre-GenAI, n = 3), the period after GenAI had been readily available for
about year (i.e. September 2023 through June 2024; GenAI available, n = 3), and the
period after the quiz format had been changed to explicitly allow the use of GenAI
for the applied portion of each assessment (i.e. January through June 2025; GenAI
integrated, n = 2) and to restrict use of GenAI on the knowledge portion (see Table 1).

Item type
The quiz items either tested factual knowledge about data handling, summarising,
and visualising (e.g. which of the following is a measure of central tendency?) or
applied skill in summarising a data set (e.g. in the data set, how many college
students were enrolled from out-of-state?).

Time
During each academic term, the quizzes progressed in order from Quiz 3 to Quiz
6 in consecutive weeks.

Academic performance
For each item in each academic term, difficulty (in percent responding correctly)
and variability (in standard deviation) were recorded. Average difficulty and average
variability were calculated for each quiz, each term.

Discussion
Applied questions
In our first hypothesis, we embraced the moral panic that students would all choose
to use GenAI and would all be able to use it effectively, even on material they had
not learned or understood. This hypothesis was somewhat supported, as students
performed significantly lower on some quizzes under one condition; however, it was

not in the direction we had anticipated (see Figure 1). Compared to the pre-GenAI
baseline, students performed significantly less well on the applied questions during
the GenAI-available period on Quizzes 4, 5, and 6. As we have established that the
applied questions could not be easily answered by GenAI, this drop in performance
supports the interpretation that the students were less well-prepared for these quizzes
and were either unable to use GenAI effectively to generate accurate answers or
unable to evaluate the output critically.
   In subsequent terms, when the instructor and TAs instructed students on how
to use GenAI and gave them explicit instructions to use GenAI tools for their
applied questions, student performance returned to pre-GenAI levels–and exceeded
it for one of the more difficult quizzes (see Figure 1). We hypothesised that, with
permission to use GenAI and instructions on how to utilise it effectively for sum-
marising a dataset, performance would exceed baseline and approach ceiling. However,
we found that performance returned to high, but not perfect, levels. This performance
bolsters our confidence that providing proper training in the ethical use of these
tools helped to level the playing field and allowed all students to demonstrate their
understanding. These results may suggest that equitable access to GenAI tools for
all students, along with instruction on how to use them, has the potential to reduce
equity gaps related to accessibility (Carvajal, Franco, and Isaksson 2024; James and
Andrews 2024; Weng and Fu 2025).
   To further understand performance on the applied questions, we investigated
the variability of scores (see Figure 3). Based on our pilot findings, we had
hypothesised that both the GenAI-available and the GenAI-integrated conditions
would have higher variability than the baseline condition (H3). The data partially
supported this hypothesis. Variability was dramatically higher for the
GenAI-available condition, but the variability returned to pre-GenAI levels under
the new format. We believe that this variability under the pre-GenAI and
GenAI-integrated conditions is an accurate reflection of the inherent variability
between students’ performance on these quizzes and that the elevated variability
during the GenAI-available period reflects differences in student willingness to
access a new and unauthorised technology, plus differences in the effectiveness
of being able to understand the output. When we trained all students to use
GenAI effectively for the applied sections of all the quizzes, the variability returned
to baseline.

Knowledge questions
The data partially supported our second hypothesis. The evidence suggests that our
underlying reasoning was sound; however, once again, our assumptions about both
the ubiquity of GenAI adoption and its effectiveness were influenced by our own
susceptibility to moral panic. As we knew that the knowledge questions were easily
answered by GenAI, we hypothesised that the availability of GenAI would increase
performance on these questions and that the new format would bring performance
back to baseline levels. We found that GenAI availability maintained performance
at baseline levels, whereas the GenAI-integrated condition resulted in performance
levels below baseline (see Figure 2).

   The pattern of responses suggests that some students used GenAI unethically
during the GenAI-available period, and that GenAI use was neither universal nor
universally effective. Previous studies have demonstrated that while many students
do choose to use GenAI unethically, the majority aim to supplement their learning
and avoid cheating–even when the rules and guidelines are unclear (Gamage et al.
2023; Prothero 2024). So, even during this relatively uncertain time, while the TAs
did catch several students actively using GenAI to answer questions, cheating was
not ubiquitous. To further understand this seemingly paradoxical finding that stu-
dents performed very similarly on the factual knowledge questions in the pre-GenAI
and the GenAI-available periods, we considered performance as a function of student
knowledge plus their access to correct answers through GenAI. This suggests two
possible interpretations for consistency in scores: 1) the students have learned the
material to similar levels, or 2) students have learned less in the GenAI-available
period and artificially boosted their scores on the quizzes by using GenAI.
   Bringing in evidence from the variability on these knowledge questions points to
the existence of underlying differences in the quality of learning and supports the
essence of H2: that some students used GenAI unethically to locate some answers.
Although there is no significant difference in central tendency, when we consider the
variability, we see that students in these two conditions behaved differently (see Figure
4). The students in the GenAI-available condition responded with significantly more
variability than the pre-GenAI condition on some of the quizzes. Despite the surface
similarity of the central tendency, we interpret the elevated variability in the
GenAI-available condition as supporting the interpretation that heterogeneous use of
GenAI masked underlying differences in learning and as evidence that some students
were cheating by unethically obtaining correct answers on some of the knowledge
questions using GenAI. These findings validated our decision to reduce the opportunity
to cheat on the knowledge questions by introducing the new quiz format.
   When we introduced the new format and delivered the knowledge quizzes on
paper, performance dropped significantly, as we hypothesised (see Figure 2). We
had hypothesised that this drop would return performance to baseline; however,
our data show that this drop moved performance below baseline. This supports the
interpretation that students in the GenAI-integrated quarters were less prepared to
take these quizzes than those in previous cohorts. A possible explanation for this
lower preparation is that the students in the new format had completed less hands-on
practice before taking the knowledge portion of the quiz. Part of the new format
included releasing the second dataset for each unit after the knowledge quiz. In
contrast, in the other two formats, the students had already practiced their skills
by summarising the second dataset before taking the knowledge quiz. Another
possible explanation, consistent with previous literature, is that students who engaged
with GenAI while working through the assignments may have found themselves
unprepared for the in-class assessments, having insufficiently studied or over-relied
on GenAI to summarise the content on their behalf (Abbas, Jam, and Khan 2024;
Wecks et al. 2024).
   The variability for the GenAI-integrated condition, however, was consistently low
and significantly lower than the other two conditions for most of the quizzes (see
Figure 4). By removing the opportunity for some students to use GenAI tools, where

others would not, we interpret this finding as evidence that using paper-and-pencil
assessments helped to create a more equitable environment in the classroom (James
and Andrews 2024).

Limitations
The primary limitations of this study stem from the small sample size and the
non-experimental design. Mining the data from existing cohorts allowed us to analyse
a natural experiment followed by an intentional intervention. We compared two
historical conditions in which students were indirectly exposed to different treatment
conditions, and then the curriculum was directly revised, and the effect was observed.
We found this opportunity to yield valuable results on how the availability of AI
may have altered performance in the classroom, specifically in a course where
technology was explicitly allowed and encouraged.
   The present analyses are based on a small number of naturally occurring course
cohorts (N = 8 sections across three conditions), which reflects a fundamental feature
of classroom-based research. Despite being a small number of cohorts, the obser-
vations of academic performance and variability were based on large numbers of
students (n = 84–202) for each quiz in each cohort. As a small-N design, the infer-
ential statistics reported here should be interpreted with caution. Given this sample
size, neither parametric nor non-parametric approaches are exempt from concerns
about violating assumptions. Trend-based non-parametric tests (e.g. Jonckheere-Terpstra
or Friedman) would be at the edge of their intended sample-size assumptions and,
more importantly, would not capture the possible interaction between AI availability
and time. A mixed-design ANOVA is conceptually appropriate for examining both
main effects and the interaction and provides a clearly interpreted framework. We
employed this test with the acknowledgment that assumptions of normality and
sphericity cannot be meaningfully evaluated at this sample size, and we report effect
sizes alongside inferential statistics to more fully describe the observed patterns.
   We cannot determine causation using this retrospective design. There are many
possible alternative explanations for the changes in performance across cohorts.
However, our analysis revealed that the three groups performed differently and that
dividing them according to GenAI availability accounted for 35% of the difference
in performance and 78-88% of the differences in variability. Alternate explanations
would need to be such that they impacted the AI-available cohorts differently from
previous cohorts and were also reduced by our curricular redesign. Our curricular
redesign, specifically testing knowledge questions without access to the internet and
training students to effectively use GenAI to demonstrate data summarising skills,
coincided with or may have caused changes in both performance and variability.

Conclusion
During the GenAI-available condition, performance on the applied questions
decreased, while performance on the knowledge questions remained at baseline. For
both question types, variability in scores mostly increased. We hypothesised that,

since students had access to GenAI during this period, they would use it effec-
tively–leading to uniformly higher scores. However, these data do not support this
hypothesis. Some students may have chosen not to use GenAI, and others may not
have been able to use it effectively. The decrease in scores suggests that these stu-
dents were overall not learning the material to the same proficiency as previous
cohorts, which is a different but equally troubling issue. The increase in variability
suggests that these conditions contributed additional variability on top of the expected
variations between student understanding, which may indicate decreased equity.
   Separating the knowledge from the applied questions in the new format quizzes
effectively resolved our concerns over both academic integrity and assessment authen-
ticity. We created an assessment of student knowledge that has high integrity and
an application of student skills that has high authenticity. As scores on the knowledge
portion remained below baseline, we clearly have more adjustments to make to
teach this cohort of students this knowledge. In the future, we can investigate the
factors contributing to this deficit by incorporating more practice (e.g. a third dataset
so that students can practice their skills before the knowledge portion) and by
working with students to understand how they are engaging with the tutorial material
to investigate if overreliance on GenAI might be hindering learning.
   As we all navigate this disruptive technology, it is important to monitor our own
hypotheses about student use of GenAI and student learning and to hold in mind
the core of our learning objectives and teaching philosophies. Though a disruptive
technology may initially seem overwhelming, we can draw strength from having
successfully navigated previous disruptions and find ways to design authentic assess-
ments of student learning under new circumstances.
