# The care-full craft of feedback in an age of generative AI

Naomi E. Winstone, Karen Gravett, Margaret Bearman, Christy Noble, Lasse X Jensen, Anna Jones & Kelli Nicola-Richmond (University of Surrey; Deakin University; University of Queensland; University of Copenhagen; King's College London)

Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, 51(5), 911-927, published online 18 Mar 2026. Open Access (CC BY-NC-ND 4.0). doi:10.1080/02602938.2026.2643333

ABSTRACT

The disruptive and transformative presence of Generative Artificial
Intelligence (GenAI) is rapidly reshaping education and learning. In
this paper, we consider how GenAI is redefining what it means for
students and educators to engage in feedback encounters, raising
questions not only about what matters in feedback, but also about
the very values that underpin meaningful learning. Taking stock of
this significant shift, we examine what we know about effective
feedback encounters and consider how GenAI technologies can be
integrated into a feedback ecosystem that still prioritises meaningful learning as its primary goal. In doing so, we consider feedback
encounters as matters of care, where a practice of care is something we do as scholars and educators. Arguing for the potential
value of multiple modes of feedback in an age of GenAI, we examine some of the ways in which a care-full crafting of feedback processes might be understood. These core principles include
recognising feedback encounters as more than comments; feedback as relational encounters; understanding the role of trust in
feedback encounters; and recognising the professional craft of
feedback. Our paper closes by offering directions for researchers
and educators in developing feedback processes within this evolving landscape.

For feedback theory-practice, the arrival of GenAI presents a definitive turning
point. After decades of research orientating practice away from transmission-focused
modes of information delivery towards more meaningful feedback encounters between
educators and students (Boud and Molloy 2013; Winstone and Carless 2019), students are increasingly engaging with GenAI technologies independently of educators,
and via modes and technologies that we are only beginning to understand. At the
same time, technosolutionist discourses tempt with their promises of making educators’ lives easier and more productive, prompting educators to critically question
the values and purposes of feedback practices. Taking stock of this significant shift,
we consider how GenAI technologies can be integrated into educational experiences
that prioritise meaningful learning as the primary goal. In doing so, we consider
feedback processes as ethical practices, and as matters of care (de la Bellacasa 2017;
McQuillan 2022), where a practice of care is something we do as scholars and
educators, and where meanings of care involve thinking about more than just what
humans do. We assert four key values for the future of feedback in an age of GenAI,
built upon our belief that matters of care are manifested by a commitment to holistic
approaches that resist reducing students to ‘instrumentalist and homogenised careless
motivations and aspirations’ (Motta and Bennett 2018, 636). From such a standpoint,
we consider a future where the entanglement of multiple modes of feedback, both
human and non-human, is the norm. We explore how to promote care-full feedback
practice and research, and we also consider what and whom might be lost, neglected,
or uncared for (McQuillan 2022) within such a landscape.

The role of GenAI in contemporary higher education: a focus on
feedback
With its ability to generate coherent text and analyse vast amounts of information
in seconds, many argue that GenAI has the potential to revolutionise marking and
feedback practices (Bearman, Ryan, and Ajjawi 2023). GenAI can grade assignments,
generate feedback information, and evaluate student work with notable speed and
accuracy, in some cases outperforming human evaluators, and demonstrating impressive capabilities (Corbin, Tai, and Flenady 2025).
At this point in the chronicle of GenAI feedback, research findings indicate that
human-generated feedback is still rated by students as superior in quality (Steiss
et al. 2024), helpfulness (Henderson et al. 2025), and clarity (Lu et al. 2024) in
comparison to AI-generated feedback, as well as more likely to be accepted and
acted upon by students (Lu et al. 2024). While much of the research focuses on
open or commercial GenAI systems rather than educator-developed chatbots for
particular feedback purposes, the roles of human and GenAI feedback can be
understood as fundamentally different, albeit potentially complementary. Corbin,

Tai, and Flenady (2025) argue that only human feedback processes can be recognitive, where recognition is defined as ‘the mutual acknowledgement of agency, vulnerability, and shared humanity between teacher and student’ (719). GenAI feedback
is described as ‘extra-recognitive’, because it is not capable of ethical judgement. In
this sense, the challenge is not to ask whether GenAI should replace human feedback, but how it might complement other feedback practices (Henderson et al. 2025).
GenAI is now firmly established within the feedback ecosystem (Corbin, Tai, and
Flenady 2025) and will become even more so as the sophistication and capability
of GenAI increases. While the integration of GenAI into feedback processes has
foregrounded new possibilities for personalisation, immediacy, and engagement, these
developments do not emerge in isolation. Rather, they intersect with evolving conceptualisations of what feedback is and what it does within learning environments.
To fully understand how GenAI is reshaping feedback practices, it is therefore
essential to situate these technological developments within broader shifts in the
representation and theorisation of feedback in academic discourse.

Framing feedback in higher education: from product to process
Recent decades have seen considerable work in the field of feedback. The key hallmark of developing conceptualisations of feedback is the shift away from viewing
feedback as the transmission of comments from giver to receiver (Boud and Molloy
2013; Carless 2015; Winstone and Carless 2019), evident in the topics discussed in
the research literature (Van der Kleij, Adie, and Cumming 2019), and the language
through which they are discussed (Winstone et al. 2022). An information-centric
view of feedback places emphasis on the delivery of comments from educator to
student, in a cognitivist paradigm (Winstone and Carless 2019). In contrast, a
process-oriented view of feedback foregrounds learning, recognising that feedback
is far more than just comments. In this view, feedback is what happens when students make sense of information about their performance and use it for future
improvement (Henderson et al. 2019).
When GenAI emerged with both a disruptive and transformative presence in
education in 2022, its potential inclusion within feedback processes soon became
apparent. Many commercial technologies which harness the power of Large Language
Models to provide feedback comments at unprecedented speed and scale appeared
to draw on outdated representations of feedback that focus on the transmission of
information from ‘provider’ to ‘receiver’. As long as GenAI feedback tools function
primarily as automated comment generators, there is a risk of replicating the feedback practices that scholars such as Sadler (1989) have critiqued as ‘dangling data’,
where information is transmitted to students but not used in meaningful ways. Even
the most detailed feedback comments provided by GenAI are of little value unless
those comments are understood and used in some way; thus, the use of GenAI to
produce feedback comments in place of human assessors does nothing to move us
beyond the well-rehearsed critiques of an information-centric view of feedback
(Corbin, Tai, and Flenady 2025). A further risk here is that we lose ground, as
educators, in ensuring that our students develop capabilities to make sense of feedback information required for their future performance and work.

The notion of feedback as a series of ‘encounters’ (Esterhazy 2018; Gravett and
Carless 2024) is a useful framing, as a feedback encounter positions feedback processes as emergent, sometimes from educator comments, but also from students’
own interactions with digital technologies, peers, or their own self-evaluation. For
example, students are regularly engaging in feedback encounters with chatbots
(Henderson et al. 2025; Jensen et al. 2025). These modes of feedback increasingly
appeal to students because of their ease of access, understandability, and because
they are perceived to be less daunting than seeking feedback from educators
(Henderson et al. 2025). However, educators must seek to understand the complex
interplay between the relational networks students exist within and the ways in
which emerging technologies such as GenAI are being used (Kahn et al. 2025).

Feedback processes as matters of care
Research has also extended the field to consider feedback as situated and relational
encounters (Esterhazy 2019; Gravett 2022; Jensen, Bearman, and Boud 2023), and
the power of such encounters when premised on care and affirmative ethics (Gravett
2023). Considering the affirmative is, wherever possible, about developing practices,
pedagogies and modes of knowledge production that are generative, collaborative
and non-competitive. This conception of feedback resonates with research that
foregrounds care and relationships within higher education whilst being mindful to
understand concepts of care and connection as complex and ambivalent relational
practices (Gravett, Taylor, and Fairchild 2024). Here, relationality and care include
the relations we have to each other, but also to and within a much broader, material,
world that includes all the spaces, objects, and technologies of education. Thinking
about feedback processes as matters of care, and as care-full, prompts educators to
consider teaching and learning not as a mode of information transmission, but as
affective and relational. Despite its ambivalences, care is understood as a vital force
(Care Collective 2020), and as something we can do as educators. Care can be
disruptive, and can offer a commitment to think about how things could be different
(de la Bellacasa 2017).
Thinking with matters of care, we argue for the importance of feedback encounters that seek to create more meaningful relationships and connections in higher
education, and that affirm the value of the labour and care invested in educational
practices. At the same time, we recognise the position of GenAI within an evolving
landscape that involves the integration of multiple modes of feedback, both human
and non-human. We suggest that the ways in which feedback encounters are crafted
to manage this entanglement also need to be practices of care.

The care-full integration of GenAI into feedback encounters
How might feedback processes be enacted as matters of care? This paper stems in
part from producing a Manifesto for Feedback in the Age of GenAI (Winstone et al.
2025; see Box 1 for a summary).
In writing these ten principles, we have identified four core values that frame a
care-full approach to feedback.

Feedback processes should support meaning-making
The care-full integration of GenAI into a multimodal feedback landscape means
that we must uphold ‘feedback’ as being more than comments or information.
Feedback information, whether originating from educators, peers, self-evaluation, or
GenAI, is merely the input into a process. These ‘bits and bytes’ of feedback information (Winstone et al. 2022, 214) are not unimportant, but in the absence of
student sense-making and uptake, do not constitute feedback (Henderson et al. 2019).
Feedback, we assert, is a complex process that is situated, iterative, affective, and
fundamentally relational (Bearman et al. 2025; Esterhazy 2019; Gravett 2022; Jensen
et al. 2025). We would contend that any institutional attempt to integrate GenAI
into the feedback landscape must start from this understanding. However, in contemporary higher education, pressure from rising costs and workload constraints
has made the outsourcing of assessment and feedback increasingly commonplace.
Even before GenAI, the outsourcing of feedback to sessional staff or digital tools
was framed as a way to shift human effort from mechanical marking to higher-value
interactions with students (Knight and Drysdale 2020).
The potential for GenAI to minimise human workload by automating the provision of comments is driven in part by the narrative surrounding the
workload-intensive nature of assessment and feedback activities in contemporary
education (Chan and Colloton 2024). A common argument for the use of AI is that
time spent on feedback is undesirable (e.g. described as ‘labour-intensive’; Lu et al.
2024, 617). But what is the broader value of this labour, to educators, to students,
to education? Do we want to eradicate this labour, as if it is solely problematic?
What does it mean for feedback quality when GenAI can only ‘know’ what is shared,
while much of academic and professional practice remains tacit, relational and
contextually bound? Recognising feedback as meaningful challenges the notion that
time spent on student work is solely a cost to be minimised, and instead upholds
feedback practices as matters of care.
By focussing on meaning-making, it becomes clear that quantity and speed are
not the same as quality. In the context of the general proliferation of AI-generated
content, Madsen and Puyt (2025) discuss the growth of ‘AI slop’, defined as

‘low-quality, mass-produced content’ (1). They argue that increasing volume and
velocity of information can negatively affect the value of that information (via erosion of its epistemic worth), and its verification (by limiting trust and reliability).
This leads us to interrogate the potential for ‘human slop’ too, as there are many
feedback practices enacted by humans that also focus on mass-produced speedy
information transfer, such as comment banks, ‘quick marks’, and standardised feedback templates. In a care-full approach to feedback in a time of GenAI, feedback
processes should instead foreground meaning-making.
Feedback processes should build educative relationships
Effective feedback is not merely corrective; it fosters a sense of connection between
student and educator. Indeed, perceptions of care and connection are critical to
feedback receptivity. Evidence shows that when such connection is minimised, for
example in anonymous grading practices, students report weaker relationships with
their educators and perceive feedback as less beneficial (Pitt and Winstone 2018).
McQuillan (2022, 115) describes care as an ‘epistemological corrective’ to AI, drawing
attention to learners’ situated vulnerability and dependency and counteracting the
detachment inherent in algorithmic systems. Care-full educational technologies, he
argues, must recognise the ways sociotechnical practices shape learning, refuse
neglectful or discriminatory design, and act responsibly in their educational interactions (McQuillan 2022). In contrast, thoughtless feedback, whether AI-generated,
automated comment banks, or quick marks, lacks relational and moral engagement:
‘starting from the principle of care is a counterproject to AI’s thoughtlessness and
“view from nowhere”’ (McQuillan 2022, 116). Whilst human feedback processes may
not necessarily involve presence and care, it may be even harder for GenAI feedback
agents to demonstrate these features (Chan and Colloton 2024). Students in a focus
group study by Otaki et al. (2026) acknowledged the benefit of being able to repeatedly seek feedback information from GenAI, but this repetition of interaction was
not accompanied by a feeling of ongoing connection; students described how ‘each
interaction [with GenAI] feels like a new beginning’ (10). In contrast, feedback
interactions within human relationships represent ‘a continuous timeline that builds
gradually over time, allowing for personalised guidance and support’ (10). Similarly,
in the context of doctoral education, Jensen et al. (2025) discuss how feedback
encounters with supervisors are sequential, situated, and often refer to previous
encounters, whereas interactions with ChatGPT are atemporal, only referring to the
current prompted interaction.
In general, humans prefer to gain information about their performance from
other humans as opposed to AI, and some students choose not to engage in feedback encounters with GenAI precisely because they value human connection
(Henderson et al. 2025). However, the presence of GenAI is already showing us
that a sense of human connection in feedback processes may not be the universal
benefit it might appear. There is an interesting paradox in the literature on
AI-generated feedback: for some students, interacting with AI-generated feedback
reduces the perceived personal risk of exposing weaknesses, asking ‘stupid’ questions,
or making mistakes (Henderson et al. 2025; Otaki et al. 2026; Zhan et al. 2025;

Zhan and Yan 2025). Corbin, Tai, and Flenady (2025) see the extra-recognitive
nature of GenAI feedback as an advantage in this respect, acting like a ‘pedagogical
sandbox’ (719) to enable students to try out ideas and gain feedback information
without subjecting themselves to the discomfort of human judgement. In these
situations, GenAI may facilitate feedback seeking by lessening emotional obstacles,
particularly in cultures with pronounced power hierarchies between educators and
students (Zhan et al. 2025). This highlights that while human connection is often
fundamental to trust, in some cases AI can serve as a complementary mechanism
to support learners’ engagement. However, this picture might also be more complex
than it first appears. Whether some students may have more confidence to approach
their educators for feedback, and to benefit from this expertise, is a long-standing
issue (Gravett and Winstone 2019) that may be exacerbated by the increase of such
‘easy access’ modes of information. Indeed, some argue that GenAI feedback might
come at the cost of a decline in meaningful interactions between educators and
students (Yildiz Durak and Onan 2025). In sum, while GenAI feedback may offer
a psychologically safe space for vulnerability, it risks undermining the priming of
students for the inevitable friction of feedback interactions in professional working life.
Feedback processes should be trustworthy
Trust is fundamental to meaningful education because learning is inherently vulnerable. Even before the rise of GenAI, research indicated that students evaluate
feedback considering the perceived expertise of the provider (Strijbos, Narciss, and
Dünnebier 2010; Van der Kleij and Lipnevich 2021). According to Sperber et al.
(2010), judgments about whether to accept or reject communicated information can
be directed at either the source (who to believe) or the content (what to believe).
Perceived credibility and expertise of the feedback giver influence learner responses,
affecting perceived trustworthiness, engagement, acceptance, motivation, and effort
(Carless 2012, Lechermeier and Fassnacht 2018). When credibility is in doubt,
receptivity suffers (Fulham, Krueger, and Cohen 2022; Hendriks, Kienhues, and
Bromme 2015).
To understand how learners decide whether to seek and accept feedback information, we turn to the concept of epistemic trust: a psychological stance that reflects
belief in the trustworthiness of an informant (Landrum, Eaves, and Shafto 2015).
Crucially, epistemic trust does not imply uncritical acceptance. Rather, it involves
epistemic vigilance: a balance between openness to learning and caution against the
risks of deception or misinformation (Hendriks, Kienhues, and Bromme 2015; Sperber
et al. 2010).
Recent studies highlight social biases in how trust is ascribed to humans compared
to AI as providers of feedback information. Ruwe and Kuklick (2026) explored how
German university students viewed feedback comments ostensibly from either a
human or AI (the comments were in fact the same) and varying in levels of quality.
They found that feedback is seen as more trustworthy when it is of high quality,
and that comments presented as AI-generated feedback are rated as less trustworthy
than those presented as human feedback. They suggest that students’ interpersonal

connection with, and perceived credibility of, human educators play a role. Similarly,
Henderson et al. (2025) surveyed Australian students and found that 90.5% reported
educator feedback information as somewhat or very trustworthy, compared with
only 60.1% for AI-generated feedback information. Key concerns included reliability
and credibility. Similarly, studies have shown that students evaluating ChatGPT
feedback expressed doubts about its accuracy (Tam 2025), particularly when comments diverged from context-specific guidance given by human educators (Zhan
and Yan 2025).
Nevertheless, trust is not static, it is dynamic. Hoff and Bashir (2015) emphasise
that trust develops over time, and repeated interactions with AI systems may increase
students’ confidence in them. Yet algorithm aversion (Dietvorst, Simmons, and
Massey 2015) demonstrates that while people may initially place trust in algorithms,
errors tolerated in humans are less acceptable in machines, leading to sharper
declines in trust when AI fails (Dietvorst, Simmons, and Massey 2015). Misleading
or false information, whether deliberate or accidental, can erode epistemic trust
(McCraw 2015). This has direct implications for GenAI, where known issues such
as bias, hallucinations, and factual inaccuracies threaten learners’ and educators’
confidence in the reliability of feedback, just as human errors can.
Students bring prior experiences of feedback, credibility, and authority to their
encounters with GenAI. If GenAI provides inaccurate, irrelevant, or biased feedback,
trust may erode rapidly; if it proves useful and reliable over time, trust may gradually grow. Yet limitations remain. Venter, Coetzee, and Schmulian (2025) identify
a series of challenges specific to GPT-4 in feedback contexts: outdated training data,
ambiguity in student prompts, over-generalisation, biases in training corpora, lack
of disciplinary depth, repetition and verbosity, and inaccuracies or hallucinations.
Such limitations strike at the very dimensions of expertise, integrity, and benevolence
that underpin epistemic trust.
To deepen this understanding, we shift from a psychological to a sociological
perspective. Trust is not a fixed trait, but a relational process. Drawing on Bourdieu,
Frederiksen (2014, 168) argues that trust is ‘embedded in relations’ and develops
through the situations and interactions in which agents engage one another. Trust,
therefore, accumulates over time as relationships become ‘carriers of trust’ (Frederiksen
2014, 169). Similarly, Möllering (2013) frames trust as a process of becoming: not
a fixed state but an ongoing practice of trusting. In this view, trust is best understood as a verb rather than a noun (Wright and Ehnert 2010), grounded in dynamic
and evolving interactions (Frederiksen 2014).
In sum, trust in feedback is not just about accuracy or authority, but about the
evolving relationship between learner and feedback source, whether human or AI.
By integrating psychological perspectives of epistemic trust with sociological relationality, we see a richer picture of how trust is built, challenged, and sustained in
feedback practices.
Feedback is a professional craft
Designing and participating in effective feedback encounters is not easy, and for
many educators (including ourselves), this represents a professional craft, or an art

(Otaki et al. 2026). Tuck’s ethnographic research on academics’ marking practices
(Tuck 2012, 2024) revealed the complex, non-linear, and context-sensitive nature of
feedback, and how comments do more ‘work’ and carry greater intention that the
mere words convey. This complexity is often realised through multimodal feedback,
where written feedback information is complemented by dialogic exchanges, enabling
clarification, responsiveness, and mutual understanding. Such multimodal feedback
practices, which blend written, verbal and digital modes, exemplify the professional
judgement and ethical sensitivity that characterise feedback as a craft.
In an age of GenAI, this professional craft increasingly demands skill in navigating
and integrating multiple feedback modalities, including dialogic and conversational
feedback. While AI systems may generate feedback information with speed and scale,
they lack the capacity for genuine dialogue: the back-and-forth exchange that enables
clarification, negotiation of meaning, and co-construction of understanding. Dialogic
feedback is not simply a pedagogical preference or contextualised construct; it is a
relational and interpretive practice that supports learners in making sense of feedback
information and integrating it into their future work. As GenAI becomes more
prevalent, the educator’s role in facilitating these multimodal feedback encounters
becomes even more critical: not diminished, but elevated and essential. Assessment
and feedback are never as simple as evaluating decontextualised words on a page.
As Tuck observes, educators’ ‘readings of texts were tangled up with ‘readings’ of
students’ (Tuck 2024, 82). One marker adjusted her written comments based on a
student’s personal circumstances, crafting reassurance and prioritising encouragement
over technical correction (Tuck 2024). Such attention to students’ ‘back stories’ influences not only what is written but also what is deliberately omitted, reflecting nuanced
professional judgement and empathy (Tuck 2024, 78). While AI-generated feedback
might offer immediate responses to student work, it cannot replace the nuanced
relational work that is foundational to the professional craft of multimodal feedback.
Of course, in many Higher Education Institutions, students’ work is assessed
anonymously, meaning that the ‘back stories’ of individual students may not be
accessible to educators. However, through the professional craft of feedback there
are still opportunities to be mindful of and respond to students’ ‘back stories’ even
with anonymous assessment. For example, an assessor may choose not to comment
on every error for a student who is clearly needing to master some threshold competencies. This approach also enables assessors to connect to students’ ‘future stories’
by crafting feedback comments in ways that relate to future assessments, in-class
activities, etc. These perspectives are also echoed by students in Otaki et al.’s (2026)
study. Students, who were non-native speakers of English, expressed a preference
for human feedback over AI-generated feedback due to the more nuanced ability
to identify specialised elements of their work and guide students towards improvement. Students also valued the subjective experiences of humans where educators
imbue their comments with experiences and expertise. In this way, then, students
placed a ‘premium on pedagogical expertise’ (10). In the same study, educators
described how, when writing feedback comments, they used their judgement to take
into account ‘intricate emotional dynamics’ (12). This emphasis on ethical judgement
and attention to context reflects broader debates about the nature of teaching as an
art and craft rather than a purely mechanistic process (Felix 2020; Selwyn 2022).

Also important to feedback as a professional craft is the embodiment by feedback
agents of ethical practice. While AI systems are often framed as objective tools,
they inherit biases embedded in their training datasets, and prompt important
questions regarding data protection and privacy, and the power of technology corporations. Indeed, the increasing dominance of GenAI within education has been
accompanied by legitimate critical attention and ethical concern (Selwyn 2022;
Williamson and Eynon 2020). Human educators are not immune to bias, but they
can engage in reflection, training, and institutional practices to identify and mitigate
biases. Through ongoing discussions about equity and fairness, educators can make
nuanced judgments that align with ethical principles and the individual needs of
students, something AI systems are ill-equipped to replicate.
A care-full approach to integrating GenAI into multimodal feedback encounters
requires us to consider the unintended consequences of the presence of GenAI on
educators’ professional craft. The processes of assessment and feedback are important
for educators too, in seeing evidence of student understanding and development
within their work. Even if human educators remain ‘in-the-loop’ and approve
AI-generated feedback comments for quality before they are released to students,
there is still a risk that biases in the judgments made by AI can be internalised by
humans leading to them becoming more biased themselves (e.g. Glickman and
Sharot 2025). Furthermore, if the primary generation of feedback information is
outsourced to GenAI, there is a risk of stagnation in educators’ skill in the craft of
feedback, leading to ‘unthinking’ (Costa and Murphy 2025, 5). In this context,
maintaining and developing feedback skills becomes not only a pedagogical priority
but a professional necessity.

Feedback and GenAI: an agenda for the future
In times of uncertainty, values and principles can act as a compass to support
navigation across an unfamiliar terrain. The values discussed in this paper, alongside
the manifesto principles, are not exhaustive. However, we have argued for the
importance of considering the current and potential role of GenAI as an agent in
a multimodal feedback landscape whilst holding true to education as a matter of
care. How might these values influence future developments in research and practice,
and how might researchers and educators respond to the diversity and breadth of
new modes and conceptions of feedback within a changing landscape? We now turn
to a discussion of four areas through which we believe a care-full approach to the
integration of GenAI in feedback can guide decision-making in both research and
practice.
Respect for scholarship
Assessment and feedback is a thriving subfield of higher education research. Over
the past decade, there have been significant reformulations of our understanding of
feedback, that should continue to inform feedback processes in higher education.
For practice, this means that the design for integration of GenAI in feedback
encounters should be intentional and recognise established and emerging

conceptualisations of what feedback is and does. For example, given that there is
strong agreement within the literature that comments, on their own, do not constitute feedback, designing AI systems that generate comments, no matter how
copious or instantaneous, represents the replication of outdated representations of
feedback within new modes of technology. A more generative approach to the integration of GenAI, and one that adopts a care-full respect for scholarship, would be
to start from challenges we know exist in feedback encounters and ask ‘what role
can GenAI play in addressing these challenges?’ For example, assessment tasks in
different modules or units in courses are not commonly connected. It is unlikely
that an individual educator who assesses students’ work in one module/unit will
then assess their work in a subsequent module/unit. This creates a barrier to ipsative
feedback (e.g. Malecka and Boud 2023), where students gain insight into how their
work has developed or improved relative to their previous work. A potentially beneficial deployment of GenAI would be an assessment and feedback system which,
rather than focusing on grading and commenting on assignments in isolation, creates
a continuous analysis of how students’ skills, thinking, and conceptual understandings
are developing over time. By tracking students’ progress over time, not only is
feedback information potentially more beneficial and motivating than feedback
information on isolated pieces of work, it would also give educators insight into
where students can be enabled to further develop particular skills or competencies
(Chan and Colloton 2024).
For researchers, respect for scholarship means asking and seeking to address
challenging questions using a panoply of complementary methods that build a rich
tapestry of the reality of the entanglement of GenAI in a multimodal feedback
landscape. In the field of feedback research, there have been calls for the use of
methods beyond student self-report, that seek to understand behaviour and outcomes,
not just opinions and beliefs (Winstone and Nash 2023). The same approach can
benefit research into the impacts of GenAI feedback, by exploring how learners and
educators interact with multiple modes of feedback and the impact on learning,
identity, and skills development.
Respect for equity
Integrating GenAI into feedback ecosystems requires a renewed focus on our ethical
and moral imperatives to support student equity of opportunity, and to continue to
prioritise meaningful educational practices as matters of care. For practice, a critical
consideration for any intentional use of GenAI is ensuring that all students have
equitable access to meaningful feedback encounters, however they are mediated, and
to respect the values of those students who conscientiously object to using GenAI.
Equally important is to consciously avoid making assumptions about who will benefit
and how from the integration of GenAI into the feedback landscape.
For research, not enough is known about the everyday ways in which students
engage with GenAI, and the impact of their practices on processes of inclusion and
exclusion, and thus on equity more generally. It will be important to understand how
students’ engagement with GenAI feedback influences well-established attainment
differentials between different groups of students (e.g. Cabral-Gouveia, Menezes, and

Neves 2023). An essential part of future feedback processes, we argue, will be to
scaffold the development of feedback literacies and evaluative judgement (Bearman
et al. 2024), enabling all students to critically evaluate and act upon multiple modes
of feedback. Failure to pay attention to the development of such capacities runs the
risk of amplifying existing differentials in student attainment. In education, ‘Matthew
Effects’ represent the likelihood that advantage begets further advantage (see, for
example, Walberg and Tsai 1983). Students who have already developed advanced
capital in this regard stand to reap greater benefit from feedback encounters with
GenAI, exacerbating the divide between those with and without this pre-existing capital.
Respect for professional craft
We have framed educators’ engagement in feedback encounters as often drawing
upon a professional craft. For practice, this means that any integration of GenAI
into a multimodal feedback landscape needs to be respectful of the craft. One such
approach is to distinguish what is unique to relationship-rich feedback encounters
that GenAI cannot currently replicate (e.g. Costa and Murphy 2025). This includes
the ability to design and enact care-full, multimodal feedback, blending written,
verbal and digital forms, to support student learning in nuanced and responsive
ways. Respect for feedback as a professional craft also means protecting against the
danger that the affordances of meaningful feedback encounters might become deprioritised due to their labour intensity and purported inefficiency. Care-full, multimodal
feedback, while potentially more time-consuming, enables educators to uphold the
values of attentiveness, responsiveness and relationality, qualities that are central to
meaningful learning.
For research, we believe it is imperative that attention is directed towards the
impacts on educators’ identity and practice as their feedback practices become
entangled with those of other agents like GenAI. For example, what happens to
educators’ understandings of their students and their learning when they do not
engage to the same depth with student work through the processes of assessment
and feedback? And is there any evidence that the craft of feedback becomes eroded
when educators become one of a multitude of feedback modes? If educators increasingly rely on GenAI to generate written comments, there is a risk that their skills
in enacting effective multimodal feedback, particularly in facilitating dialogic or
conversational feedback, may diminish over time. If outsourcing can have an effect
on cognitive processes in humans, then concerns about skill atrophy (e.g. Ganuthula
2024) and metacognitive laziness (Fan et al. 2025) that have been expressed in
relation to students’ reliance on GenAI for academic tasks may also be relevant to
educators’ feedback craft.
Respect for human connection
Meaningful feedback can be empowering and affecting. It can offer a moment of
connection and affirmation, where we can demonstrate we value another, or feel
that we ourselves are valued. In a world underpinned by individualism, competition
and speed, such opportunities for connections matter. These connections can be

fostered through multimodal feedback to enhance presence and responsiveness. For
practice, this means that we need to understand how key concepts such as trust,
connection and engagement are evolving with GenAI technologies, and we must
find ways to preserve the situated, meaningful, and care-full elements of feedback
encounters. For research, it is important to understand the impact of the integration
of GenAI on how relationships between students and other agents in the landscape
evolve and are affected over time, and in practice.

Conclusion
There is much more to learn about the entanglement of GenAI with feedback: what
it means for students, educators, and the very nature of educational practices. We
believe that developing a care-full approach to feedback encounters, and continually
asking how we can evaluate and evolve our practice, remain critical endeavours that
respect scholarship, equity, the professional craft of feedback, and the principle and
practice of relationality.
