# Feedback futures: beyond the limits of human and GenAI capacities

Ying Zhan, James Wood, David Carless & Zi Yan (The Education University of Hong Kong; Durham University; University of Hong Kong)

Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, 51(5), 811-820, published online 02 Aug 2026. Editorial (special issue). doi:10.1080/02602938.2026.2672830

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) disrupts university assessment systems by
offering students tools that produce plausible text on demand. It shifts feedback into a
new phase, not only by making it faster, but by introducing a new kind of feedback
source into the pedagogic landscape, whose role in supporting student learning may be
among its most educationally productive yet underexplored uses, particularly in contrast
to its dominant framing as an assessment challenge.
Feedback scholarship has long challenged the idea of feedback as one-way transmission
from teacher to student, instead emphasising dialogue, learner engagement, judgement,
action and learner agency as central to meaningful feedback processes (Buckley 2025;
Carless and Boud 2018; Wood and Pitt 2025). GenAI now enters that conversation as
an immediate, interactive, and always-available source of feedback, capable of producing
human-like dialogues with students at scale (Zhan et al. 2025). This is significant because
feedback has long been one of higher education’s most valued yet most challenging
pedagogic practices, constrained by workload, massification, time pressure, and limited
opportunities for sustained dialogue. As a result, feedback often reaches students only
after submission, when the window for formative uptake has already passed (Henderson,
Ryan, and Phillips 2019).
Yet feedback is far from students’ most frequent use of GenAI. Large-scale survey
evidence from Australia consistently shows that feedback-seeking, while reported by
around half of GenAI users (Henderson et al. 2026; Chung et al. 2026), sits downstream
of more immediate task-oriented uses. Students are making complex and not always
critical judgements about when and how to rely on GenAI outputs, including adopting
ideas with low scrutiny, submitting content with minimal appraisal, and in some cases
genuinely failing to recognise the extent of GenAI’s contribution to their own submitted
work (Walton et al. 2025). Institutional responses to GenAI have nonetheless often
outpaced robust and ecologically grounded evidence about how students are actually
using these tools, creating a risk of policy and pedagogic responses that are reactive
rather than informed (Chung et al. 2026).
The significance of GenAI for feedback extends beyond a problem of such institutional
lag. It touches some of the most pressing questions now facing higher education: what
counts as authorship and academic work, how students develop the capacity to judge
quality, and whether assessment systems are organised to reward genuine learning or
mainly its performative appearance. GenAI matters, then, not simply because it has
become another feedback source, but because it unsettles assumptions about what feedback is for, who or what can provide it, and how it can remain genuinely educative.

This editorial for the special issue argues that the central question goes beyond
whether GenAI feedback is useful, asking instead how human and GenAI feedback can
be combined to sustainably support learning rather than merely improve immediate
performance. Drawing on the seven papers in this special issue and wider literature, we
suggest that teacher and student feedback literacy is necessary but not sufficient for
such a combination to realise its potential in supporting the kind of learning that persists
beyond task completion. What it also requires is deliberate pedagogic design, institutional
conditions that prioritise learning over efficiency, and the purposeful development of
human judgement that cannot be delegated to the tool itself.
The papers address this across several interconnected concerns. First, they consider
the temporal shift GenAI introduces by enabling feedback during production rather than
after submission. This shift can reduce emotional resistance, support iteration, and give
students greater control over when they access support. Second, they ask whether this
change genuinely expands access to learning support or whether it increasingly substitutes
for student authorship. Third, they examine questions of trust, uptake, agency, and
equity, and how these shape the feedback relations GenAI is reconfiguring and with
what consequences. Those are the questions to which we now turn.

Five tensions in GenAI feedback
Our reading across the papers in this special issue identifies five recurring tensions that
clarify what is now at stake in GenAI feedback in higher education. These tensions
arise from the interaction between student practices, pedagogic design, and institutional
conditions, helping to explain why the availability of GenAI feedback does not easily
translate into educative value.
Taken together, the papers do more than document emerging practices. They also
point to a tendency to frame GenAI feedback as a response to longstanding challenges
of scale, timing, workload, and access while positioning the tool itself as the intervention
point. Whereas this perspective foregrounds efficiency, uptake, and output quality, it
risks obscuring the conditions under which feedback becomes truly developmental. The
five tensions that follow give that risk a more precise shape.
The first tension concerns the interplay between usefulness, trust, and uptake. GenAI
is valued for its speed, clarity, and accessibility, whereas teacher feedback is more readily
trusted because it is associated with contextual understanding, disciplinary expertise,
accountability, and human connection (Henderson et al. 2026; Winstone et al. 2026;
Zhan and Yan 2026). That distinction matters because uptake is not simply a function
of informational quality. Students receive, interpret, and act on feedback differently
depending on whether they experience care, recognition, and genuine human presence
in the exchange (Jensen et al. 2026; Winstone et al. 2026; Wood 2023). These relational
conditions remain difficult for GenAI to reproduce, and their absence shapes not only
whether feedback is believed, but whether it is engaged with at all (Henderson et al. 2026).
The second tension is between immediate task achievement and longer-term learning.
GenAI is especially effective at helping students perform the task at hand (Tlili et al.
2025), but this may come at the expense of deeper learning and the development of
evaluative capability over time (Hawkins, Taylor-Griffiths, and Lodge 2026; Jensen et al.
2026). Evidence across the studies suggests that GenAI feedback can orient students
towards performance and avoidance goals rather than mastery, prioritising task completion over the development of evaluative judgement (Hawkins, Taylor-Griffiths, and Lodge
2026). Zhan and Yan (2026) lend further empirical weight to this concern by showing
that students’ metacognitive engagement with ChatGPT feedback was weaker than their

cognitive engagement, and that interactions were often one-off rather than iterative. This
points to a form of feedback use oriented towards superficial local revision and high
uptake, rather than the development of transferable learning over time.
The third tension is between agency and dependency. Agency in feedback is not
simply a matter of receiving comments, but of seeking, questioning, and deciding how
or whether to use them (Lee et al. 2024; Wood 2023). In GenAI contexts, agency may
be extended through iterative prompting, refinement, and comparison across responses.
But it may also become thinner than it first appears, particularly where students remain
active at the level of interaction while ceding much of the evaluative work to the system
itself (Zhan and Yan 2026; Walton et al. 2025). Jensen et al. (2026) sharpen this point
by showing that while doctoral supervisors use questioning and perspective-taking to
prompt reflection, chatbots tend to frame feedback as steps to be taken, making revision
easier while leaving less room for judgement processes to be worked through. What
matters is whether students retain control of the judgments that count: whether they
can question GenAI outputs, decide what role they should play, and take genuine
responsibility for the work they submit (Bearman et al. 2024). The evidence suggests
that for many students, this threshold is not yet being met.
The fourth tension is between access, avoidance, and advantage. GenAI is unlikely
to benefit all students equally, and evidence suggests that it does not do so in practice
(Beckman et al. 2025). Some are better positioned than others to convert access into
educational advantage, while others remain cautious, avoidant, or hesitant in their use.
Students who reported not using GenAI for feedback in Henderson et al. (2026) cited
concerns about trustworthiness, preferences for human feedback, and academic integrity,
despite using it in other ways for their studies. Access alone, then, does not guarantee
uptake, and productive use depends in part on student feedback literacy (Hawkins,
Taylor-Griffiths, and Lodge 2026) and broader AI literacies. The issue is therefore not
only whether GenAI is available, but who is able, willing, and supported to use it to
genuine educational benefit, and what that support needs to look like at module, programme, and institutional levels.
Whereas the first four tensions principally concern student decision-making and
agency, the fifth shifts attention to teachers’ professional judgement and the redistribution
of their labour. GenAI may reduce some burdens of feedback production, but it redistributes that labour rather than removing it. Teachers still need to assess accuracy, tone,
relationality, and pedagogical value, and without careful design and oversight, GenAI
tools can potentially increase rather than reduce workload (Aldino et al. 2026; Winstone
et al. 2026). The issue is therefore not whether GenAI can make feedback faster, but
what kinds of professional judgement and labour remain necessary to ensure that faster
feedback is still educationally worthwhile.
These tensions do not operate in isolation. Questions of equity are shaped in turn
by institutional design, by the forms of feedback universities signal that they value, and
by assessment arrangements that may privilege endpoint performance over the kinds of
learning that feedback is meant to cultivate. Read together, these papers offer a wider
empirical and conceptual view of where feedback in higher education now stands, where
it may be heading, and what course corrections may be needed.

GenAI and human feedback: conditions of partnership
Our reading across the special issue suggests that GenAI and human feedback are best
understood as complementary, but only if that complementarity is specified rather than
assumed. The question is not simply whether both have a role, but what each enables,

under what conditions, and with what effects. The issue, then, is not replacement, but
the careful sequencing and design of feedback processes, together with the staff understanding, training, and pedagogic judgement needed to enact them well. Even before
GenAI, the circumstances in which feedback becomes genuinely developmental are
demanding and often only partially realised in practice. While this challenge did not
begin with GenAI, its emergence has sharply accelerated the need for reforms that
institutions previously hesitated to prioritise.
Read together, the papers point not to a simple division between human and GenAI
feedback, but to an ecology of configured encounters in which feedback is sequenced,
compared, edited, and governed across multiple sources and moments. Aldino et al.
(2026) suggest that GenAI can function as a reflective scaffold for teachers reviewing
their own feedback, drawing attention to missing learner-centred components and prompting reconsideration of its balance, focus, and structure. Jensen et al. (2026) hone this
further by showing that the difference is not only relational but temporal: supervisory
feedback draws on shared history and orients students towards longer-term development,
while chatbot feedback does not in itself constitute the kind of pedagogic relationship
through which developmental judgement is built and sustained. Winstone et al. (2026)
reinforce this by advocating an approach that foregrounds scholarship, professional craft,
equity, and human connection as the foundations of that partnership. That makes the
idea of complementarity more meaningful, but it also brings a subtler risk into view:
not replacement so much as displacement. The danger is that the speed and convenience
of GenAI-generated feedback, especially when paired with near-instant redrafting, may
gradually displace the slower, more careful work of educational judgement without anyone
consciously deciding that it should.
A consistent principle follows from this: human agency must be proactively maintained
rather than assumed. When GenAI is used to support feedback, humans remain responsible for judging, adapting, and, where necessary, rejecting GenAI-generated responses.
This matters because the risk is not only that GenAI replaces human connection, but
that it may further entrench what Winstone et al. (2026) describe as ‘human slop’:
low-quality, standardised feedback produced through comment banks, and anonymous
or template responses that already weaken feedback practices in many institutions.
Whether GenAI reinforces or unsettles that pattern depends on the capacities students
and teachers bring to these encounters, as well as on the pedagogic and institutional
conditions under which those encounters are designed and governed. It is to those
capacities that we now turn.

Feedback literacy and the effective use of GenAI feedback
If GenAI and human feedback can, under certain conditions, be complementary, then
the key question becomes what enables that complementarity to function educationally
rather than merely procedurally. Across the papers in this issue, the answer lies less in
the nature of the tool itself than in the capacities students and teachers bring to it.
Hawkins, Taylor-Griffiths, and Lodge (2026), for example, found that in a GenAI-enhanced
essay-writing task, feedback literacy was a significant predictor of performance, whereas
frequency of GenAI use, trust in GenAI, and prior knowledge were not. The implication
is not that GenAI is inconsequential for learning, but that its educational value is shaped
by the learner’s capacity to seek, evaluate, and act on feedback.
Existing accounts of student feedback literacy (Carless and Boud 2018; Molloy, Boud,
and Henderson 2020; Zhan 2022), already understood to be context-sensitive, therefore
need to be extended rather than applied unchanged to GenAI settings. Papers in this

special issue help specify that extension by identifying GenAI-specific capacities spanning
evaluative, metacognitive, behavioural, affective, and ethical dimensions. Ethical
decision-making is especially important: Zhan and Yan (2026) identify it as a key capacity,
while Cui et al. (2026) treat feedback ethics as a distinct dimension of GenAI feedback
literacy. This involves not only recognising the contribution of GenAI tools but also
making judgments about when and how their use supports rather than substitutes for
students’ own intellectual work. Upholding academic integrity requires students to critically assess and interpret GenAI feedback rather than accepting it at face value, and
to make principled decisions about the timing, manner, and purpose of its use so that
their work remains a genuine reflection of their own capabilities (Zhan and Yan 2026).
Of the capacities discussed across the issue, evaluative judgement and metacognitive
skill are particularly salient. Evaluative judgement matters because GenAI-generated
feedback can appear authoritative even when it is inaccurate, misleading, or hallucinatory
(Hawkins, Taylor-Griffiths, and Lodge 2026; Henderson et al. 2026; Winstone et al.
2026), and students need to distinguish useful feedback from convincing but unreliable
output (Bearman et al. 2024; Hawkins, Taylor-Griffiths, and Lodge 2026). Metacognitive
skills matter because students need to monitor when GenAI support is helping them
think more effectively, when it is narrowing that thinking, and when it is beginning to
substitute for their own reflective work (Zhan and Yan 2026; Walton et al. 2025).
Together, these capacities shape how students configure their interactions with GenAI:
what background they supply about their work, what uncertainties they surface, and
what kind of response they decide would be genuinely useful. This deliberate situating
of oneself in relation to the work is itself a form of feedback seeking, and arguably a
more pedagogically significant one than technical prompt refinement alone (cf. Zhan
and Yan 2026).
Teacher feedback literacy in GenAI contexts remains less well theorised, although
the Carless and Winstone (2023) framework, spanning design, relational, and pragmatic
dimensions, offers a useful starting point. In the design dimension, teachers need to
design assessment sequences so that GenAI and human input are used productively
rather than substituted for one another, for instance, through drafting, GenAI-supported
reflection, and revision cycles that require students to interpret and decide rather than
simply accept generated output (Jensen et al. 2026; Chen and Carless 2026). This also
requires teachers to design workable feedback workflows through which GenAI and
human input are deliberately sequenced, monitored, and made mutually accountable,
rather than simply added together.
In the relational dimension, through a laboratory experiment, Aldino et al. (2026)
show that teachers introduce human feeling into GenAI-generated feedback, moderate
praise and relationship-building language generated by the tool, and calibrate affective
tone to align with their own pedagogic voice. Yet this picture is complicated by a wider
pattern in the literature. Henderson et al. (2026) found that teacher feedback was more
frequently described as negative in tone and interpersonally risky than GenAI feedback.
Weidlich et al. (2025), in a blinded experiment controlling for source-credibility effects,
found teacher feedback was rated as significantly less fair and less acceptable than LLM
feedback, and associated with lower willingness to revise despite resulting in stronger
improvements in student work. As Kaliisa et al. (2026) note in their meta-analysis of
41 studies, the human feedback quality on which assumptions of relational superiority
rest was rarely verified or reported in the primary studies reviewed and remains theoretical. Relational oversight, then, cuts in both directions, and while GenAI does not
exercise it autonomously, it can perform aspects of it when deliberately designed into
the feedback process. The human recognitive work of feedback, anticipating how

comments will land and how they may be interpreted by particular students in light of
their histories, circumstances, and needs (Jensen et al. 2026; Winstone et al. 2026; Corbin,
Tai and Flenady 2025), is as likely to involve amplifying or repairing the deficits of
human feedback as it is tempering GenAI excess.
In the pragmatic dimension, teachers need clarity about what must remain human-led
if feedback is to support deeper learning. Jensen et al. (2026) elucidate this point by
suggesting that what must remain human-led is not only relational connection but
accountable judgement: a supervisor can defend advice, contextualise uncertainty, and
take responsibility for the directions they recommend in ways a chatbot cannot. At the
institutional level, ensuring equitable access to meaningful feedback encounters, regardless
of students’ differential access to commercial GenAI tools, remains a structural responsibility that teachers alone cannot discharge (Winstone et al. 2026).

Where the conversation should go next
The field now needs to move beyond asking how and when GenAI feedback is useful,
towards more precise questions brought into focus by the tensions identified across this
issue: questions of governance, assessment design, and the development of student and
teacher literacies for GenAI-rich contexts, together with the research agenda needed to
inform these priorities.
Governance is a particularly complex challenge. Conventional university policy-making
is largely led by senior academics, but they may be remote from the nuances of student
co-construction of work with GenAI tools. GenAI policy cannot be just implemented
top-down but is negotiated and contested through the interactions of multiple actors
and contexts (Tsao 2025). The extent of GenAI support which is acceptable, desirable
or recommended is likely to vary across courses, disciplines and teachers. Where clear
institutional direction has been absent or deliberately kept ambiguous, responsibility has
tended to migrate downwards from institution to department and from department to
individual teachers, in ways that individualise the challenge without resolving it (see for
example Orfanidis 2025; Tsao 2025). The consequences are felt on both sides: teachers
bear the emotional labour of making consequential decisions without institutional backing, while students navigate a landscape in which the rules governing GenAI use can
differ from one course to the next.
A pressing issue is therefore not only how feedback is generated, but how assessment
systems are reformed: how institutions address authorship, transparency, accountability, and
ethical use in ways that are pedagogically grounded rather than merely reactive. The development, updating and enactment of GenAI policies necessitate various dilemmas: policy-making
risks becoming rapidly outdated; flexibility is necessary but risks vagueness; enforcement is
a minefield and surveillance software is more likely to be a problem than a solution, as they
tend to produce false positives and erode trust regardless of outcome. These kinds of threats
to trust, student-teacher relationships and institutional reputation are readily apparent when
originality of student work becomes contested (Luo 2024). The success of policy and governance also depends on whether students are supported to develop the ethical and evaluative
capacities needed to make informed, contextually situated decisions about when, how, and
why GenAI should be used.
Closely connected to this is assessment design, because many of the tensions identified
across this issue intensify when institutional practice treats final products as direct evidence
of learning rather than as proxies for it. Without structural change (Corbin, Dawson, and
Liu 2025), GenAI amplifies this problem by making fluent performance easier to produce
while further obscuring the processes, judgments, and forms of participation through which

learning is developed and demonstrated. What is needed, then, is an assessment design that
makes these more visible and valued, and that is precise about the kinds of capability being
cultivated. The challenge is for institutions and programmes to be deliberate about when
GenAI use is itself part of the learning being assessed, when it is not, and to ensure that
pedagogic arrangements for both exist in practice, something that, in our experience, currently
remains rare.
The field also needs research that gets closer to what actually happens when students and
teachers encounter GenAI feedback in real settings, how it is interpreted, acted on, questioned,
or set aside, and how those encounters shape, or fail to shape, the evaluative capacities that
feedback is meant to develop over time. Methodologically, this points towards approaches
that can capture feedback use in situ: think-aloud protocols, trace or log data, screenshot-based
elicitation, discourse analysis of prompts, responses, and follow-up dialogue, such as the
stimulated recall method used by Zhan and Yan (2026). These approaches are likely to be
especially important if the field is to understand not just what GenAI produces, but how
learners and teachers actually work with it during feedback encounters.
Beyond how individual feedback encounters are processed lies a further and still underexamined question: how learners coordinate across the multiple feedback sources now available to
them: human, GenAI, and artefactual, while retaining agency over their own learning and
responsibility for judgement and decision-making. Feedback literacy in GenAI contexts requires
more than knowing how to interpret or act on individual sources; it also requires what Wood
and Pitt (2025) describe as the capacity to orchestrate feedback across sources that differ in
credibility, relevance, and pedagogical value. In GenAI contexts, that capacity must extend to
weighing and deciding how to use a source that is immediate, fluent, and always available. Yet
how learners develop and enact that capacity, and what pedagogic designs best support its
development, remains insufficiently understood. Research that moves beyond self-report and
surface indicators of output quality will be especially important if the field is to distinguish
between feedback that improves immediate output and feedback that develops learners’ longer-term
capacities (Hawkins, Taylor-Griffiths, and Lodge 2026; Jensen et al. 2026; Zhan and Yan 2026).
The same applies to teachers: how sustained engagement with GenAI tools shapes teacher
feedback literacy, professional judgement, and pedagogical practice over time remains largely
unexamined. Developing empirically grounded models of teacher feedback literacy specific
to GenAI contexts, ones that can inform professional development and guide the design of
feedback practices, should be a priority. Without sustained attention to both learner and
teacher capacity, feedback literacy frameworks risk describing what participants should do
without clarifying how they can learn to do it.
Equity cannot remain an afterthought. The tensions between access, avoidance, and advantage identified across this issue point to a research agenda that remains in its early stages.
We know that differential access to GenAI tools, feedback literacy, and institutional support
shapes who benefits and who does not. We know far less about how those gaps develop over
time, how they interact with disciplinary context and assessment design, and what forms of
deliberate teaching, scaffolding, and equitable provision are effective. Research that tracks
equity not only at the level of tool access but at the level of educationally productive use,
asking who is able to configure GenAI interactions in ways that genuinely support learning
and under what conditions, should be a priority for the field.

Conclusion
This special issue shows that GenAI is much more than a faster channel for feedback.
It involves reorganising feedback encounters, redistributing pedagogic labour, and

reimagining the role of feedback. Across the papers, a coherent but unsettled picture
emerges: GenAI expands access, responsiveness, and student-elicited support, while,
where conditions and capacities allow, human feedback remains distinctive, in offering
contextual judgement, accountability, and relational presence.
At the same time, this editorial resists a romanticised view of human feedback. There
remains a substantial gap between what feedback scholarship says feedback ought to be and
what it often becomes in institutional practice. Long before GenAI, the quality of feedback
processes in many settings was already undermined by time pressure, impersonality, and
the need to serve accountability as much as learning. GenAI has not created such problems,
but it has made the field’s longstanding difficulty in addressing them harder to ignore.
What this editorial clarifies, more than it resolves, is what is now at stake in debates
about GenAI feedback. The key question goes beyond whether GenAI can generate
useful responses, towards asking what kinds of feedback encounters the sector wants to
design, value and protect. Most importantly, the field needs to hold onto a distinction
that now matters more than ever: between outputs that substitute human effort to get
work done and processes that truly benefit learners. Whether that distinction is preserved
or eroded will shape not only the future of feedback, but also the credibility of what
universities claim to recognise and reward.
