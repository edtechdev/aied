# GenAI Knowledge, Epistemic Orientation, and Intellectual Values Predict Undergraduate Students' Critical GenAI Use

Markus H. Hefter, Benjamin Paaßen & Kirsten Berthold (Bielefeld University)

AI Educ. (MDPI), 2, 27, published 02 Aug 2026. Open Access (CC BY 4.0). doi:10.3390/aieduc2030027

Abstract
Critically using generative AI (genAI) tools, such as comparing their outputs with other
sources or the literature, is vital for students to avoid risks of reduced cognitive processing
and inaccurate results. We propose two potential protective factors against these risks:
knowledge about genAI and the disposition to engage in critical thinking encompassing
epistemic orientation (i.e., a tendency to move away from more absolutist beliefs toward
more evaluativist beliefs) and intellectual values (i.e., viewing intellectual engagement
as worthwhile). Our correlational study with 67 undergraduate psychology students
(59 female; mean age = 22.58) aimed to investigate how these factors predict students’
critical use of genAI. Regression and correlation analyses revealed that both knowledge
about genAI and the disposition to engage in critical thinking predicted the critical use
of genAI. Exploratory analyses revealed that students’ knowledge about genAI partly
comprises misconceptions (i.e., incorrect knowledge with high confidence) and their critical
genAI use correlates with need for cognition (NFC). These findings suggest that higher
education institutions should implement interventions targeting conceptual misconceptions
as well as epistemic orientation and intellectual values. Consequently, future research
should experimentally evaluate such interventions to foster responsible AI engagement in
higher education.
Keywords: generative artificial intelligence (genAI); critical AI use; AI literacy;
critical thinking; epistemic orientation; intellectual values; higher education; undergraduate
students

Using generative AI (genAI), particularly chatbots such as ChatGPT or Google Gemini, has become an omnipresent element of university students’ everyday lives. Across
the world, prevalence numbers keep rising and become outdated even before they are
published. Already in 2023, one year after the public release of ChatGPT, about 70% of
500 students at an Italian university reported using chatbots for personal activities, and
about 41% for university-related activities (Farinosi & Melchior, 2025). In a 2023 survey
of 360 Harvard students, about 65% of respondents reported using or planning to use
chatbots for academic work (Deschenes & McMahon, 2024). Furthermore, the UK’s Higher
Education Policy Institute reported in 2025 that 92% of the 1000 surveyed students (66%
in 2024) used some form of AI, with 88% (53% in 2024) using it for assessments (Freeman,
2025). Explaining concepts and summarizing materials were disclosed as the main purposes. Of about 500 students and scholars at three Indian universities, about 90% use AI

for their academic activities (Archana et al., 2025). Finally, the Digital Education Council
(2026) reported 92% prevalence of AI use among over 22,000 Latin American students. Two
years earlier, they reported 86% prevalence among 3800 students from 16 countries (Digital
Education Council, 2024).
Given genAI’s obvious ubiquity among university students, it makes sense to note
that a mindless, uncritical use of genAI tools, such as chatbots, generally carries two
risks: the first concerns reduced cognitive processing and weaker learning outcomes.
These detrimental consequences, referred to as inversion effects, occur when students
outsource the majority of their cognitive effort to genAI (Bauer et al., 2025). Such reduced
performance and decline in knowledge is described as deskilling (Ferdman, 2026). For
instance, in their field experiment, Bastani et al. (2025) found reduced performance in math
education among students who had used generative AI, after access was subsequently
removed. Furthermore, a recent systematic review revealed that students’ excessive reliance
on genAI undermines the development of critical thinking skills (Zhai et al., 2024). The
second risk is that the potential inaccuracy of genAI outputs may go unnoticed. After all,
genAI tools, particularly chatbots, tend to generate results that are misleading at best and
plain inaccurate at worst (Bauer et al., 2025). Such misleading or false outputs cannot be
considered an aberration but a fundamental property of genAI with no technological fix
(Banerjee et al., 2025; Ilievski et al., 2025).
Considering these risks, the importance of critical genAI use cannot therefore be
overstated. Such a critical use of genAI comprises, for instance, comparing AI-generated
answers with other sources or literature. The present paper proposes and empirically tests
two potential protective factors against these risks and thus potential predictors of critical
use of genAI: knowledge about genAI and a disposition to engage in critical thinking. Both
have frequently been named as components of AI literacy (e.g., Allen & Kendeou, 2024;
OECD/European Commission, 2026).
1.1. Knowledge About GenAI
Knowledge about genAI refers to at least a basic understanding of large language
models, the technology behind genAI tools. Such knowledge should include, for instance,
that a genAI system is a statistical text-completion machine rather than “intelligent” in a
human sense (Bender et al., 2021; Ilievski et al., 2025). Seeking appropriate instruments to
assess students’ knowledge about genAI, Lintner’s (2024) systematic review of 22 studies
and 16 AI literacy scales is a sensible starting point. It should be noted that 13 of these
16 reviewed instruments rely on self-assessment items to be answered on Likert-type scales.
Only three of these 16 instruments are performance-based. Obviously, self-assessment has
its limitations, as it can be influenced by bias, overconfidence, and other factors (Praslova,
2018). Therefore, in the present paper, we decided to assess both self-reported and objective
knowledge (i.e., knowledge assessed via a performance test).
Finally, we also aimed to examine potential misconceptions students might have about
genAI. We follow the approach of Eitel et al. (2021) and Hefter et al. (2022) and differentiate
between misconceptions and a lack of knowledge (i.e., missing conceptions) by considering
respondents’ certainty. Briefly put, whereas a misconception manifests in an incorrect
answer given with high confidence, a simple lack of knowledge is rather indicated by low
confidence regardless of correctness.
1.2. Epistemic Orientation and Intellectual Values—The Disposition Toward Critical Thinking
Critical thinking has been receiving unabated enthusiasm and appeal as a key goal of
education for many decades. Kuhn (1999) described critical thinking as “reflecting on what
is known and how that knowledge is justified.” (p. 23). Almost 30 years later, the relevance

of critical thinking has been further increased by the Internet (Tarchi et al., 2025), and it is
still considered one of the most important future skills of the 21st century (BMBF, 2024).
It makes sense to assume that an individual with a disposition towards critical thinking
would also use genAI in a rather critical way, such as comparing AI-generated answers
with other sources or literature.
In the current paper, we therefore focus on the disposition toward critical thinking,
in particular on two prerequisites for critical (and argumentative) thinking proposed by
Kuhn (2001): evaluativist beliefs and intellectual values. Evaluativist beliefs reflect the
highest level of epistemological understanding (Kuhn, 2001). Briefly put, epistemological
understanding basically describes how an individual thinks about the nature of knowledge
and knowing (Hofer & Pintrich, 1997). The evaluativist level of epistemological understanding provides a rational basis for considering critical thinking reasonable. It is the final
and most sophisticated level of epistemological understanding (following the absolutist
and multiplist levels). Because such an evaluativist level of epistemological understanding
develops only over many years (if at all), it seems reasonable not to focus directly on this
level but rather on a tendency to move away from more absolutist beliefs toward more
evaluativist beliefs. This tendency is referred to as the so-called epistemic orientation
(Hefter et al., 2015).
The second prerequisite, intellectual values, refers to viewing intellectual engagement,
such as critical thinking, as worthwhile (Kuhn & Park, 2005). Hefter (2024) identified
intellectual values as a protective factor for distance learning from a how-to video. Whereas
smartphone usage time negatively predicted learning outcomes, intellectual values positively
predicted them. Furthermore, Hefter et al. (2015, 2018) successfully promoted both epistemic
orientation and intellectual values through short-term video-based training interventions.
At first glance, intellectual values might seem similar to the need for cognition (NFC).
However, in contrast to NFC, which refers to an individual’s tendency to “engage in and
enjoy cognitively challenging activities” (Petty et al., 2009, p. 318), intellectual values
represent the “perceived value of intellectual activity to a cultural group” (Kuhn & Park,
2005, p. 115). Furthermore, NFC is rather considered a personality trait, whereas intellectual
values appear malleable via interventions (Hefter et al., 2015, 2018). Nevertheless, in
addition to assessing epistemic orientation and intellectual values as central variables, we
will also assess NFC to address exploratory research questions.
1.3. The Present Study
Despite the growing literature on educational generative AI, a notable knowledge
gap and practice deficiency remain regarding the specific individual factors that prevent
uncritical overreliance. While prior research often examines general AI literacy, the novelty
of this study lies in the simultaneous investigation of both subjective and objective genAI
knowledge (Section 1.1) alongside deeper psychological dispositions, namely epistemic orientation and intellectual values (Section 1.2), as predictors of critical genAI use. Addressing
this gap holds substantial research significance and practical value for higher education, as
it shifts the focus from purely technical tool training toward cultivating potential protective
factors required for responsible genAI engagement.
Therefore, the present study’s main goal is to investigate possible connections between
students’ critical use of genAI, their knowledge about genAI, and their disposition to
engage in critical thinking (i.e., epistemic orientation and intellectual values).
To explore these connections, we chose a quantitative, correlational approach in
an ecologically valid setting. In this study, we focused specifically on undergraduate
university students, as this population represents a primary demographic navigating the
rapid integration of genAI tools into higher education coursework.

Referring to our specific hypotheses in light of the aforementioned background, we
assume that students’ knowledge about genAI would predict their critical use of genAI
(Hypothesis 1). Furthermore, students’ disposition to engage in critical thinking (i.e., a high
degree of epistemic orientation and intellectual values) would predict their critical use of
genAI (Hypothesis 2).
The present study pursues two additional exploratory goals. First, we aim to take
a closer look at students’ knowledge about genAI and identify potential misconceptions.
In this regard, we will analyze the correctness of the students’ answers along with their
reported certainty. Second, we aim to examine potential relationships between students’
actual use of genAI, their perceived usefulness of genAI, their interest in genAI, and their
need for cognition (NFC).

2. Method
2.1. Sample and Procedure
To determine the required sample size, we conducted an a priori power analysis using
GPower (Faul et al., 2007). For a multiple linear regression with two predictors (fixed
model, R2 deviation from zero), assuming a medium effect size (f 2 = 0.15, an alpha level of
α = 0.05, and power of 1 − β = 0.80, the analysis indicated a required sample size of N = 68.
We recruited 70 undergraduate psychology students via our university’s online platform for research participants. The final sample that provided informed consent and
completed the study comprised N = 67 (59 female, 7 male, 1 n/a; Mage = 22.58 years,
SDage = 5.47). Almost all students (95.5%) reported using ChatGPT for their studies,
followed by Google Gemini (14.5%) and Microsoft Copilot (4.5%).
To address the hypotheses outlined in Section 1.3, this study employed a quantitative, non-experimental correlational research design. We selected this design because it is
uniquely suited to examining the predictive relationships between psychological dispositions (i.e., epistemic orientation, intellectual values) and students’ (self-reported) behavioral
outcomes in an ecologically valid educational setting. Establishing this correlational baseline is a necessary first step to identify the potential cognitive and dispositional predictors of
critical genAI use before targeted instructional interventions or experimental manipulations
can be designed and evaluated.
Our study took place completely online, and the participants completed the following questionnaires in that order: demographics (age, sex, and genAI tools used for their
studies), interest in genAI, self-reported knowledge about genAI, knowledge test about
genAI, usage of genAI, critical use of genAI, epistemic orientation, need for cognition,
and intellectual values. We chose this fixed sequence intentionally to minimize potential
priming effects and social desirability bias. Specifically, behavioral measures (usage and
critical use) were assessed prior to eliciting reflections on deeper psychological dispositions and normative constructs (epistemic orientation, need for cognition, and intellectual
values). This order ensured that participants were not unconsciously primed to alter their
reported critical genAI behaviors to align with their subsequent reflections on intellectual
values or cognitive dispositions. Furthermore, self-reported knowledge assessments were
administered before the performance knowledge tests to reduce self-report biases driven
by test performance feedback.
2.2. Measures and Instruments
2.2.1. Interest in GenAI
To assess students’ interest in genAI, we used four items based on Schiefele’s (1991)
studies on topic interest and its emotional and value components. The stem “I consider the
topic ‘learning with genAI’ as. . .” was followed by the attributes “boring” and “interesting”

for the emotional component and “unimportant” and “useful” for the value component.
Students had to answer all four items on a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (fully disagree)
to 4 (fully agree). After reverse-scoring the negatively keyed items, we used the mean of all
four items as a measure of interest in genAI, with higher means indicating greater topic
interest. The internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.76).
2.2.2. Self-Reported Knowledge About GenAI
We selected 18 items from Laupichler et al.’s (2023) 31-item “Scale for the assessment
of non-experts’ AI literacy (SNAIL)” to assess self-reported knowledge about genAI. Please
see Appendix A for the full 18-item list. Each item was an “I can. . .”-statement about a
genAI-related skill and was answered on a Likert-type scale from 1 (fully disagree) to
6 (fully agree). We selected the 18 items from the 31 original items based on content relevance and ecological validity for the higher education context. We focused on knowledge
we assume is relevant for students who use genAI for university or leisure, such as “I
can tell if the technologies I use are supported by artificial intelligence.” We deliberately
did not select items that we considered too far away from a typical university learning
scenario, such as “I can explain why data security must be considered when developing
and using artificial intelligence applications.” We also decided not to select items that focus
too much on the technical background of AI, such as “I can explain how ‘reinforcement
learning’ works on a basic level (in the context of machine learning).” We used the mean
of all 18 items (Please see Appendix A) as a measure of self-reported knowledge about
genAI, with higher values indicating greater knowledge. The internal consistency was high
(Cronbach’s α = 0.90).
2.2.3. Objective Knowledge About GenAI
For our questionnaire on objective knowledge about genAI, we used 12 single-choice
items. The first six items were adapted from Köhler and Hartig’s (2024) ChatGPT knowledge scale and featured two alternative answers: “correct” or “incorrect.” An example item
is “GenAI provides the same answer to the same question. (incorrect)”
The other six items featured four alternative answers, with one being correct. We
selected these six items from Hornberger et al.’s (2023) AI literacy test, which comprised
30 items. Our decision on the six items was based on face validity related to knowledge
we assume is relevant to students who use genAI for university and leisure purposes. For
instance, we selected the item “Imagine you are chatting with an assistant on the Internet.
How could you proceed to find out whether you are interacting with a human or with an
AI?” We decided not to include items we considered irrelevant to students’ daily life or too
technical, such as “What is a key criterion for the quality of a model in machine learning?”
We simply coded correct answers as +1, incorrect answers as 0, and calculated the mean
across all 12 items, with higher values indicating greater objective knowledge about genAI.
Please see Appendix B for the full 12-item list.
2.2.4. Misconception Score
Each of the 12 items we administered to assess objective knowledge about genAI was
accompanied by a subjective certainty rating. Students had to answer “How certain are
you?” on a Likert-type scale from 0 (very uncertain) to 4 (very certain). This approach
enabled us to calculate a so-called misconception score according to the procedures of Eitel
et al. (2021) and Hefter et al. (2022). First, we coded correct answers with +1 and incorrect
answers with −1. Then, we multiplied each correctness value (+1 or −1) by the subjective
certainty rating for each item, resulting in a possible range from −4 to +4. Higher positive
values represent greater confidence in a correct answer, indicating “stronger certainty about
correct knowledge.” Higher negative values, however, represent greater confidence in an

incorrect answer, indicating stronger endorsement of a misconception. Finally, values near
zero indicate low confidence regardless of correctness, suggesting missing conceptions
rather than misconceptions or correct knowledge. The mean of all twelve multiplications
yielded the misconception score, with higher values indicating fewer misconceptions.
2.2.5. Actual Usage of GenAI
To assess the student’s genAI usage, we adapted the ten items by Köhler and Hartig
(2024) concerning typical AI usage scenarios, such as “I have used genAI to get feedback
on the texts I created.” Furthermore, we developed nine additional items for even more
scenarios we considered typical for students, such as “I have used gen AI to generate study
materials or summaries to prepare for exams.” Appendix C provides the complete list of all
19 items. We used Köhler and Hartig’s (2024) four-option response format:

This response format enabled us to address two dimensions, namely actual usage and
perceived usefulness. To assess students’ actual use of genAI, we followed Köhler and
Hartig’s (2024) procedure and coded “yes” as 1 and “no” as 0. We used the mean score
across all 19 items as a measure of actual usage, with higher values indicating a greater
extent of actual usage. The internal consistency was good (Cronbach’s α = 0.83).
2.2.6. Use Value of GenAI
We relied on the aforementioned 19 items to measure students’ actual use of genAI.
Again, we followed Köhler and Hartig’s (2024) procedure, but this time we coded “can
image it” and “was helpful” as 1, and “don’t intend to” and “was not helpful” as 0. We used
the mean score across all 19 items as a measure of use value, with higher values indicating
greater perceived usefulness. The internal consistency was good (Cronbach’s α = 0.75).
2.2.7. Critical Use of GenAI
To assess students’ critical use of genAI, we developed 10 theory-guided items mapping critical validation behaviors (such as cross-checking outputs and verifying factual
accuracy), with students rating their agreement on a 5-point Likert-type scale from 1 (fully
disagree) to 5 (fully agree). One example item is “I verify whether AI-generated calculations
or statistical results are correct.” After reverse-scoring the negatively keyed item, we used
the mean score across all 10 items as a measure of critical use of genAI, with higher values
indicating a more critical use. The questionnaire showed an acceptable internal consistency
(Cronbach’s α = 0.78). Please see Appendix D for the complete list of all 10 items.
2.2.8. Epistemic Orientation
We used Hefter et al.’s (2015) short scale to assess epistemic orientation. It comprised
five statements, with which an individual who has moved away from the level of absolutist
epistemological understanding would either agree (positively keyed items, e.g., “Even
uncertain knowledge, such as is the case when scientific positions contradict each other, can
be evaluated”) or disagree (negatively keyed items, e.g., “Scientific statements are either
clearly true or false”). We let students mark their agreement on a 5-point Likert-type scale
from 1 (fully disagree) to 5 (fully agree). After reverse-scoring the negatively keyed items,
we used the mean of all five items as a measure of epistemic orientation, with higher values
indicating a greater tendency toward non-absolutist beliefs. The internal consistency was
acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.70).

2.2.9. Need for Cognition (NFC)
We relied on the 4-item NFC short scale by Beißert et al. (2014). It comprised
four statements, such as “I prefer my life to be filled with puzzles that I solve.” Students
had to mark their agreement to these statements on a 7-point Likert-type scale from 1 (fully
disagree) to 7 (fully agree). After reverse-scoring the negatively keyed items, we used the
mean of all four items as a measure of NFC, with higher values indicating a greater extent
of NFC. The internal consistency was low (Cronbach’s α = 0.54), which is in line with the
developers’ validation studies’ values of α = 0.51 to 0.54 (Beißert et al., 2014). They argue
that their 4-item short-scale was never expected to reach high internal consistency because
its four items address different facets of NFC. Consequently, a lower internal consistency is
an acceptable trade-off for such an ultra-short scale. It prioritizes broad construct coverage
and reduced participant burden over item homogeneity, provided that the instrument
maintains its conceptual validity.
2.2.10. Intellectual Values
We assessed students’ intellectual values with Hefter et al.’s (2015) 14-item scale.
Each item described a fictive person who considers an aspect of intellectual engagement
worthwhile, such as “Person B enjoys discussing complex scientific subjects critically
with other people.” or “Person K thinks it is worthwhile, to connect knowledge from one
domain with knowledge from another.” Participants had to rate their similarity (“How
similar is this person to you?”) to these 14 fictive persons subjectively on a Likert-type scale
from 1 (not like me at all) to 6 (very much like me). We used the mean of all 14 items as a
measure of intellectual values, with higher means referring to higher intellectual values.
The internal consistency was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.90).

3.1. Knowledge About GenAI Predicts Critical GenAI Use
In our first hypothesis, we assumed that students’ knowledge about genAI would
predict their critical genAI use. Correlation analyses revealed a significant positive correlation between subjective knowledge about genAI and critical use of genAI, r = 0.55,
p < 0.001. Furthermore, we found a significant positive correlation between objective
knowledge about genAI and critical use of genAI, r = 0.31, p = 0.011. Figure 1 illustrates
both correlations in scatterplots.

3.2. Epistemic Orientation and Intellectual Values Predict Critical GenAI Use
In our second hypothesis, we assumed that the students’ disposition to engage in
critical thinking (i.e., a high degree of epistemic orientation and intellectual values) would
predict their critical use of genAI. We therefore conducted a multiple linear regression
analysis to examine whether and to what extent students’ epistemic orientation and intellectual values predict their critical use of genAI. The variance inflation factors (VIFs)
were low (VIF = 1.05) for both predictors, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern. Furthermore, the data met the assumption of independent errors (Durbin-Watson
value = 2.23). Additionally, inspection of residual plots indicated that assumptions of
normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity were met. The overall result of the regression
was statistically significant, F(2, 64) = 17.19, p < 0.001, R2 = 0.33.
More specifically, epistemic orientation was a significant positive predictor of critical
use of genAI, β = 0.36, p < 0.001, as were intellectual values, β = 0.40, p < 0.001. The
overall model fit (R2 = 0.33) describes how 33% of the variance in students’ critical genAI
use is predicted by their epistemic orientation and intellectual values. Both standardized
coefficients reflect substantial medium predictive contributions. Figure 2 illustrates these
results in a 3D scatter plot with a regression plane.

3.4. Exploratory Correlation Analyses
To address our second exploratory goal outlined in Section 1.3, we examined the
relationships between students’ actual genAI use, perceived usefulness, interest, and their
need for cognition (NFC). In our more exploratory correlation analyses, we found that
students’ actual use of genAI correlated significantly only with their interest in genAI,
r = 0.54, p < 0.001, and their perceived usefulness of genAI, r = 0.58, p < 0.001. However, we
could not detect correlations with other factors such as students’ knowledge about genAI,
their disposition to engage in critical thinking (i.e., epistemic orientation and intellectual
values), or their need for cognition.
Moreover, regarding the specific role of cognitive dispositions, students’ perceived
usefulness correlated negatively with their need for cognition, r = −0.27, p = 0.027, suggesting that individuals with a higher need for cognition perceive the genAI tools’ utility more
skeptically. Finally, the students’ need for cognition correlated positively with their critical
use of genAI, r = 0.25, p = 0.044, empirically suggesting that a stable disposition toward
cognitive effort is directly linked to more critical genAI use.

4. Discussion
4.1. Knowledge About genAI and the Disposition Toward Critical Thinking
In the present study, our main goal was to examine possible connections between
students’ critical use of genAI and two distinct predictors thereof: knowledge about genAI
and a disposition toward critical thinking (i.e., a high degree of epistemic orientation
and intellectual values). We found empirical evidence that critical use of genAI is indeed
positively associated with these two predictors. First, knowledge about genAI (both
subjective and objective knowledge) predicted students’ critical use of genAI (Hypothesis 1).
It is noteworthy that we relied on instruments to assess both subjective knowledge (via
self-report scale) and objective knowledge (via performance–based test). This approach
enabled us to cross-validate both instruments, as recommended by Lintner (2024).
Descriptively, students’ critical genAI use exhibited a stronger correlation with subjective knowledge (r = 0.55) compared to objective knowledge (r = 0.31). However, this
descriptive, not statistically significant difference should not be over-interpreted on a theoretical level. It likely reflects methodological rather than substantial cognitive differences.
Specifically, both the subjective knowledge scale and the critical use scale rely on selfreported, Likert-type questionnaires. This structural and partly content-related similarity
(see Appendices A and B) may introduce a common-method bias, artificially elevating their
association. In contrast, the objective knowledge assessment utilized a performance-based
test format, which naturally minimizes shared method variance.
Second, both facets of the disposition to engage in critical thinking (i.e., epistemic
orientation and intellectual values) accounted for about one-third of the variance in students’
critical use of genAI (Hypothesis 2). This result underscores the importance of intellectual

values as a protective factor against cognitive miserliness (Barr et al., 2015). Cognitive
miserliness refers to the unwillingness to incur cognitive costs and the preference to
outsource information and cognitive processes to technology, such as genAI tools, but
also smartphones. For instance, Hefter (2024) found that intellectual values were beneficial
to distance learning outcomes, whereas smartphone usage time was detrimental.
Overall, the present findings allow us to conclude that both knowledge about genAI
and the disposition to engage in critical thinking (i.e., a high degree of epistemic orientation
and intellectual values) can serve as protective factors against uncritical overreliance on
genAI. From an educational and practical perspective, these findings can lay the groundwork for promising instructional interventions. After all, both protective factors against
uncritical overreliance on genAI (i.e., knowledge about genAI and the disposition to engage
in critical thinking) can be effectively trained and fostered. Obviously, knowledge about
genAI can be acquired through various (online) courses, both within and outside academia,
such as those available on the KI-Campus online platform (Stifterverband, 2026). Furthermore, even the disposition to engage in critical thinking can be fostered. For instance,
Hefter et al. (2015) successfully promoted epistemic orientation and Hefter et al. (2015,
2018) fostered intellectual values through short-term video-based training interventions.
4.2. Potential Misconceptions About GenAI
Through an exploratory lens, we were interested in potential misconceptions about
gen AI. Calculating misconceptions scores by combining students’ answer correctness
and confidence enabled us to identify potential misconceptions. As mentioned above,
misconceptions manifest in incorrect answers given with high confidence. Our findings
showed that only 9% of participants had misconceptions. Overall, only three of the 12 items
had misconception scores below zero, indicating the presence of actual misconceptions.
For instance, 96% of the participants believed with confidence that “AI chatbots perform
web searches,” a typical misconception. However, this item, adapted from Köhler and
Hartig’s (2024) ChatGPT knowledge scale, might be misleading because it can be considered
surpassed by the technical capabilities of current chatbots. This aspect will be discussed
further in the Limitations section.
Most items had positive scores, indicating that participants did not give incorrect
answers with high confidence, suggesting they did not have misconceptions. For instance,
about 90% of the participants correctly answered that “GenAI may provide content that
is not based on facts.” This item’s misconceptions score was 2.91, the highest of all items,
indicating correct answers with rather high confidence.
Overall, with our specific sample and questionnaire, we could not detect a high
prevalence of misconceptions about genAI. Rather, only certain aspects of genAI, such as
the aforementioned web search, were associated with misconceptions. From an educational
and practical perspective, these findings give cause for cautious optimism. A general lack
of knowledge about genAI could be tackled with various (online) courses. For the specific
misconceptions, interventions based on extended refutation texts are recommended for an
effective and convenient remedy (e.g., Hefter, 2026).
4.3. Exploratory Correlation Analyses and Role of NFC
Finally, the results of our additional exploratory analyses also appear noteworthy.
In particular, the negative correlation between students’ perceived usefulness of genAI
and their NFC is understandable as NFC reflects a positive attitude toward engaging in
cognitive tasks (Cacioppo et al., 1984). Delegating cognitive tasks to genAI tools would
contradict this need. Furthermore, the positive correlation between students’ NFC and

their critical use of genAI further validates our newly developed questionnaire for critical
use of genAI, as uncritical use of genAI outputs would also run counter to NFC.
Synthesizing these findings, NFC should not be viewed as an isolated exploratory
variable, but rather as a foundational individual disposition that contextualizes our main
hypotheses. While genAI knowledge and critical thinking dispositions provide the cognitive frameworks necessary for critical genAI use (Hypotheses 1 and 2), NFC points to the
intrinsic motivational drive required to execute these cognitively demanding validation
behaviors. Individuals with higher NFC are inherently more willing to invest the mental
effort needed to cross-check outputs, thereby bridging the gap between theoretical genAI
literacy and actual critical behavior. Integrating NFC into the broader argument of the
paper suggests that considering learners’ NFC might be advisable when developing the
instructional interventions to enhance critical use of genAI. For instance, during an intervention to foster the disposition to engage in critical thinking, learners with low NFC might
need extra incentives to engage mentally, such as feedback or interactivity.
4.4. Limitations and Outlook for Future Research
For a start, our study’s relatively small sample consisted of undergraduate psychology
students. Hence, our sample is not representative of students in other subjects, let alone of
other populations, limiting our study’s generalizability. Furthermore, voluntary participation in the study might have introduced selection bias, as our participants may have had a
greater interest in or knowledge of genAI than average students. Additionally, the sample
exhibits a substantial gender imbalance (88% female), which reflects typical demographics
in undergraduate psychology programs. These sample characteristics may further constrain generalizability in specific ways. For instance, psychology students receive rigorous
training in evaluating scientific sources, potentially resulting in higher baseline levels of
epistemic orientation and intellectual values than in other disciplines. In contrast, students
in STEM fields might possess higher objective technical genAI knowledge but different
critical thinking dispositions. While the foundational mechanisms (i.e., knowledge and
critical thinking dispositions predict critical genAI use) are expected to operate universally,
the relative weight and baseline levels of these predictors likely vary across disciplines,
educational levels, and demographic backgrounds. Finally, while our sample size was
explicitly powered to detect medium-large predictive relationships, it limits the statistical
power to identify very small or nuanced exploratory correlation effects, which warrants
replication in larger samples.
Moreover, our new instrument of critical genAI use relies solely on the learners’
self-reports. Hence, it shares the typical disadvantages of subjective scales, such as a
possible bias (Praslova, 2018). Future research could thus also develop and implement
performance-based assessments of critical genAI use. More specifically, researchers could
adopt standardized performance-based measures such as Jin et al.’s (2025) Generative
AI Literacy Assessment Test (GLAT), which was designed to objectively assess higher
education students’ genAI literacy. Implementing such performance-based instruments
as well as objective tracking of real-time student behavior (e.g., via logs) would reduce
self-report biases and provide a more objective assessment of students’ actual critical
evaluation skills.
Beyond behavioral testing, future research could also implement multi-method validation approaches to strengthen confidence in the observed predictive paths. To mitigate
common-method variance, studies could integrate educator or peer evaluations of students’ genAI engagement. Furthermore, establishing criterion validity through objective
behavioral artifacts—such as screen-capturing or browser-tracking—would cross-validate
subjective metrics against real-world actions. Finally, transitioning from correlational to

experimental or longitudinal research designs is required to establish causality and demonstrate whether fostering genAI knowledge, epistemic orientation, and intellectual values
directly enhances critical genAI behavior.
Another outlook for future research concerns the knowledge and potential misconceptions about genAI and its impact on critical genAI use. We suppose that it is not
only knowledge about genAI but also knowledge about human learning that plays a major role when it comes to critical genAI use. For instance, an individual who compares
genAI responses with other sources or literature might do so due to knowledge that genAI
outputs could be incorrect. However, it seems plausible that this individual also does
it because of knowing that outsourcing cognitive processes harms one’s own learning.
Future research might thus explore the role of knowledge about human learning as a
sort of meta-knowledge for informed decisions of critical genAI use. This metacognitive
understanding of human cognition and learning processes could serve as an additional,
powerful predictor of critical genAI use. According to theories of self-regulated learning
(SRL) and metacognition, learners with metacognitive knowledge about how memory,
cognitive effort, and deep processing operate are better equipped to monitor their learning
and adapt their tactics and strategies accordingly (e.g., Winne & Hadwin, 1998). They
potentially understand that premature cognitive offloading to genAI curtails their own
competence development (cf. Ngai & Gilbert, 2026; Xu et al., 2025). Future research should
therefore explicitly assess knowledge about human learning alongside knowledge about
genAI to map the nomological network of critical genAI use fully.
Another key focus for future research concerns the development of interventions on
critical genAI use. Recent articles (Abedin et al., 2026; Lund et al., 2025) underscore the
importance of teaching students about AI literacy, AI ethics, and the risk of AI overreliance.
Based on our findings, interventions to foster critical use of genAI could focus on addressing
its predictors: knowledge about genAI and a disposition toward critical thinking. As critical
thinking remains highly relevant (Tarchi et al., 2025), digital training interventions that
foster the disposition toward it (e.g., Hefter et al., 2015) provide a valuable framework to
build upon.
Moreover, future research might also focus on directly promoting the critical use of
genAI without taking the detour through its predictors. A promising starting point for
such direct interventions is modeling examples (e.g., Hefter et al., 2022). Such modeling
examples could demonstrate concrete strategies of critical genAI use, such as comparing
AI-generated answers with other sources or with the literature.
Finally, genAI tools are evolving constantly. As a consequence, an item such as “AI
chatbots perform web searches”, which we adapted from Köhler and Hartig (2024) and
used for assessing objective knowledge and potential misconceptions about genAI, can
already be considered surpassed by the technical capabilities of current chatbots. Strictly
speaking, the large language model (LLM) underlying a chatbot such as ChatGPT cannot
perform a web search. However, the students’ subjective experience is, of course, that it
does, because ChatGPT triggers a web search and uses the results in the final generated
output. Furthermore, an item such as “What makes AI intelligent?” by Hornberger et al.
(2023) may be more applicable to symbolic AI systems and reinforcement learning agents,
less so to modern chatbots (which most students associate with AI). Such an item might
even be misleading if students equate LLMs and AI. Students will probably not consider
the goal of an LLM (i.e., maintaining a high likelihood of output token sequences) as a
rational act.
Importantly, the ongoing technical shift raises broader questions about the instrument’s validity for assessing genAI knowledge. Because the technological landscape
changes rapidly, static items can shift from measuring technical understanding to measur-

ing mere awareness of user interface features, such as the aforementioned web search item.
While this does not compromise the internal validity of our current findings—as it accurately captured the students’ conceptual understanding at the time of data collection—it
underscores a critical limitation and outlook for future research. To maintain psychometric
rigor, future measurement frameworks should shift toward dynamic, feature-independent
items that evaluate foundational architectural principles of AI rather than evolving and
changing software functionalities. Overall, future research should focus on these updated
items for the genAI knowledge test that better reflect current genAI tools’ evolving technology and performance.

5. Conclusions
This study demonstrated the predictive power of both knowledge about genAI and
the disposition to engage in critical thinking (i.e., epistemic orientation and intellectual
values) on students’ critical genAI use. Furthermore, our exploratory analyses highlighted
widespread conceptual misconceptions and identified the need for cognition (NFC) as
another key variable. Although the small, subject-specific sample limits immediate generalizability and long-term instrument validity, this study contributes foundational theoretical
knowledge to the literature by establishing a multifaceted framework of individual protective factors against uncritical AI overreliance. Practically, our findings imply that higher
education institutions should move beyond basic AI training. Consequently, we recommend that future research focus on developing and experimentally evaluating instructional
interventions that target conceptual misconceptions, epistemic orientation, and intellectual
values to foster responsible AI engagement in higher education.
