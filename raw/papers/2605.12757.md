# A Framework for Institutional Change in the Age of AI

**Authors:** David Perl-Nussbaum, Noah D. Finkelstein (University of Colorado Boulder)
**Submitted:** 12 May 2026
**Subjects:** Physics Education (physics.ed-ph); Computers and Society (cs.CY)
**License:** CC BY 4.0

## Abstract

Generative AI is rapidly reshaping STEM higher education. Not only are our educational practices changing, but how we think about educational transformation must adapt. Existing models of institutional change in STEM, aimed at interactive engagement, have largely followed an adoption logic: relatively stable, well-researched educational practices are evaluated and then scaled. These assumptions do not hold for generative AI, which is an arrival technology — entering classrooms before a sufficient pedagogical evidence base could form. Building on recent decades of work on STEM institutional change, we propose a framework identifying six dimensions along which prior change models must be reconsidered in light of AI: three concerning the tools at the center of reform (the tool's evidence base, rate of change, and scope), and three concerning the people involved in change (faculty, change agents, and students). For each dimension, we examine how AI-era assumptions differ from those underlying prior interactive engagement reforms and derive design implications, including: privileging humble and local inquiries; organizing reform around pedagogical approaches rather than specific tools; repositioning change agents as facilitators of collective inquiry; and engaging students as partners in reform. Collectively, the six dimensions and design implications constitute a new framework for adapting change models to support institutions under conditions of genuine uncertainty. Finally, we illustrate how the framework may be applied through a brief case-study of a faculty workshop series carried out in a university physics department to support instructors adapting to this modern AI era.

## Introduction

Interactive engagement (IE) reforms (Peer Instruction, Tutorials, LA programs, PhET simulations) were based on adoption technologies: innovations were researched, evaluated, and then scaled. AI follows an arrival technology pattern — it reaches students and faculty before institutions can respond or evidence can be established. Unlike earlier tools (calculators, phones) that arrived with longer adaptation intervals, generative AI evolves on timescales of months, not years, making sustained adaptation extremely difficult.

Central dilemma: Institutions cannot wait for best practices, but cannot responsibly scale unjustified innovations. Neither banning nor uncritical embrace is tenable.

## Background: Traditional Institutional Change Models

- Diffusion of Innovations (Rogers, 2003): Assumes a stable, well-defined innovation can be evaluated and adopted through stages across adopter categories. Relies on uncertainty reduction via evidence.
- Organizational frames (Bolman & Deal, 2017; Kezar, 2018): Multi-frame perspectives (structural, human resources, political, symbolic) — but most still assume a known destination (IE).
- Henderson et al. (2011) framework: Categorizes change strategies by individual/system and prescribed/emergent. STEM reforms primarily worked on the prescriptive side.
- Science Education Initiative (SEI): Funded discipline-based Science Teaching Fellows partnering with faculty on course transformation.
- Departmental Action Team (DAT) and DeLTA: More emergent, collective inquiry approaches.
- Core assumption shared by most prior models: The direction of change is justified in advance; innovations are evidence-based.

## The Framework: Six Dimensions for AI-Era Change

### Dimension 1: Evidence Base
IE assumption: Reform begins with evidence-based tools; adoption follows validated research.
AI reality: Tools arrived without pedagogical evidence. Students already use AI; educators must act before evidence exists.
Design implications: Privilege humble, local inquiries; document what faculty are trying; use AI literacy frameworks as tentative guides; provide resources for experimentation cycles; draw on IE and learning theory to focus inquiry.

### Dimension 2: Rate of Change
IE assumption: Tools are stable; materials can be curated and transferred.
AI reality: AI capabilities accelerate, with models changing significantly within months.
Design implications: Organize reform around pedagogical approaches rather than specific AI tools; build institutional capacity for continuous adaptation; develop modular, updatable resources.

### Dimension 3: Scope
IE assumption: Reforms target specific, bounded interventions.
AI reality: AI's impact is broad, affecting many aspects of teaching and learning simultaneously (assessment, homework, writing, problem-solving).
Design implications: Take a systemic view; coordinate across courses and departments; recognize that piecemeal responses are insufficient.

### Dimension 4: Faculty
IE assumption: Faculty adopt externally developed, proven practices.
AI reality: Faculty must engage in ongoing, local experimentation with uncertain tools.
Design implications: Support faculty as inquiry-driven experimenters; provide safe spaces for trial and error; value local knowledge alongside research evidence.

### Dimension 5: Change Agents
IE assumption: Change agents are disseminators of best practices.
AI reality: Change agents become facilitators of collective inquiry when best practices don't yet exist.
Design implications: Reposition change agents as community organizers and inquiry facilitators; build faculty learning communities around shared questions.

### Dimension 6: Students
IE assumption: Students are recipients of reformed instruction.
AI reality: Students are often ahead of faculty in AI use; they have unique perspectives on what works.
Design implications: Engage students as partners in reform; leverage their AI experiences; co-create responses to AI challenges.

## Design Implications (Synthesized)

1. Privilege humble and local inquiries over top-down, generalized solutions.
2. Organize reform around pedagogical approaches rather than specific, ephemeral AI tools.
3. Reposition change agents as facilitators of collective inquiry, not just trainers.
4. Engage students as partners in the reform process.
5. Build institutional capacity for continuous adaptation under uncertainty.

## Case Study: Faculty Workshop Series

A faculty workshop series was conducted in a university physics department to support instructors adapting to the AI era. The workshops demonstrated the framework in practice — facilitating collective inquiry rather than prescribing solutions, organizing around pedagogical goals rather than specific tools, and engaging participants as co-investigators of effective AI integration.
