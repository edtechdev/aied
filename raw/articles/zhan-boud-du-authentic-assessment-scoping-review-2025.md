Authentic assessment (AA) has been a valued and established practice in higher education for over three decades (Gravett, 2025). It was initially proposed at the beginning of the 1990s as a counterbalance to standardised tests in US secondary schools, which could directly "examine student performance on worthy intellectual tasks" (Wiggins, 1990, p.1). In higher education, AA began to be taken up in the late 1990s with a focus on the relevance of academic outcomes to the workplace (Seagraves et al., 1996). Joughin (1998) argued that authenticity in assessment should reflect the extent to which assessment replicates the context of professional practice or real life.

Recently, the prevailing view of AA as mere replication of workplace tasks has been critically challenged (Ajjawi et al., 2024; McArthur, 2023). Ajjawi et al. (2020) argue that AA should encompass various forms of authenticity—contextual, task, and personal—that reflect different contexts and purposes. McArthur (2023) contends that AA must enable students to "influence the future and transform society" rather than merely replicate existing tasks. Without deeper understanding, AA may devolve into "marketing jargon" (Vallis, 2025).

This paper is a scoping review (Arksey & O'Malley, 2005) of empirical AA studies from 2000–2024, using Web of Science, ERIC, and Education Source Ultimate. Search terms included "authentic assessment", "performance assessment", "authentic tasks", etc. Inter-rater reliability = 0.81. Final sample: 37 empirical studies after excluding non-empirical (35), weak AA focus (26), work-based settings (29), and insufficient design detail (46).

Sample: Peak publication 2019 (n=7); Western dominated (n=27 vs Eastern n=10); pre-service teacher education most common (n=12); >50% vocational (n=25); 49% mixed methods, 43% qualitative, 8% quantitative.

## Proposed Six-Dimensional Framework

Existing frameworks (Gulikers et al., 2004; Ashford-Rowe et al., 2014; Villarroel et al., 2018) emphasise realism, cognitive challenge, criteria, and feedback but fail to anticipate future societal/AI changes, centre student experience, or address social relations.

This review proposes a six-dimensional framework:

1. **Authenticity in assessment** — multiple meanings: assessment, professional, digital, self, and social authenticity
2. **Cognitive challenges**
3. **Assessment criteria**
4. **Feedback**
5. **Student agency** — choices/autonomy and self-reflection
6. **Social collaboration** — peer, teacher-student, external partnerships

## Findings by Dimension

### Authenticity in Assessment
- Professional authenticity (workplace scenarios): n=22
- Assessment authenticity (portfolios, projects, concept maps): n=12
- Personal authenticity (student experience, identity, well-being): n=9
- Digital authenticity (Twitter, podcasts, YouTube, LMS): n=6
- Social authenticity (citizenship, sustainability, ethics): n=3

Authenticity is often expressed as multiple intertwined meanings, though broader perspectives remain rare.

### Cognitive Challenges
- Knowledge construction: n=29
- Professional skills: n=22
- 21st-century skills: n=29 (critical thinking n=17, communication n=13, problem-solving n=7)
- Digital literacy: only n=5

### Assessment Criteria
- 22 studies stated rubric use
- Only 3 studies (Chang 2001, Kaya 2008, Kearney & Perkins 2014) co-designed rubrics with students
- Only 7 studies involved students as assessors via self/peer assessment
- Students mostly passive users of rubrics

### Feedback
- Formative feedback: n=23 (portfolios, drafts, discussions, workshops, online, peer, self)
- Summative feedback: n=12 (marking explanations, oral vivas)
- Sustainable feedback: n=4 (self/peer assessment for lifelong evaluative judgement)

### Student Agency
- Choice/autonomy in what/how/when/where: n=8
- Self-reflection: common but often assigned/graded, potentially performative

### Social Collaboration
- Peer collaboration: n=11
- Teacher-student partnership: n=10
- External partners (industry, community): n=6

## AI and Future Challenges

"Rapid and emergent technological developments, such as generative AI, have brought unpredictable futures for students" (Bearman et al., 2023). AA must adapt by:
- Aligning with human capabilities for the digital age (critical digital literacies)
- Developing adaptable strategies incorporating emerging technologies
- Acknowledging that technology shapes future employment and digital identities

## Proposed Design Framework (4 Steps)

1. **Deciding assessment goals and focus** — educators and students collaboratively negotiate purpose and authenticity
2. **Creating assessment context and task** — mirror real-world scenarios, cognitively challenging
3. **Designing assessment criteria and student engagement** — co-design criteria, embed dialogue, peer/self assessment
4. **Planning feedback and self-reflection** — future-oriented feedback, transformative reflection

## Key Discussion Points

- Most studies restrict AA to workplace replication, overlooking social authenticity
- Students' voices rarely included in AA design
- Self-reflection can become performative when graded
- Student choice and self-reflection mutually influence each other; imbalance exists
- Sustainable feedback (transferable to future contexts) underutilized vs formative feedback
