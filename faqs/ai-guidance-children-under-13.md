# How Should Parents and Teachers Approach AI with Children Under 13?

Before age thirteen the question is not whether children will meet AI but which version of it they meet, in whose company, and with what support. What distinguishes this age band is developmental rather than technical: children this young form their model of what a machine *is* from what it does in front of them, they accept fluent output at face value more readily than adolescents do, and much of their AI exposure happens through play, toys, companion apps and family routines rather than through a school platform. [[ai-toys-child-development-2026|Xu, Girouard and Shi (2026)]] describe the central mechanism as a **redistribution of agency in play**: control over narrative, dialogue, relationship and information shifts partly from the child to the child–toy interaction, which constrains play when the toy organizes it and enables play when the toy responds to the child's own imagination and inquiry. [[creative-project-approach-ai-early-childhood-2025|Yang, Li and Lee (2025)]] add the caution that generative social robots can produce content that preoperational children accept as true, that these systems are not developmentally calibrated in the feedback they give, and that their cost and availability widen existing [[digital-divide|divides]].

For teachers and parents alike this means the same four moves come first: keep adult co-use in the loop, prefer unplugged and play-based exploration before generative tools, teach how the system works before teaching how to prompt it, and treat the child's own meaning-making as the thing to protect. This page collects the guidance the research supports, distinguishes it from what it does not yet support, sets out what the evidence does and does not say about banning AI for this age group (see [Should AI be banned for children under thirteen?](#should-ai-be-banned-for-children-under-thirteen)), and links to [[early-childhood-elementary-ai-education|AI in early childhood and elementary education]] and [[parents-and-families|Parents and Families]] for the underlying evidence.

## What is actually different under thirteen

**Developmentally tiered design exists, and younger learners respond to it differently.** [[age-tiered-ai-literacy-guidebooks-2026|Wang, Chuang and Wu (2026)]] evaluated an age-tiered AI literacy resource for Taiwan's K-12 system in two editions: an **Elementary edition for ages 9 to 12** built on scaffolded, platform-based, **non-generative** activities, and an **Advanced edition for ages 13 to 18** with authentic applications, ethical reasoning and supervised [[generative-ai|generative AI]] use. In a single post-exposure survey of 831 participants, the Elementary cohort rated the materials significantly higher on performance expectancy, effort expectancy, playfulness and behavioral intention, and perceived playfulness was the strongest correlate of intention in both groups. The authors are explicit that this measures short-term acceptance after roughly thirty minutes of exposure, not [[learning-gains|learning]], ethical competence or actual use. The design logic is still the actionable part: generative use is treated as the advanced tier, not the entry point.

**Play is the mechanism, not a reward for finishing the lesson.** [[play-ai-pre-k-kindergarten-ai-literacy-2026|Play With AI (PL-AI)]] is a play-centered, design-based curriculum for pre-K and kindergarten whose four principles are [[embodied-learning|embodied play]] as conceptual grounding, tangible coding as a procedural bridge, guided dialogue as ethical and reflective [[scaffolding]], and teacher co-design as the [[sustainability]] anchor. Teachers' self-rated comfort teaching AI topics rose across design cycles, and children showed formative, multidimensional learning: reasoning about AI as human-designed and instruction-following, developing sequencing and debugging through tangible coding, and persisting and collaborating, with ethical reflection on fairness and human decision-making surfacing naturally through guided dialogue. Activities were embedded in existing classroom routines rather than run as a stand-alone unit. [[ai-play-framework-early-childhood-2026|AI-Play]] takes the complementary unplugged route, teaching AI concepts through family-centered, screen-free activities that parents can replicate at home.

**Under-13 AI literacy is about how machines work, not how to get more out of them.** The elementary tier of the guidebook work is deliberately non-generative for this reason, and the kindergarten [[educational-robotics|robotics]] evidence points the same way: [[tsingidou-ct-robotics-kindergarten-2026|Tsingidou et al. (2026)]] find problem-based learning, storytelling and scaffolding are the most-used strategies in robot-mediated computational thinking, with sequencing, debugging and algorithmic design the most-assessed skills (most often via TechCheck-K, though many tools are developed ad hoc without validation). The recognized risks of skipping this stage are the ones that show up later as [[cognitive-offloading|over-reliance]] and shortcut-seeking.

## In the classroom

**Keep the human in the parts that matter most.** [[human-ai-complementarity-social-emotional-learning-2026|Raave and colleagues (2026)]] compared a generative AI conversational agent with human educators facilitating story-based social-emotional learning activities with a static AI child — 18 simulations per facilitator, 108 observations, rated blind to facilitator type. The agent was strong on basic relational and instructional domains (respectful tone, routine procedural scaffolding); the human educators were stronger at deeper SEL instruction, guiding reflection and promoting social-emotional knowledge. The conclusion is complementarity rather than substitution, which is the right default for early [[social-emotional-learning|social-emotional learning]] and for anything requiring judgment about a child's state.

**Cross-cutting skills can be taught directly and cheaply.** [[demir-akar-ai-media-literacy-children-2026|Demir and Akar (2026)]] ran an 18-hour, 5E-based AI-enhanced critical media literacy program for 36 Turkish fourth-graders covering data privacy, safe communication and media ethics, with between-group Cohen's *d* of 1.12 (reading), 1.18 (writing) and 1.31 (total literacy). Programs of this kind teach children what the tools do with their attention and data before they are handed one.

**Write with AI in ways that keep the writing visible.** [[elementary-writing-genai-systematic-review-2026|A systematic review of 8 studies of AI in elementary writing instruction (2019–2025)]] finds conversational tools supporting writing practice and performance and multimodal tools extending composition beyond text — while concluding that automated assessment still requires [[human-in-the-loop-ai|human oversight]] for fairness and accuracy. Where a whole program was tested, the pattern is narrower than the enthusiasm: [[genai-writing-program-primary-l2-motivation-engagement|a nine-week GenAI-supported opinion-writing program]] with 301 Grade 5 and 6 students raised ideal writing self and academic buoyancy and lifted behavioral and emotional engagement, but did not move growth mindset, cognitive or metacognitive engagement, or organization; the value of the study is that it names the risks — over-reliance, shortcut-seeking and diminished self-monitoring. [[lu-ai-multimodal-writing-critical-thinking-2026|A Grade 5 multimodal writing study]] points to the same boundary from the other side: visualizing children's stories produced sustained gains in interpretation, analysis, evaluation and explanation, but **no gain in inference**, and children reported less need to infer implicit meaning once images made it explicit.

**Robots and role-play are legitimate SEL vehicles when the design is deliberate.** [[remind-robot-mediated-roleplay-antibullying-2026|REMind]] uses robot-mediated applied drama to support anti-bullying bystander intervention, with children observing a bullying scenario enacted by social robots, reflecting on character perspectives and rehearsing defense strategies by puppeteering a robotic avatar; in a mixed-methods play-test with 18 children aged 9–10 it supported self-efficacy, perspective-taking and understanding the outcomes of defending.

## At home

**Co-use, with roles kept distinct, protects the parent's part.** [[paratutor-parent-child-tutoring|ParaTutor (Luo et al. 2026)]] separated parent and child roles in LLM-mediated home mathematics tutoring and evaluated it with 23 parent–child dyads (children aged 10–12) across four conditions: generic assistance tended to displace the parent's tutoring role, while the role-separated interface preserved it. The study is also candid about what parents struggled with — problem structure, content knowledge and communication. Interactive [[conversational-ai|AI companions]] read to children deserve the same care: [[liao-role-adaptive-ai-companion-book-talk-2026|Liao (2026)]] found a fixed peer-role companion produced significantly longer interactions but a lower share of words and sentences from the student, and was stronger on factual recall than on emotional or future-oriented reflection — the reason the proposed framework adds a **Parent Advisor** role and names a support vacuum for teachers and parents.

**Expect the division of responsibility to be unclear, and settle it explicitly.** In interviews with 33 US K-12 teachers, [[k12-teachers-ai-companion-literacy-2026|Xiao et al. (2026)]] found 10 named the parent-facing companion scenario as the most concerning, 17 said a teacher should get involved and 11 said it depends; teachers' visibility test left home AI use outside their jurisdiction absent observable effects, and parents were named as primarily responsible while being described as unaware or overstretched — a jurisdictional vacancy. [[family-school-autonomy-support-genai-2026|Fan, Li and Zhang (2026)]] formalize the possible family–school arrangements as additive, synergistic and compensatory models and state plainly that the synergistic version has no supporting evidence, distinguishing dependent from autonomous offloading. Practical translation: agree who monitors which tool, tell families what the school uses and why, and give parents something concrete to do rather than a general warning.

**Know what the tools are before trusting them with a child.** [[child-safety-genai|A child-safety evaluation framework grounded in expert guidance and real incident data]] reports that most AI safety frameworks and benchmarks target adult users despite evidence of heavy youth engagement — a national survey cited in that work found 72% of US adolescents had used AI companions — and that when three Llama Guard models were tested on education-related unsafe prompts, they struggled to identify them. That is an argument for evaluating tools on child-specific hazard categories rather than assuming a general safety layer covers them.

## Adoption, anxiety and school-level decisions

Preschool teachers' intentions to use AI are driven by familiar factors with one addition that matters for professional development: [[preschool-teachers-ai-behavioral-intention-2026|a study of early-childhood settings]] finds perceived usefulness and ease of use central, **AI self-efficacy** a meaningful predictor, **AI anxiety** a deterrent, and subjective norm — colleagues and leadership — shaping intention. Confidence-building and clear institutional norms are therefore levers, not soft extras. The obligations that sit behind those decisions — [[privacy]], data governance, disclosure to families and consent — are covered in [[equity-ethics-pedagogical-safety-research|incorporating equity, ethics, privacy and safety into AIED research]] and in the [[pedagogical-safety]] and [[ai-use-disclosure]] concepts.

Access is the other school-level constraint, and it falls hardest on exactly the children this guidance concerns. [[all-girls-genai-makerspace-gender-equity-2026|An all-girls makerspace case study (Liu et al. 2026)]] documents parents reporting that girls became scared and spoke less in mixed-gender settings, a free image generator that dropped its free functions for a paid tier the nonprofit could not absorb, and English-only prompts as a further barrier. Tools that are free in a pilot and paywalled in production are the norm rather than the exception, so plan for the cost and language assumptions up front.

## What the evidence does and does not support

The largest synthesis in this area is careful about its claims. [[young-people-learning-generative-ai-rapid-review-2026|Arthars and colleagues (2026)]] reviewed 271 empirical papers on generative AI in PreK-12 and found **no single "GenAI effect"**: affective gains are common but weak indicators of learning, the most consistent evidence concerns improved *immediate* performance and product quality, and evidence on durable learning, [[transfer-of-learning|transfer]] and sustained self-[[self-regulated-learning|regulation]] is uneven. Outcomes depend on five entangled conditions — the learner, the tool, the task, the social arrangements, and the cultural and institutional context. Their framework distinguishes whether a student **surrenders, offloads or exercises agency** across cognitive, metacognitive and affective dimensions, and separates four pedagogical functions of AI: learning *from* it, *with* it, *about* it, or *by shaping* it. For children under thirteen, the defensible defaults follow directly: *about* and *by shaping* before *from*; adult co-use wherever the child cannot yet evaluate the output; and no claims about durable learning on the strength of a good afternoon's product.

## Should AI be banned for children under thirteen?

This knowledge base contains no study of the age-based bans now appearing in some school systems, so nothing here can settle whether they work. But three bodies of evidence bear on the question — what the risks are for this age group, what enforcement mechanisms actually do, and what happens when access is restricted versus taught — and they point in the same direction: the case for restricting young children's generative AI use is stronger than the case for a blanket ban, and the case against prohibition is mostly about implementation rather than about risk. Parents, teachers and policymakers are weighing the same evidence, and should probably expect to reach the same conclusions from it.

**The case for restricting access is genuinely strong.** [[child-safety-genai|Child-specific safety evaluation]] shows the protective layer is not there: most safety frameworks and benchmarks target adult users despite evidence of heavy youth engagement (a national survey cited in that work found 72% of US adolescents had used AI companions), and when three Llama Guard models were tested on education-related unsafe prompts they struggled to identify them. The developmental risks are documented rather than hypothetical — generative agents in early childhood can produce content that preoperational children accept as true and are not developmentally calibrated in their feedback, while [[ai-toys-child-development-2026|AI-enabled toys redistribute agency in play]], constraining it when the toy organizes the activity. The benefit side is thin for this band: [[young-people-learning-generative-ai-rapid-review-2026|the 271-paper PreK-12 review]] finds the most consistent evidence concerns immediate performance and product quality rather than durable learning, [[transfer-of-learning|transfer]] or self-regulation, and [[elementary-writing-genai-systematic-review-2026|the elementary writing review]] concludes that automated assessment still requires [[human-in-the-loop-ai|human oversight]] for fairness. Age tiering already encodes a version of this judgment: in [[age-tiered-ai-literacy-guidebooks-2026|the age-tiered AI literacy study]], the edition for learners aged 9 to 12 uses scaffolded, non-generative activities, and supervised generative use is placed in the 13-to-18 edition. Data protection and consent add their own reasons for caution.

**But a ban governs the least important channel, and young children's AI exposure is mostly domestic.** Companion apps, AI toys and family tutoring happen at home, beyond school reach — the pattern [[k12-teachers-ai-companion-literacy-2026|Xiao et al. (2026)]] describe as a jurisdictional vacancy, in which 10 of 33 teachers named the parent-facing companion scenario as their most concerning, 17 said a teacher should get involved and 11 said it depends, and teachers' own visibility test left home use outside their jurisdiction absent observable effects. A school ban leaves the household ungoverned precisely where the risk concentrates. Regulation also has to reach the families who most need information, which is a communication problem rather than a prohibition problem — and [[family-school-autonomy-support-genai-2026|Fan, Li and Zhang (2026)]] note that the synergistic family–school model has no supporting evidence yet, so it has to be built rather than assumed.

**Detection-based enforcement is not a defensible mechanism.** [[bassett-ai-detectors-education-2026|Bassett et al. (2026)]] argue AI detection should not be used in education at all, on three grounds: its estimates are probabilistic and cannot be independently verified because real-world text origin is unknown, its use violates procedural fairness since detector scores do not meet the balance-of-probabilities standard integrity investigations require, and its human-versus-AI dichotomy is meaningless for work created *with* rather than *by* AI. Their conclusion is that detection "does not safeguard academic integrity; it undermines it," fostering suspicion and eroding [[trust]]. Any ban whose enforcement runs through detectors inherits those defects.

**Stronger enforcement can make concealment more attractive.** [[mohamed-temimi-assessment-imperfect-information-disclosure-2026|Mohamed and Temimi (2026)]] model assessment as a problem of imperfect information in which the student knows how the work was produced and the institution sees only the artifact. Their sharpest result is that deterrence operates through a detector's *discrimination* between hidden use and legitimate work rather than its raw catch rate: when false positives rise faster than true positives, stronger monitoring makes concealment relatively *more* attractive. Rules enforced by surveillance can therefore push use underground instead of reducing it. Behavioral evidence points the same way in the absence of bans — [[qu-wang-disclose-or-not-genai-2026|Qu and Wang (2026)]] found non-disclosure among 409 undergraduates was strategic adaptation to perceived peer norms and low interpretive trust rather than moral negligence.

**Where access was taught rather than merely permitted, outcomes recovered.** The clearest comparison in this corpus comes from [[moral-panic-genai-classroom|the study of GenAI availability versus integration]]: making GenAI available without teaching students how to use it was associated with significantly lower performance on applied questions (ω² = 0.35) — presumably because students could not evaluate the output — whereas explicitly teaching students to use it for tasks like data summarizing returned applied performance to pre-GenAI levels and exceeded baseline on one harder quiz. In other words, the alternative to a ban is not laissez-faire; unrestricted access without instruction also failed. This age band can be taught: [[demir-akar-ai-media-literacy-children-2026|the fourth-grade media literacy program]] produced between-group effects of d = 1.12 to 1.31, and the play-centered and unplugged curricula show teachers and children handling AI concepts productively before generative tools are introduced at all.

**Policy coherence is itself a problem, and bans tend to be local improvisation.** [[genai-policies-higher-ed-computing|A comparative analysis of institutional policies and course syllabi]] found institutions broadly encouraging GenAI use (63% of 116 policies) while half of the 98 course syllabi outright prohibited it — a top-down versus bottom-up gap in which instructors improvise local rules that may not align with institutional guidance. [[nash-preservice-teachers-classroom-ai-policies-2026|Nash and Burriss (2026)]] found the same reflex among 27 preservice teachers writing their own classroom policies: 26 of 27 permitted some AI use on teacher-specified terms, only one prohibited it entirely, and the limits were rarely operationalized — one policy allowed AI "to get your thinking started" and then declared "this is where the line should be drawn" without saying where. [[adarkwah-genai-unesco-policy-2026|A UNESCO-framework analysis of 30 universities' GenAI policies]] adds the pattern at policy level: core ethics and governance principles are widely adopted while inclusion, equity, internet access and environmental impact are often overlooked, and many provisions remain declarative rather than operationally assured. Equity is not peripherally affected by age bans but central to them, since enforcement falls unevenly and families with resources retain access at home.

**What the evidence supports instead of a blanket ban.** Age-tiered rules rather than a single cut-off: scaffolded, often non-generative activity for roughly ages 9 to 12, and generative use with adult supervision and a stated purpose thereafter. Regulation of what the school can actually control — provisioned tools, procurement conditions, child-specific hazard evaluation, data minimization, and disclosure to families with consent procedures. Investment in the alternative that the evidence shows working: [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] and critical media literacy teaching, teacher confidence-building ([[preschool-teachers-ai-behavioral-intention-2026|AI self-efficacy predicts early-childhood adoption while AI anxiety deters it]]), and structured co-use at home. Enforcement through design and disclosure rather than detection. Monitoring of inputs rather than outputs, the recommendation that follows from [[stromberg-generative-ai-learning-penalty-secondary-2026|a 26,811-student secondary analysis]] in which homework scores rose while closed-book exam scores fell. And a plainly stated evaluation plan, because the honest position is that no one yet has evidence on whether banning under-13 use produces the intended effect, and the closest analogues — detector enforcement, prohibition-heavy syllabi, and untaught availability — all performed worse than expected.

## A practical checklist for parents and teachers

- **Under 9:** screen-free and embodied first — tangible coding, story, role-play and hands-on making — with AI taught as something people design rather than something that knows. Unplugged family activities work and travel well.
- **Ages 9–12:** scaffolded, often non-generative activities on platforms you have vetted, plus explicit teaching about data privacy, persuasion and what AI cannot know; introduce generative tools with a named adult role and a stated purpose.
- **In both bands:** keep the child's own meaning-making visible — drafting, explaining, reflecting, making — and watch for the substitution pattern where output quality rises while unaided work falls.
- **At home:** decide who monitors which tool and what the school will tell you; use companions and tutors as joint activity rather than quiet supervision; check what an app does with recordings, transcripts and voice data.
- **Before adopting a tool:** ask which child-specific hazards were tested, what it does with data by default, what it costs families if the free tier disappears, and whether it works in the languages your families speak.
- **For policymakers:** prefer age-tiered access rules and tool-vetting conditions to blanket bans, monitor inputs as well as outputs, and publish an evaluation plan — the corpus has no evidence yet that bans change use or learning.
- **When reporting what a child learned:** treat engagement, enjoyment and satisfaction as weak indicators, and prefer evidence of unaided performance and delayed [[transfer-of-learning|transfer]].
