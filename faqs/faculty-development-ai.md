# How Do I Design Faculty Development for AI That Actually Changes Practice?

The programs that change practice are rarely the ones that explain the tools. Across the studies collected here the same pattern recurs: what shifts behavior is structured work on a participant's own course problem, sustained long enough for a new habit to displace an old one, and backed by institutional permission to experiment. This page sets out what that means in design terms. It is the design counterpart to [[faculty-ai-competencies|What Competencies Do Faculty Need in Regard to AI?]], which establishes *what* faculty should be able to do; this page asks how to get them there.

## Design the course task, not the tool demonstration

The clearest comparison here comes from [[faculty-development-centers-genai-training-optimization-2026|Bi et al. (2026)]], who surveyed 568 faculty across five universities and then ran a quasi-experiment with 160 of them. Both arms received four 90-minute workshops over consecutive weeks with identical contact time, platform and facilitator support: one conventional GenAI faculty development, the other a structured cycle of prompt formulation, generated-output review, course-material revision and written reflection, working from a seven-component template covering objectives, task constraints, assessment criteria, verification, disclosure and risk control. The structured group scored higher on all seven readiness dimensions at post-test, with the largest advantage in prompt design at roughly two standard deviations, and it was the only group whose prompt-design gain survived delayed testing; the conventional group's smaller gain had fallen back below baseline by follow-up.

Why that works is visible in [[ai-teaching-innovation-ai-tpack-2026|Bai and Hsieh (2026)]], who surveyed 898 university teachers on [[tpack|AI-TPACK]] scales. Teachers who understood how generative tools work, or could operate them functionally, were no more likely to report inventing and implementing new teaching practices than those who could not. The integrated AI-TPACK construct had no direct path to innovative behavior at all; its influence ran entirely through [[ai-literacy]], teaching [[self-efficacy]] and professional identity.

Two further results set a ceiling on what a workshop alone buys. In [[pishtari-teacher-ai-training-learning-design-2026|a within-subjects study with 13 higher-education teachers]], chatbot access raised the share of activities attaining two or more higher-order Bloom tasks from 41.7% to 91.7% (p = .01) and cut perceived cognitive effort from a median of 6.33 to 3.33 (p = .004), yet the pedagogical prompting training layered on top did not further improve design quality and slightly increased effort. In [[teachers-collaborative-evaluation-ai-content-2026|a workshop with 60 middle-school science teachers]], it was collaborative review of ChatGPT-generated assessment questions that surfaced conceptual-precision problems and the risk of reinforcing [[misconceptions]].

## What improves, and by how much

[[genai-pd-ai-pck-learning-gain-2026|Talebzadeh (2026)]] ran an 8-hour program across four two-hour sessions with 163 teachers and pre-service teachers. Total AI-PCK scores rose from 151.65 to 220.14 (*d* = 2.36, *p* < .001), and the largest component effect was on Assessment Rubrics (*d* = 2.19) — also the weakest area at pretest, making assessment rather than teaching method the sharpest gap. Pre-service teachers gained significantly more than experienced ones (*p* = .033), mostly in scenario-based task design and rubric design. Participants reported converting theoretical knowledge into procedural knowledge and reduced cheating anxiety, while experienced teachers said eight hours was too short to change deep-seated habits and asked for long-term, mentor-based support.

[[sutedjo-faculty-genai-tpack-21-2026|Sutedjo, Chowdhury and Liu (2026)]] show where the deficit sits in higher education: among 127 respondents, self-perceived pedagogical content knowledge was high (M = 4.70) and content knowledge near ceiling (M = 5.15), while technological pedagogical knowledge (M = 2.62), technological content knowledge (M = 2.75) and holistic TPACK (M = 2.55) were markedly lower. Content knowledge showed no significant correlation with any technology-integrated domain (r = .11–.15), so subject-matter mastery does not carry over. Those three domains correlated r = .81–.91, suggesting they may function as one GenAI-integration capacity worth teaching as a shared foundation rather than three separate skills.

[[ai-emotional-intelligence-teacher-development-2026|Aponte et al. (2026)]] add a criterion most programs ignore: relationships. AI contributes to teachers' socio-emotional development, they argue, only when it works as relational infrastructure — protected time for mentoring, peer dialogue, communities of practice — and they offer *relational densification* as the standard, counting trust, continuity of support and co-regulation rather than frequency of contact. Two claims translate into design: freed time is reabsorbed as extra demand unless an institution formally requires it to be reinvested, and conversational agents can become a low-friction substitute for the difficult human negotiations that produce growth. Their framework is a heuristic, not a tested causal claim.

## What resists change

[[laidlaw-genai-identity-crisis-faculty-2026|Laidlaw (2026)]] argues from an autoethnographic account that the resistance is not a skills deficit. Faculty asking what the point of teaching is in a GenAI world are not asking how to use a tool safely; the question is ontological. Reading GenAI as a threshold concept — transformative, troublesome, irreversible, integrative, bounded — she concludes that anxiety, resistance and confusion are necessary parts of crossing it rather than problems to be corrected. Her recommendations invert the usual workshop: open with identity questions rather than demonstrations, run discipline-specific cohorts, allow different timelines, and treat principled non-adoption grounded in disciplinary values as legitimate. She also warns that communicating rules can slide into an "enforcement illusion" that substitutes compliance messaging for support.

[[ai-emotional-intelligence-teacher-development-2026|Aponte et al. (2026)]] locate the other hard limit: AI cannot compensate for chronic overload, punitive accountability cultures, weak leadership or the absence of protected time. A related friction point appears in [[faculty-development-centers-genai-training-optimization-2026|Bi et al. (2026)]], where continued-use intention was the highest-rated satisfaction dimension and tool usability the lowest, so the obstacle is platform friction rather than motivation. And [[ai-teaching-innovation-ai-tpack-2026|Bai and Hsieh (2026)]] found that professional identity predicted innovative behavior nearly twice as strongly among infrequent AI users as among daily users — a borderline result suggesting identity work matters most for those who barely use the tools.

## Institutional conditions, not national background

The strongest finding on context comes from [[faculty-development-centers-genai-training-optimization-2026|Bi et al. (2026)]]. Kazakhstani faculty reported higher baseline readiness than their Chinese counterparts on all seven dimensions, with the widest gaps in disciplinary transfer, prompt design and basic understanding. But in hierarchical regression the Kazakhstan coefficient, clearly significant on its own, fell to non-significance — a reduction of about four fifths — once prior GenAI use, recent AI training, institutional support, perceived permission, multilingual resource access, policy clarity and risk sensitivity were entered. Institutional support and perceived permission carried the most positive weight; risk sensitivity was the only predictor working against readiness. More than twice as many Kazakhstani as Chinese faculty reported AI-related training in the previous six months.

For program design the levers are therefore local and proximate: hands-on practice, explicit permission to experiment, materials in the languages faculty actually work in, and support from the unit. National policy framing alone does not carry readiness, and where the risk-sensitivity finding bites, the response is not to suppress caution but to give it somewhere to be exercised — rubric design and verification routines are how nervousness becomes judgment.

## Disciplines, skeptics and the anxious

[[designing-ai-professional-development-itpack-2026|Dogan (2026)]] supplies the most detailed design architecture here. Building from a PRISMA review of AI professional development plus a search that surfaced 17 further studies, the i-TPACK framework aligns five knowledge domains — intelligent technological knowledge, technological content knowledge, technological pedagogical knowledge, integrated i-TPACK and AI ethics — with four evidence-based pathways: [[active-learning]], models and examples, coaching and expert support, and [[feedback]] and reflection, governed by five principles running from Collective Synergy Over Silos to Flexibility within Structure. Its criticism of the field is worth repeating: in many existing programs, ethics is superficially addressed or entirely absent. The limit is that it is conceptual, with no program-level outcome data.

[[crompton-faculty-technology-integration-standards-2026|Crompton, Burke and Nickel (2026)]] offer the institutional scaffold. Their design-based research across two macro cycles and 114 participants — faculty from 28 U.S. institutions and 11 countries, plus centers-for-teaching directors and accreditation coordinators — produced six standards spanning the whole academic role rather than the teaching slice of it: Instructor, Coordinator, Leader, Researcher, Learner and Contributor. The gap is real: existing frameworks and educator standards target [[k-12]] teachers or address only teaching. Standards of this kind let a development center align its offer with appraisal and accreditation instead of running disconnected workshops.

On differentiation, three sources converge. [[sutedjo-faculty-genai-tpack-21-2026|Sutedjo and colleagues]] argue for deliberate [[discipline-specific-aied|discipline-specific]] programming because expertise does not transfer; [[laidlaw-genai-identity-crisis-faculty-2026|Laidlaw (2026)]] wants discipline-specific cohorts as the setting for identity conversations; and [[ai-teaching-innovation-ai-tpack-2026|Bai and Hsieh (2026)]] suggest differentiating by experience, with limited users needing foundational guidance and frequent users benefiting more from interdisciplinary projects. For the anxious participant, [[ai-emotional-intelligence-teacher-development-2026|Aponte et al. (2026)]] add a governance condition rather than a motivational one: separating wellbeing support from managerial evaluation, minimizing data, limiting purpose, keeping participation voluntary, and guaranteeing human oversight are what make it safe to admit uncertainty in an AI-mediated space.

## How to evaluate a program honestly

Most of this literature measures self-report. [[faculty-development-centers-genai-training-optimization-2026|Bi et al. (2026)]] measured [[self-report-measures|self-reported]] readiness, not enacted teaching or student learning, and its groups were formed by voluntary sign-up and institutional scheduling rather than randomization, making its results associations rather than causal estimates. [[ai-teaching-innovation-ai-tpack-2026|Bai and Hsieh (2026)]] is cross-sectional and self-selected, drew on eight universities in one country, and measured every construct from the same respondents at one time; their innovation scale also kept the wording of a general innovation measure. [[sutedjo-faculty-genai-tpack-21-2026|Sutedjo and colleagues]] cannot establish direction of causation, which matters because their strongest claim — that technological knowledge acts as a gateway — is exactly what a cross-sectional design cannot settle. [[genai-pd-ai-pck-learning-gain-2026|Talebzadeh (2026)]] reports very large effects without randomization, follow-up or discipline-level breakdown, so *d* = 2.36 should read as short-term rather than as proof of durable practice. [[laidlaw-genai-identity-crisis-faculty-2026|Laidlaw (2026)]] is an autoethnographic reflection, and [[ai-emotional-intelligence-teacher-development-2026|Aponte et al. (2026)]] state that their framework is conceptual.

A defensible evaluation therefore measures artifacts rather than opinions: the course materials a participant revised, the rubrics written, the tasks designed. It tests at a delay, because the gap in [[faculty-development-centers-genai-training-optimization-2026|Bi et al. (2026)]] appeared only at follow-up, where the conventional group had decayed and the structured group had not. It treats satisfaction and confidence as weak signals — [[pishtari-teacher-ai-training-learning-design-2026|Pishtari and colleagues (2026)]] note that quality gains alone do not prove internalisation, since rising design quality can equally reflect delegating routine work or accepting AI-generated structure. And it adds at least one relational indicator, following [[ai-emotional-intelligence-teacher-development-2026|Aponte et al. (2026)]], because improving artifacts while isolating participants is not what the program claimed.

## A checklist for program designers

- Anchor every session in a real course artifact — objectives, rubric or assessment task — that participants bring with them and revise before they leave.
- Use a prompt-task cycle with verification, disclosure and risk control built into the template rather than bolted on as ethics content.
- Run long enough, and pair it with mentor-based follow-up, because experienced staff report short formats cannot shift deep-seated habits.
- Build [[discipline-specific-aied|discipline-specific]] cohorts and differentiate by experience level instead of running one universal workshop.
- Open with identity questions before demonstrations, and treat principled non-adoption as a legitimate professional position.
- Secure institutional support, perceived permission, protected time and access in faculty's working languages; these carry more weight than national policy framing.
- Evaluate with artifacts, delayed measurement and at least one relational indicator; report effect sizes honestly against self-report and cross-sectional designs.
