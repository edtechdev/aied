## Main highlights and questions

- [Can education really be automated?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/videla-why-education-cannot-be-automated-2026/) Videla, Ross and Aros argue that AI slides into schooling so easily because schooling already treats learning as information processing: input, storage, output. An [adaptive tutor](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/adaptive-learning/) that picks the next item, or an [automated grader](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/automated-assessment/) that scores a response, extends that model. Drawing on enactivism, a theory of learning as [embodied action](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/embodied-learning/), they locate learning in participation and productive friction: the hesitation before choosing a method, the failed prototype, the revision that follows. They propose AI for Embodiment: tools that expand perception and joint action. This is a conceptual position paper with no data. Use it as a question set for a vendor demo or pilot meeting: What will students actually do themselves once the tool handles the obvious part? Which thinking step disappears when the answer arrives instantly? If the tool vanished tomorrow, what could students still do?
- [Does reasoning-first scaffolding beat an answer-giving assistant?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/fan-human-first-scaffolding-diagnostic-2026/) [Medical students](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/medical-education/) who used reasoning-first [scaffolding](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/scaffolding/) before seeing an answer diagnosed more accurately than students given direct answers, and the advantage persisted after the AI was removed. The scaffold-first groups were the only ones to beat both unsupported classmates and a frontier model once the AI was gone. The trial is a preprint and tests a one-week withdrawal at one institution. Design the task so students commit to a reasoning step before the assistant can answer.
- [Does an adaptive AI tutor beat ordinary computer-assisted learning?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/ma-ai-cal-rct-rural-china-2026/) A large three-arm randomized trial in rural Chinese [primary schools](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/k-12/) found that an [adaptive AI tutor](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/adaptive-learning/) produced no measurable advantage over a matched non-adaptive program, and the study could rule out anything beyond a very small effect. The adaptive label did not add detectable value to a conventional program. If you are buying an adaptive tutor, ask what it adds beyond the software and teaching you already have.
- [Do more minutes in the tutoring system mean more learning?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/schaaf-its-frequency-math-gains-2026/) A one-semester study of Grade 8 and 9 classes in [disadvantaged communities](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/differential-effects-across-learner-groups/#socioeconomic-status-geography-and-age) in Germany found that class-level time in the [intelligent tutoring](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/intelligent-tutoring/) system did not predict gains; students averaged under ten minutes a week. Students who used the system more than classmates during lessons did slightly better, while extra home use showed no advantage. Put the system where a teacher can see the work and intervene, and treat home use as practice rather than instruction.
- [Can AI practice erode the skill it is meant to build?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/yao-scaffold-stays-on-ai-skill-formation-2026/) Practice records from more than 10,000 Codeforces users show a growing pattern after 2022: more first attempts accepted, fewer attempts per solved problem, and fewer rapid retries. The authors treat that pattern as a signature of AI-assisted practice. Outside proctored contests, a stronger signature predicted a smaller rating gain. The design is observational, so it shows a pattern consistent with students letting AI do the work, not proof that AI caused the loss. Protect unassisted practice if you want the [skill](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/transfer-of-learning/) to survive the assessment.
- [Why do students over-rely on AI even when they know better?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/gao-too-good-to-resist-overreliance-2026/) In a survey of [vocational foreign-language learners](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/language-learning/), fear of missing out and treating the chatbot as a person were the strongest predictors of self-reported [over-reliance](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/cognitive-offloading/). Self-rated [AI literacy](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/ai-literacy/) and reported [self-regulated learning](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/self-regulated-learning/), such as planning and monitoring, showed no direct relation to it. Students who felt confident but did not plan, monitor, or revise their own work were the ones who over-relied. Design for the emotional pull and for routines that restrain it; another literacy module is unlikely to be enough.
- [Does instruction make AI-supported study more self-regulated?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/liu-genai-srl-meta-analysis-2026/) A meta-analysis found better outcomes with [generative AI](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/generative-ai/) than with no AI, and a larger advantage when instruction was added to the tool. The difference between the two no-AI comparisons was not significant, so the study does not establish that combined instruction beats standalone AI across designs. The practical takeaway for [self-regulated learning](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/self-regulated-learning/) is to pair the tool with structured prompts, monitoring, and reflection.
- [Does adaptive AI feedback improve writing, or just grammar?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/al-washahi-ai-adaptive-feedback-meta-analysis-2026/) A meta-analysis of [adaptive writing feedback](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/ai-feedback-quality/) studies found the largest gains in grammar and surface accuracy, smaller gains in mechanics, and no significant effect on argumentation quality. Effects were strongest in [English-as-a-foreign-language](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/language-learning/) contexts and in longer interventions, and results varied widely across studies. A grammar gain is not evidence of better argumentation. If argumentation is what you grade, measure it and teach it.
- [What should teachers hand to AI, and what should they keep?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/farooq-standard-anchored-delegation-teachers-2026/) Interviews with [teachers in Punjab, Pakistan](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/global-south/), found a consistent division of labor. Most accepted AI drafts or substantial help with [lesson planning](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/learning-design/), none accepted AI-drafted learning objectives, and most kept encouragement and emotional support for themselves. The pattern, standard-anchored delegation, is a practical rule: hand over a component when an external standard defines a correct output and you can check the result; keep the work that depends on relationship, judgment, and accountability.
- [Does Bloom's taxonomy still fit AI-assisted work?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/austin-bloom-taxonomy-generative-ai-2026/) A conceptual analysis compares [Bloom's taxonomy](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/bloom-taxonomy/) with Fink's significant learning, an inverted Bloom's sequence, and a recursive model that starts with the AI-assisted product and works backward. It reports no sample or measured outcome. The argument is that when answers are instant, the useful question shifts from which rung a task occupies to whether the process shows [metacognition](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/metacognition/), revision, and understanding. Use the levels as a shared vocabulary; they do not measure what the student did.

## New and revised FAQs

One page is new this week; two existing pages were substantially revised.

### [How can AI help or hurt collaborative learning in my course?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/ai-help-hurt-collaborative-learning/)

**New this week.** Studies find several benefits from AI in group work, including faster coordination, more ideas, and [feedback](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/feedback/), while most of the harms are still predictions rather than measured outcomes. The pattern is that AI helps when it supports thinking about the task, addresses the group rather than one student, stays brief, and remains under the teacher's control. The new page separates what is measured from what is argued, then points to the assignment-level guidance on [group work](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/collaborative-learning/).

### [How should I handle AI in group and collaborative assignments?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/group-work-ai/)

**Revised this week.** The other group-work page is the how. It tells you to write permissions for each phase of the task, make the group's AI negotiation a submitted artifact, sequence the work so it cannot be split into parallel chatbot sessions, and keep one part each member answers alone. Read it while drafting the brief. Read the new page above while deciding whether the design is worth the trouble.

### [How do I keep students from over-relying on AI?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/reducing-over-reliance/)

**Revised this week.** The page adds a verdict section on whether students can learn to recognize [over-reliance](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/cognitive-offloading/). The answer is yes, with a condition: the skill depends on criteria supplied before the query, not on more hours with the tool. The page also states the limit: no study in the corpus tests whether that recognition transfers to an unaided task.

## New concepts

### [Bloom's Taxonomy](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/bloom-taxonomy/)

Bloom's taxonomy is doing two jobs in AI education: a classification instrument for coding items and machine output, and a design framework for ordering cognitive demand. The classification side is fragile: classifiers trained to assign Bloom levels often fail on AI-generated questions, and an item's intended level explains little about its difficulty. The design side remains useful, especially for asking whether an AI-assisted task scaffolds thinking or shortcuts it.

### [Critiques of AI in education](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/critiques-of-ai-in-education/)

This collection gathers the conceptual, normative, and political arguments that ask what AI is doing to education while the field measures whether it works. It returns to four concerns: AI extends a model of learning as information processing, teaching becomes optimization, adoption is treated as inevitable, and private providers concentrate decision-making. Before adopting a tool, ask what the institution is for and what kind of learning it wants to protect.

### [Learning environments as systems](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/learning-environments-as-systems/)

A learning environment is a stack of nested levels, from the learner and task through the course, institution, and policy, and effects cross those levels. Add an AI tutor to one course, for example, and the extra drafting may shift the workload onto a later assignment, a writing center, or a student's job. A study that measures only the course can miss where the effect landed. Decide which level you are changing and which level you will measure.

### [Rubrics](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/rubrics/)

An analytic rubric scores separate dimensions independently; a holistic rubric reads the whole piece. It also explains why generative AI unsettled rubrics built around the final product: a fluent artifact no longer shows what the student contributed. The design consequence is to add process descriptors and evidence of judgment, alongside clearer wording on the final product.

## For instructors

### [Clarify authorship before you grade the artifact](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/chen-authorship-blurs-responsibility-dilutes-2026/)

[Chen and Cheng](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/chen-authorship-blurs-responsibility-dilutes-2026/) followed Chinese high school students across three waves. Greater ambiguity about which ideas were the student's predicted that students felt less ownership of the work, deflected responsibility away from themselves, and reported more GenAI-related academic behaviors. The sequence matters: students who cannot tell what is theirs report feeling less accountable for what they submit.

Add a short [authorship statement](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/academic-integrity/) that names which ideas, drafts, and edits are the student's, and discuss it before the assignment rather than after a suspicious submission. A conversation about ownership does more than a detector, because it gives the student a claim to defend.

See related: [How can I reduce AI cheating in my course?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/reduce-ai-cheating/)

### [Count the verification work AI adds](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/saleem-redefining-assessment-age-ai-2026/)

[Saleem and colleagues](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/saleem-redefining-assessment-age-ai-2026/) interviewed academic staff at private universities in Malaysia. They described extra time checking authenticity, re-evaluating work, and giving repeated feedback, and a shift in the [teacher role](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/teacher-role/) from facilitator toward monitor and gatekeeper. Their response was to move [assessment](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/assessment/) toward in-class and [process-oriented formats](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/process-oriented-assessment/), including oral presentations, live case analysis, debate, and reflection.

Add one live or [oral defense](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/oral-assessment/) to a major assignment and budget the marking time it creates. The workload is real, and a policy that adds verification without adding hours shifts the cost onto the instructor.

See related: [How do I redesign assessment so that a grade still tells me something defensible?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/redesign-assessment-ai-era/)

### [Calibrate support to what students already know](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/yusuf-genai-expertise-reversal-cognitive-load-2026/)

[Yusuf and colleagues](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/yusuf-genai-expertise-reversal-cognitive-load-2026/) ran a series of experiments with undergraduate computer science students. Lower-expertise learners gained far more from [generative AI](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/generative-ai/) support than higher-expertise learners, and detailed guidance produced a crossover in which the help that benefited novices added mental effort that did not help them learn and hurt more knowledgeable students.

Gate help by prior knowledge instead of giving every student the same assistant. A [scaffold](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/scaffolding/) that models the first steps is useful to a novice and can interrupt an expert who already has a procedure.

See related: [How do I keep students from over-relying on AI?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/reducing-over-reliance/)

### [Put AI use inside the rubric](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/estaphan-ai-aware-rubric-transition-2026/)

[Estaphan and Zulfiqar](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/estaphan-ai-aware-rubric-transition-2026/) interviewed educators who moved a second-year health and medicine course from a section-based rubric to an AI-aware analytic [rubric](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/rubrics/). The educators reported more confidence in grading and more explicit teaching of [AI literacy](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/ai-literacy/), along with heavier marking and uncertainty about concealed use.

Add a row for ethical and reflective AI use, and require evidence of the process the student followed. The rubric change is a teaching change, so plan the marking load and the classroom time it needs.

See related: [Can I use AI to grade student work fairly and reliably?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/ai-grading-fairness-reliability/)

### [Move accessibility from endorsement to practice](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/martinez-genai-accessibility-minnesota-educators-2026/)

[Martinez and colleagues](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/martinez-genai-accessibility-minnesota-educators-2026/) surveyed Minnesota educators. Most who used AI endorsed improved [accessibility](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/accessibility/) as a benefit, while far fewer reported using AI to support students with disabilities. The comparison describes two different survey answers, not proof that the same people failed to act, because the items differ in scope.

Ask for disability-specific uses in your next course review, such as text-to-speech preparation, caption cleanup, or alternative-format drafts. General differentiation and language support are useful, but they are not the same as accommodation.

See related: [How can AI support disabled and neurodivergent learners in my course?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/ai-disabled-neurodivergent-learners/)

### [Measure whether confidence matches skill](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/chen-teacher-self-efficacy-ai-collaboration-review-2026/)

[Chen and Liu](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/chen-teacher-self-efficacy-ai-collaboration-review-2026/) reviewed a large international body of research on teachers' [confidence](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/self-efficacy/) in working with AI. That confidence is not a single trait; it combines operational confidence with pedagogical confidence, and the [match between confidence and skill](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/self-assessment/) is the pivot. Exaggerated confidence predicts over-reliance, while confidence that matches skill supports deeper collaboration.

Build faculty development around spotting bad output and deciding when to override it. Comfort with the tool is a different skill from judging its output. A teacher who feels ready but cannot judge the output is the one most likely to [over-rely](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/cognitive-offloading/) on it.

See related: [How do I design faculty development for AI that actually changes practice?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/faculty-development-ai/)

## For developers

### [Assume students will split the answer across sessions](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/muttineni-llm-tutor-cross-session-leakage-2026/)

[Muttineni and colleagues](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/muttineni-llm-tutor-cross-session-leakage-2026/) show that a learner can assemble a solution across sessions, retries, and renumbered practice versions of the same problem. Their record of every problem each learner had already seen cut leakage through renumbered variants from complete or near-complete success to roughly one attempt in five, while honest learners still finished almost every problem. It did not reduce split-request leakage beyond a per-session gate.

Track what each learner has been shown across the whole course, not just inside the current conversation, and treat a renumbered variant as the same problem. A session-level guard catches only the simplest version of the attack.

See related: [What are best practices for developing an effective AI tutor?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/developing-ai-tutor/)

### [Put the teaching rules in code, then log them](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/kadir-ensemble-specialized-llms-adaptive-tutoring-2026/)

[Kadir](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/kadir-ensemble-specialized-llms-adaptive-tutoring-2026/) evaluated an architecture that separates the teaching rules from the model that writes the reply. A rule-based planner decides what to do; an [LLM](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/llm/) turns that plan into text. In a large simulation with synthetic students, the planner followed the attempt-before-hint rule in every dialogue, while the prompt-only baseline did not, and it used fewer tokens and less latency. Because the students are simulated, the result concerns architectural control.

Keep the policy layer independent of the model call, and log which rule fired on each turn. That trace is what lets you debug a refusal, audit a constraint, and regression-test a change.

See related: [How do we train an AI tutor to guide students rather than answer them?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/training-ai-tutors-to-guide-rather-than-answer/)

### [Blinding the model does not remove the fairness gap](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/wang-predictive-models-intersectional-fairness-2026/)

[Wang and Chen](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/wang-predictive-models-intersectional-fairness-2026/) analyzed records from the Open University [Learning Analytics](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/learning-analytics/) Dataset. Pass rates differed between disabled male and non-disabled female students, and a model trained without protected attributes widened the [equity gap](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/equity-in-ai-education/) in who was predicted to pass. Adjusting the model's decision threshold after training cut that gap substantially, with no statistically detectable loss in accuracy.

Audit error rates for each combination of disability and gender, not just overall accuracy, and test [an adjustment to the model's decision threshold](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/bias-mitigation/) before shipping a prediction. Removing the protected field from the input does not remove the pattern from the data.

See related: [How does AI bias and unfairness show up in education, and what can we do about it?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/ai-bias-fairness-education/)

## For researchers

### [Most studies cannot tell whether AI extends or replaces thinking](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/li-cognitive-agency-human-genai-realist-synthesis-2026/)

[Li, Abdul Hanid and Zhang](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/li-cognitive-agency-human-genai-realist-synthesis-2026/) reviewed 93 studies for a [realist synthesis](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/meta-analysis-systematic-review/) of [cognitive agency](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/agency/): whether students use AI as an extension of their own thinking or hand the thinking over. Most reported no learning measure taken independently of AI assistance, and only a handful combined evidence of how students worked with such a measure. The review lists seven questions researchers can test about how learners accept or scrutinize AI output.

Design at least one outcome measured without the tool, and pair it with a process trace of how the student used the output. A study that measures performance only while the AI is present cannot tell you what the student can do afterward.

See related: [What measures and research methods can an instructor use to evaluate AI-related interventions?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/evaluating-ai-interventions-methods/)

### [Use is widespread; evaluation is not](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/moeller-genai-use-evaluation-pisa-2025-2026/)

[Richter and colleagues](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/moeller-genai-use-evaluation-pisa-2025-2026/) analyzed PISA 2025 data from dozens of countries. Most students used AI chatbots for schoolwork at least weekly, while far fewer were often asked to evaluate AI output and many were almost never asked. Socioeconomic advantage predicted more frequent evaluation, independent of use.

Measure the opportunity to learn evaluation, not only the frequency of use. The gap between using a tool and being taught to judge it is where the [equity problem](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/equity-in-ai-education/) sits.

See related: [What are best practices for reporting and interpreting AI in education research?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/reporting-interpreting-aied-research/)

### [The evidence base is thinner than the demos](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/raman-agentic-ai-design-logic-evidence-theory-2026/)

[Raman, Tang and Nedungadi](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/raman-agentic-ai-design-logic-evidence-theory-2026/) coded 170 papers on [agentic AI](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/agentic-ai/), systems that can plan and act with less supervision, from 2022 to 2026 against a framework for design, outcomes, theory, context, and methods. Most did not engage a [learning theory](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/learning-theories/) at all, most never state whether the agent acts autonomously or under [supervision](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/human-in-the-loop-ai/), and few meet the authors' strong-evidence criteria. The review coded titles and abstracts from one database, so it describes what papers report, not what they did.

Before trusting a claim about an agentic tutor, ask two questions: How much can it do without a teacher, and what was it compared against? A demo can show capability without showing an effect.

See related: [How do we know an educational AI is working correctly, not just scoring well?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/checking-whether-educational-ai-works/)


## For administrators

### [Readiness is a coordination problem](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/liu-ai-leadership-readiness-school-leaders-2026/)

[Liu and Seifullinqyzy](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/liu-ai-leadership-readiness-school-leaders-2026/) surveyed school leaders in China and Kazakhstan. Understanding concrete AI-enabled teaching scenarios was the strongest predictor of whether leaders saw instructional value in AI. The analysis sorted leaders into four groups, from high understanding and readiness to low understanding and readiness, including a group that valued AI but was only averagely prepared and a group that was cautious about risk. The biggest gaps between what leaders said mattered and what they felt able to do were [ethics and risk governance](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/governance/), [teacher development](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/teacher-education/), and content quality audit.

Target leadership development at those three gaps rather than at general AI enthusiasm. A leader who values AI but cannot coordinate ethics review, staff development, and content audit is not ready to implement it.

See related: [What should we consider when adopting AI tools?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/adopting-ai-options-and-issues/)

### [Turn principles into a use-case decision record](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/nguyen-operationalising-ai-governance-policy-method-2026/)

[Nguyen](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/nguyen-operationalising-ai-governance-policy-method-2026/) proposes an eight-step method that converts AI-use scenarios into a reusable [governance package](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/governance/): a scope sheet, use-case inventory, decision matrix, risk-safeguard map, clause bank, role responsibility table, implementation toolkit, and review cycle. Several decision categories replace a simple allow-or-prohibit split, and each use case is linked to the decision, clause, role, and tool that justify it. The method was tested with experts in a walkthrough, not in a field trial.

Start with an inventory of actual uses and a decision record for each one. A principle that cannot be traced to a clause, a role, and a review date will not survive the first disputed case.

See related: [How do we write and implement an institutional AI policy?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/institutional-ai-policy/)

### [Check what your course syllabi actually say](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/wu-governance-course-policy-2026/)

[Wu and colleagues](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/articles/wu-governance-course-policy-2026/) analyzed [course syllabi](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/concepts/curriculum-design/) at one large US public research university. Most were silent or prohibitive, a few permitted specified uses or broad use with safeguards, and a few made AI itself the subject of study. Only a few were explicitly aligned with institutional guidance, and none contradicted a specific requirement.

Audit course-level policy rather than assuming the institutional statement reached students. Silence leaves students to guess what the institution meant.

See related: [How do I write a course AI policy and communicate it to students?](https://edtechdev.github.io/aied/faqs/course-ai-policy/)
