> **Shadow AI** — the use of AI tools that an institution has not approved, vetted or even catalogued, by the people inside it. The term descends from shadow IT, but the object is different: shadow IT substituted unauthorized systems for approved ones, mostly in information processing, while shadow AI covers tools that generate content, support decisions and perform knowledge work. Control therefore shifts from managing access to regulating use, and the person using the tool is usually the one making the judgment call.

## Questions to Consider

- Can you name the AI tools your staff and faculty used last month, or only the ones you bought?
- What does your institution require someone to do to get a tool approved, and how long does that take compared with the week in which a new model ships?
- If a teacher uploads student work to an unapproved tool today, who finds out — and would anyone know if it never caused a visible incident?
- Is the absence of guidance in your institution a decision, or a gap that individual educators are filling on your behalf?
- What would you have to publish for a cautious educator to choose the sanctioned path without losing a week of preparation?

## Introduction

The adoption literature keeps returning to the same gap. In [[robert-educause-impact-ai-work-higher-education-2026|EDUCAUSE's 2026 survey of 1,960 staff and faculty]], 94% had used AI tools for work in the past six months, while only 54% were aware of the policies meant to guide that use. Almost all respondents' institutions, 92%, reported a work-related AI strategy, and just 13% were measuring return on investment for it. More than half, 56%, said they used tools their institution does not provide.

That combination describes a governance surface that exists on paper and a practice that exists elsewhere. People are not waiting for permission, and in many institutions they are not being asked to. Shadow AI is the name for what fills the space between a strategy document and a classroom.

The literature complicates the moral framing. [[rameskumar-shadow-ai-legitimized-higher-education-2027|Rameskumar and colleagues]] interviewed 20 academic educators in Germany and found that informal use was not hidden cynicism but a practice educators justified to themselves in professional terms. [[kelso-unsanctioned-technology-use-educational-institutes-2025|Kelso and colleagues]] surveyed 375 educators who named 1,373 apps and services, 494 of them unique. [[dangol-relief-or-displacement-teachers-generative-ai-2026|Dangol and colleagues]] followed 22 teachers in a U.S. district that had endorsed AI before writing rules, and found them drawing their own boundaries. Shadow AI, on this evidence, is what a provision gap looks like from the inside.

## Why it happens

The proximate cause is usually missing guidance. Educators in the German study described rules that were absent, too general, fragmented, or disconnected from concrete teaching situations. One said plainly that nobody had told them what was or was not allowed.

The structural cause is speed. Procurement cycles and policy committees move at the pace of institutions; model releases move at the pace of vendors. A tool that a colleague demonstrates on Monday can be in use by Friday, and the approval process that would have caught it has not opened yet.

The motivational cause is utility, and it is not close. In the ASU survey, pedagogical utility accounted for 213 of the selections educators gave for choosing a tool, functional convenience 155, accessibility 187 and cost 164. Security was named by 24 of 375. Discovery ran through people rather than procurement: 290 learned about tools from professional peers, 141 from administrators, 100 from search, and 100 from advertising.

Cost and access deserve their own line. Free tiers are the reason many educators can try a tool at all, and the reason the institution cannot see the trial. When the sanctioned alternative is a licensed seat with a waiting list, the free tool wins on the only criterion the teacher is measuring that afternoon.

## How educators justify it

The German study's contribution is a sequence rather than a count. Educators first classified a use by whether it fit the professional role: preparing materials, organizing content and generating examples counted as role-supportive; wording an email sat outside the role; producing work that displaced student thinking was problematic. They then invoked efficiency, with time savings treated as sufficient justification on their own. They added value claims, that the material was better or more varied, not merely faster. And they closed with a responsibility move: the tool is neutral, the educator decides, and the educator remains accountable for what reaches students.

That last step is the one institutions should study. It is also the one that makes a ban feel irrelevant, because the person applying it already believes they exercised judgment. The paper reads it as moral legitimation rather than as evasion, which is a more uncomfortable finding than simple noncompliance.

## What it costs

The ASU survey gives the harm side concrete numbers. Of 375 educators, 167 encountered privacy- or security-invasive behavior and uninstalled the app, 108 of them in K-12. But 62 who worried their data was being sold or shared kept the tools anyway, citing workflow dependence. Administrators named 16 serious concerns, led by data leakage and privacy invasion.

Integration is where exposure compounds. K-12 educators connected 341 apps to institutional systems and higher education educators 116, and most of those apps required account provisioning. Nearly a third of K-12 educators, 30.3%, knew of an institutional policy covering unsanctioned use; 22 admitted violating one.

The [[privacy|privacy]] problem is structural rather than individual. A tool that receives a class list, an IEP or a disciplinary note has created a record the institution did not authorize, cannot audit, and may only learn about when a breach notification arrives.

## What teachers actually do about it

The district study is the most useful corrective to the image of the careless adopter. Teachers there had a district-wide platform, MagicSchool AI, and still used general systems such as ChatGPT and Gemini. Their boundaries were pedagogical: one allowed AI during growth work and withdrew it at mastery, so students had to demonstrate the skill unaided. A special education teacher taught fourth graders to interrogate authorship, treating rephrasing as acceptable and idea generation as not.

Voice and privacy followed. Teachers rewrote outputs so materials still sounded like them, cross-checked facts, and treated generated text as a majority source rather than an authority. They redacted student names and used district accounts deliberately, so that their usage could be audited if it were challenged. That is [[human-ai-collaboration|collaborative sensemaking]] with an audit trail, and it is closer to what a governance office says it wants than to what a compliance report usually captures.

The cost of this improvisation falls unevenly. Teachers who had time, confidence and collegial support built the practices; teachers in schools without them did not. [[equity-in-ai-education|Equity]] gaps that begin as staffing gaps end as differences in what students are asked to do.

## What institutions can do

The first move is to publish the sanctioned path, not to close the unofficial one. The teachers in the district study asked for rules, vetted-tool lists, examples and time, and none of those requests require a new platform.

The second is to shorten the path. [[sheneman-mindrouter-institutional-ai-sovereignty-2026|MindRouter]] and the [[huijts-suilen-institutional-ai-sovereignty-gateway-2025|Fontys gateway]] both show a model in which approval is a routing decision rather than a procurement event: an approved model appears in a gateway with a model card naming region, cost and alternatives, and a teacher can use it the same day. [[institutional-ai-infrastructure|Institutional AI infrastructure]] is what makes that possible; the absence of it is what shadow AI is made of.

The third is to measure what is being spent and used, including the spend that never reached a purchase order. EDUCAUSE's 13% ROI figure suggests that most institutions cannot currently answer whether the sanctioned tools are earning their seats, which leaves the argument for them resting on authority alone.

The fourth is to treat [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] as a control, not a curriculum add-on. A teacher who can read a data policy, recognize a vendor's retention terms and redact a student name is doing risk management that no approval form replaces.

## Common Misconceptions

**"Shadow AI is just shadow IT with a new name."** The German study draws the distinction: shadow IT risks attach to infrastructure, while shadow AI risks attach to content, judgment and the record of what was produced.

**"A ban will end it."** In the ASU survey, 30.3% of K-12 educators knew of a policy and 22 admitted breaking one. Prohibition without a sanctioned alternative changes where the use happens, not whether it does.

**"This is a student problem."** Every study here is about educators: preparation, grading, communication, translation. The student story is real, but it is not the one with administrative credentials attached.

**"Awareness training solves it."** 94% used the tools and 54% knew the policy, and the 40-point gap did not close when people were told. What closes it is a usable alternative with a short path to approval.

## Further Reading

Two closed-access studies address shadow AI directly. They are linked rather than ingested, so no claim on this page rests on their findings.

- [Beyond Curiosity: Habit and Organizational Learning Capability as Predictors of Shadow AI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2026.109243) — Lovett and Yang, *Computers in Human Behavior*, article 109243, November 2026. Survey evidence from recent workplace AI users, with habit carrying the largest relative-importance weight and personal payment for tools associated with elevated shadow AI.
- [Shadow AI, Human Oversight, and Institutional Trust: A Socio-technical Model of Responsible AI Governance in the Public Sector](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-38498-0_11) — Jonathan and colleagues, Springer, 2026. PLS-SEM on 214 public sector employees, treating shadow AI as the mechanism linking governance conditions to responsible use.
