> **Media literacy** — the capacity to access, analyze, evaluate, create and act on media, including the questions a reader brings to who made a message, for whom, with what technique and to what effect. In [[ai-education|AI in education]] the construct has acquired a second object: [[generative-ai|generative AI]] produces media at industrial scale — text, images, audio, video — that is cheap to make, hard to attribute and designed to be plausible, so evaluation of *synthetic* content now sits beside the older critique of representation and persuasion.

## Questions to Consider

- Media literacy traditionally asked who produced a message and why. When the producer is a model trained on everyone, what does that question become — and can a student answer it?
- Deepfakes and fabricated citations are different failures: one is a false record of the world, the other a false record of the literature. Does one curriculum address both?
- Critical media literacy is often taught through production — students make media to understand it. Is generating with AI a production pedagogy, or does it skip the craft that made the critique possible?
- Media literacy and [[information-literacy|information literacy]] share evaluation, but differ in object: one studies messages, the other sources. Which framing belongs in a course that has room for only one?

## Introduction

Media literacy is the older critical tradition in this knowledge base, and the one most exposed by generative AI. Its established questions — authorship, purpose, technique, audience, embedded values — were built for media that someone had to make, at a cost, with an intent that could be inferred. Synthetic media breaks the first of those assumptions: the cost of production collapses, attribution is uncertain, and the artifact may have no author with a purpose at all.

The research in the corpus approaches the problem from two directions. One studies critical media literacy as a curriculum and a pedagogy, and finds teachers under-trained for the tools that now produce disinformation. The other evaluates interventions, including an AI-enhanced program for primary-school children that produced gains in critical media literacy. A third strand, from public service media, argues that provision for teenagers tilts toward functional AI skills and away from critical evaluation, and proposes a competency framework to correct it.

This page is distinct from [[information-literacy]], which concerns finding and judging sources, and from [[ai-literacy]], which concerns understanding AI systems. Media literacy's object is the message itself — its construction, circulation and effect — and its nearest neighbor in the concept map is [[critical-thinking]].

## What the corpus shows

### The curriculum is present but shallow

Semi-structured interviews with seven leading experts in critical media literacy, paired with a survey of 141 university students in communication and education programs, found that technology offers real opportunities for teaching and learning but its inclusion in the curriculum is limited and often superficial ([[critical-media-literacy-education-2026|the critical media literacy study, 2026]]). Teachers were described as under-trained to manage tools that produce disinformation, deepfakes and fake news, which in turn limits students' [[critical-thinking]]. The authors argue for a [[critical-pedagogy]]-grounded version of the subject that treats evaluation as ethical and participatory practice rather than tool training.

### Intervention evidence exists, at small scale

An 18-hour intervention built on the 5E instructional model and integrated with generative AI tools produced large gains in critical media literacy among 36 fourth-grade students in a Turkish public primary school ([[demir-akar-ai-media-literacy-children-2026|Demir and Akar, 2026]]). The explanatory sequential [[mixed-methods-research|mixed-methods]] design also reported qualitative shifts toward digital safety, source verification, online ethics and critique of AI output. Thirty-six students in one school is a proof of concept, not a basis for scaling, but it is the corpus's clearest evidence that the construct can be taught to children.

### Provision tilts toward skills

A commissioned report for public service media reviewed more than 40 AI literacy frameworks, conducted 35 expert interviews and collected survey data between March and June 2025, concluding that provision for 14–19-year-olds favors technical and functional AI skills over critical evaluation ([[ai-literacies-young-adults-2025|the AI literacies report, 2025]]). Its six competency areas place critical evaluation of outputs and systems alongside ethical and societal awareness, which is a media-literacy framing applied to AI rather than a separate construct.

## What this means for practice

- **Teach evaluation of synthetic media as a distinct skill.** Recognizing a fabricated citation and recognizing a deepfake are different tasks with different tells; a unit that covers only one leaves the other unaddressed.
- **Keep production in the curriculum, but examine what AI changes.** Making media has been the pedagogy that made critique concrete; generating with AI can support it only if students still examine the choices the tool made for them.
- **Treat teacher training as the binding constraint.** The interview evidence names under-training, not student resistance, as the obstacle to a serious curriculum.
- **Pair media literacy with information literacy rather than merging them.** They share critical thinking but differ in object; a course can teach both, but the outcomes should be separate.
