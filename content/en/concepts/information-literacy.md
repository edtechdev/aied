> **Information literacy** — the set of capabilities a person needs to recognize an information need, find material that answers it, judge whether that material deserves belief, and use it ethically. In [[ai-education|AI in education]] the construct is being rewritten in public: [[generative-ai|generative AI]] now performs the *finding* and much of the *summarizing*, which leaves the judgment about sources as the part that has to be taught rather than assumed, and puts [[librarians|librarians]] and the [[libraries|library]] that employs them at the center of the question.

## Questions to Consider

- When a chatbot returns a synthesized answer with three citations, which information-literacy skill is actually being exercised — searching, evaluating, or verifying — and do current courses teach that one?
- Student surveys and circulation logs disagree about how much AI has displaced library use. Which would you trust to design an instruction program, and what would you measure instead?
- Information literacy instruction has historically arrived as one-shot workshops and LibGuides. If AI integration is real but informal, is the workshop format the problem or the delivery mechanism to keep?
- Frameworks such as the ACRL Framework were written before generative AI. Does AI literacy extend information literacy, or is it a separate competency that libraries are being asked to host without a mandate?

## Introduction

Information literacy is the oldest of the literacies now crowding the AI-in-education agenda, and the one with an institutional home. It was codified in library and information science — most visibly in the ACRL Framework for Information Literacy for Higher Education — as a set of dispositions around authority, process, and value rather than a checklist of database commands. What makes the AI era a distinct problem is not that searching became harder. It is that the search layer collapsed into an answer layer: a model retrieves, selects, and writes, and the reader receives a fluent paragraph whose provenance is invisible.

That shift moves the center of gravity of the construct. If the tool supplies the sources, then the literate act is judging the synthesis — checking whether a cited paper exists, whether it says what the model claims, whether an uncited claim can be traced at all. Several literatures in this knowledge base converge on that point from different directions: a bibliometric map of the field, a systematic review of AI literacy inside information-literacy instruction, and empirical studies of how students actually search. Their combined message is that the construct is being extended faster than it is being assessed.

This page treats information literacy as distinct from [[ai-literacy]], which concerns understanding and using AI systems themselves, and from [[critical-thinking]], which is the general disposition the two literacies draw on. It shares its evaluative core with [[media-literacy]], which studies messages rather than sources, and it is closely tied to [[librarians]], who teach it, and to [[libraries]], the institutions that house its instruction and much of its research.

## What the construct covers now

### Finding: the search layer is no longer the only door

Students do not choose between a search engine and a library database in the abstract; they choose among an engine, a chatbot, and a discovery system, and the choice is becoming demographic. A survey of 236 university students in the United States found search engines still dominant for academic retrieval, but generative AI a significant and rising component, with younger and international students especially likely to use it and frequent AI users reporting higher satisfaction with what it supplies ([[lund-ai-information-seeking-chatbots-libraries-2026|Lund et al., 2026]]). The same study found about one in ten respondents using AI daily for academic information seeking, while roughly one in seven never did — a spread that makes a single classroom intervention implausible.

### Evaluating: the judgment that resists automation

The evaluation strand is where the evidence is strongest and where AI changes the object of instruction. [[ai-assisted-seminar-learning-information-literacy-2026|Huang's (2026)]] platform for engineering research teams combined a recommendation engine, an embedded librarian, and seminar discussion; over eight weeks the integrated condition gained 0.78 points on an ACRL-based instrument against 0.25 for conventional library instruction among 60 students. The design detail matters more than the effect size: the librarian was a component of the system, consulted when the recommender was least confident, and source-evaluation questions dominated those consultations. Evaluation is the residue that the model cannot settle by itself.

### Teaching: workshops and guides before curriculum

A systematic review of 32 studies found AI literacy integration in information-literacy instruction at an "infancy stage." Thirty of the 32 studies appeared in 2024 or 2025 and 11 came from the United States, while the integration itself ran through LibGuides, tutorials, workshops and revised modules rather than a curriculum ([[mirza-arif-ai-literacy-information-literacy-review-2026|Mirza and Arif, 2026]]). That is a fragile base for a claim that libraries have absorbed AI into instruction. It is, however, a clear description of where the work currently lives.

### The research base: growing, uneven, mostly not about learning

A scientometric analysis of 1,669 publications from 2020 to 2025 shows information literacy as the anchor term of the field — 811 keyword occurrences, against 413 for libraries and 301 for librarians — with AI, ChatGPT and generative AI layered on top rather than replacing it ([[jabeen-lux-ai-information-literacy-scientometric-2026|Jabeen and Lux, 2026]]). The map also shows what the numbers cannot: citation visibility is not practice. The authors are explicit that rising attention does not prove that libraries have changed what they teach.

### The people asked to teach it

The instruction literature assumes a prepared instructor, and the survey evidence says otherwise. In a survey of 412 academic librarians across 38 Nigerian institutions, self-rated competence was high for database instruction (M = 4.07) and information-literacy delivery (M = 4.02). Composite AI competency was 2.86, below the midpoint, weakest on designing objectives (M = 2.70) and assessing outcomes (M = 2.62) ([[dada-librarians-information-literacy-ai-nigeria-2026|Dada, 2026]]). The deficit is at the design end of instruction, not the delivery end, which is where most professional development is aimed.

A parallel argument treats AI as a delivery channel rather than only a topic: retrieval, adaptive tutorials, chatbots and fact-checking could carry part of the load, with [[librarians|librarians]] owning the integration ([[offem-ai-information-literacy-library-users-2026|Offem and Arikpo, 2026]]). The paper is an opinion piece with no data: a design brief for where instruction might live, not evidence that it works.

## What this means for practice

- **Teach verification as a procedure, not a virtue.** The checkable skills — does the cited source exist, does it support the sentence, what is missing — are teachable in a single session and testable in an assignment. Confidence in one's own evaluation is not evidence of it.
- **Put the librarian inside the tool's workflow.** The strongest instructional result in this literature comes from a design where human consultation was built in, not offered afterwards. A chatbot with a "ask a librarian" escape hatch is an information-literacy intervention.
- **Measure use, not intention.** Self-reported AI use and behavioral records diverge sharply; a library that plans instruction from survey responses alone is planning for a student who may not exist.
- **Treat AI literacy as adjacent, not identical.** The competencies overlap, but the frameworks, the assessment instruments and the staff expertise are different. Merging them in a course title is easier than merging them in a syllabus.
