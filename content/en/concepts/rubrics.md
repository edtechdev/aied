> **Rubrics** — a stated set of criteria and performance descriptors that makes the difference between a judgment about work and a defensible reading of it. Analytic rubrics score separate dimensions independently, and holistic rubrics read a piece against an overall impression. Both carry level descriptors that turn a marker's intuition into something a student could apply to their own draft. Rubrics sit between [[assessment|assessment design]] and [[evaluative-judgment|evaluative judgment]]: they are teaching instruments as much as measurement instruments, and since [[generative-ai|generative AI]] made fluent output cheap, the question they answer has shifted from *what was produced* to *what the student contributed*.

## Questions to Consider

- If a student can produce a top-band artifact on demand, which cells of your rubric still measure the student rather than the model?
- Analytic rubrics are easier to calibrate and to feed to a model; holistic rubrics are faster and closer to how expert readers actually judge. Which does your setting need, and what does each cost?
- Who writes the rubric — the instructor, the class, or the model? What does each choice do to the student's relationship with the criteria?
- If a rubric is handed to a student before they write, does it scaffold thinking or teach compliance? What would tell you which is happening?
- An AI grades reliably against a rubric but cannot say why. What does your institution require before that output counts as a grade?

## Introduction

A rubric is usually described as a table: criteria down one axis, performance levels across the other, with a descriptor in each cell. That table is the visible part. Underneath it sits a claim — that the qualities worth rewarding in a piece of work can be named in advance, that they can be distinguished from one another, and that a reader who has learned the descriptors will read the same work in roughly the same way. [[walton-bearman-assessment-judgment-2025|Walton and Bearman (2025)]] treat that claim as the heart of [[evaluative-judgment|evaluative judgment]]: the capacity to weigh quality against standards that are themselves contested, which develops through repeated encounters with real work rather than through reading criteria.

Two designs dominate. An **analytic** rubric separates the dimensions — argument, evidence, method, presentation — and scores each on its own scale, which makes disagreement visible and diagnosable. A **holistic** rubric reads the piece as a whole and assigns one level, which is faster and often closer to how an expert forms a first impression, but which can hide a decisive weakness. The choice is not cosmetic: it determines what a student can learn from the result, and what a model can be asked to do with it.

Rubrics have always done double duty. They are a measurement instrument, part of the [[educational-measurement|measurement]] machinery on which [[assessment-validity|assessment validity]] depends, and they are a teaching instrument, an artifact students read before and after writing. That second role is where much of the recent work lives. [[mccorkle-aligned-genai-course-policy-2025|McCorkle (2025)]] treats an aligned, published rubric as part of a course policy students can reason about, and [[nerantzi-traffic-light-assessment-genai-2026|Nerantzi's traffic-light scheme]] turns the criteria into a self-check students run before submission.

## Why generative AI destabilised product rubrics

The premise a product rubric quietly assumes is that the artifact carries evidence of the person who made it. [[rubrics-genai-process-based-assessment-2026|Oviedo and Díaz (2026)]] argue that [[generative-ai|generative AI]] breaks exactly that premise: because a model can produce a fluent, well-structured result unaided, a score attached to the result no longer distinguishes the student's competence from the model's contribution. Their response is to shift the object of assessment from the product to the process, reading Biggs's 3P model so that the process phase — previously the least measured — becomes the primary thing a rubric has to capture. Three dimensions follow: human agency and critical supervision, dialogic [[scaffolding|scaffolding]] tied to [[prompt-engineering|prompt engineering]], and cognitive sovereignty grounded in [[academic-integrity|integrity]] and transparency.

That argument has been repeated, with variations, across the research literature. [[human-ai-collaboration-design-education-rubric-2026|The Merit Report]] extends [[tpack|TPACK]] with a fourth competency, AI-Validation Knowledge, on the grounds that a process-oriented rubric is only as good as the instructor's capacity to judge probabilistic output. [[lasekan-making-learning-visible-genai-assessment-2026|Lasekan (2026)]] and [[sharma-judgment-visible-genai-assessment-2026|Sharma (2026)]] both make the visibility of judgment the design problem: a rubric has to make the reasoning trail legible, not only the final claim. The common thread is that the rubric is no longer only a scoring aid but a specification of what counts as the student's own work.

## Designing rubrics for AI-visible work

Three design moves recur. The first is to grade the interaction rather than the artifact. Oviedo and Díaz's process dimensions, the Merit Report's collaboration criteria, and [[nerantzi-traffic-light-assessment-genai-2026|Nerantzi's]] traffic-light levels all describe what the student did with the tool — prompting, revising, verifying — rather than what the file looks like. The second is to make the criteria explicit enough to be contested. [[zhang-teachers-judge-creativity-genai-2026|Zhang and colleagues (2026)]] surveyed 654 teachers in China and the United States and found that product-level creativity criteria mislead unless they are paired with process evidence of what the student contributed, and that a rubric tuned to one population's weighting may not transfer to another — a warning about treating a rubric as context-free. The third is to involve students in the criteria, which connects rubrics to [[self-assessment|self-assessment]], [[feedback-literacy|feedback literacy]] and [[metacognition|metacognition]].

Some of this design work is now done with models. [[harmogen-ai-assessment-rubric-generation|HARMOGEN-R]] generates candidate rubrics with reasoning-enhanced models and compares them with a human baseline across 308 open-ended programming responses. [[deepshikha-ai-grading-rubric-prompt-engineering-2026|Deepshikha and Singh (2026)]] re-marked 40 graduate engineering laboratory reports and found that a revised rubric plus a zero-assumption prompt moved agreement markedly. The prompt quantified the error hierarchy and removed implicit inference and compensatory partial credit, which places the lever in the wording of the criteria rather than in the model's capability. The practical implication is that rubric drafting has become a [[prompt-engineering|prompt-engineering]] task: the rubric is a specification the model reads, and vague cells produce vague marking.

## Rubrics and automated scoring

Once a rubric is machine-readable, it becomes the interface between a model and a grade. [[zhao-ai-supported-grading-rubric-refinement-2026|Zhao and colleagues (2026)]] piloted an LLM grading pipeline in two computing courses that made the same binary present/absent decision on each rubric item a human grader makes, retrieving up to five previously hand-graded submissions as few-shot examples. AI–human agreement reached 89–100%, and the disagreements became an instrument for refining the rubric rather than a reason to distrust the model. [[rubric-aware-grading-rec-cbm|REC-CBM]] makes the rubric the model's internal representation, so the reasoning behind a score can be inspected — an answer to the objection that an accurate black-box grader is unusable because a teacher cannot verify it.

Reliability figures in this literature are conditional, and the condition is the rubric. [[olvet-genai-scoring-open-ended-medical-2026|Olvet and colleagues (2026)]] ran three iterations of rubric engineering at two medical schools and reached weighted kappa up to 0.94 on three of four questions, while the one holistic item stayed at moderate agreement — a result that maps neatly onto the analytic/holistic distinction. [[pecuchova-automated-grading-open-ended-genai-2026|Pecuchova, Benko and Drlik (2025)]] benchmarked eleven models on 1,885 open-ended software-engineering responses and found almost-perfect agreement only for the strongest model, with reference-based models penalising correct-but-divergent answers. [[auto-marking-short-answer-science-2026|Morley and colleagues (2026)]] found rubric-aware, chain-of-thought and domain-augmented models performed best in short-answer auto-marking, while cautioning against unmediated high-stakes use. Across these studies the pattern is consistent: the rubric, not the model, is what calibrates, which is why [[human-in-the-loop-ai|human oversight]] keeps appearing as a design requirement rather than a temporary safeguard. [[jukiewicz-chatgpt-teacher-assessment-feedback-2026|Jukiewicz and colleagues (2026)]] reach the same conclusion from the grading side: models match human raters on closed and short-answer work, but not on complex, open-ended, subjective tasks. [[ai-generated-feedback-higher-ed|Grion and colleagues (2026)]] found that an LLM given a co-constructed rubric and an exemplar was non-inferior to expert feedback, with the rubric doing the calibrating.

[[yasar-llms-iterative-pedagogical-design-2026|Iterative rubric co-refinement]] with GPT-4 raised LLM–human agreement on 80 design posters from 54.75% to 81.25%, treating the rubric as a revisable interface between pedagogical intent and machine inference rather than a fixed instrument.

## Exemplars, anchor papers and norming

Descriptors alone rarely settle a borderline piece; a worked example usually does. Exemplars — real pieces of student work at each level, with the reading that justifies the level — are the companion instrument to a rubric, and they are used in three distinct ways. As calibration artifacts, they let markers argue about quality against the same artifacts rather than against their own memory of the standard, which is the practice [[corbin-essay-time-genai-2026|Corbin and colleagues]] recommend for essay assessment: run exemplar-based norming where staff judge real work together and then have students do the same with the same exemplars. As scaffolding, they show students the range of valid forms a task can take, which [[corbin-essay-time-genai-2026|Corbin]] pairs with retiring the criteria sheet that teaches compliance. And as model inputs, they are the few-shot examples [[zhao-ai-supported-grading-rubric-refinement-2026|Zhao]] retrieves and [[cotal-formative-assessment-scoring-2026|CoTAL]] iterates on with educators. [[usher-faraon-who-grades-best-2026|Usher and Faraon (2026)]] use comparison across graders, including models, to expose what a rubric leaves open. Exemplars are not a substitute for criteria; they are the material criteria are read against.

## Common Misconceptions

- **A rubric makes grading objective.** It makes criteria explicit and disagreement visible, which is different. [[walton-bearman-assessment-judgment-2025|Walton and Bearman]] treat judgment as a capability that still has to be developed, and [[zhang-teachers-judge-creativity-genai-2026|Zhang and colleagues]] show criteria transferring badly across populations.
- **More cells make a better rubric.** A rubric with many dimensions and many levels can be slower to apply and harder to keep consistent than a small one, and it gives students more to comply with rather than more to think about.
- **A rubric can be handed to a model unchanged.** Every automated-scoring study here either engineered the rubric or refined it against disagreements; [[deepshikha-ai-grading-rubric-prompt-engineering-2026|Deepshikha and Singh]] attribute the gain to the wording of the criteria.
- **Rubrics are only for summative marking.** They are used formatively — as self-checks ([[nerantzi-traffic-light-assessment-genai-2026|Nerantzi]]), as course policy ([[mccorkle-aligned-genai-course-policy-2025|McCorkle]]) and as a way to make [[feedback|feedback]] legible.
- **AI-visible work needs a new kind of rubric from scratch.** The recurring move is narrower: keep the criteria, shift the object from product to process, and add process evidence.
