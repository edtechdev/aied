It is Thursday night in week eight. Three lesson outlines are due Monday, the lab reports are half marked, and Friday's department meeting has an item on what everyone has done with AI since the August training. A colleague says the tool halved her planning time. You tried it twice, spent forty minutes repairing a worksheet, and came away thinking the whole thing was oversold.

Both of you are describing the same tool. It saves time on drafting material you were going to edit anyway, and it costs time when you aim it at a judgment about a particular student. Whether the saving becomes yours depends on a decision most instructors never make: what the recovered hours are for.

The bottom line for the meeting: hand over the first draft and keep the judgment. Lesson outlines, worked examples, discussion questions, [[formative-assessment|formative]] quiz items, alternative explanations, rubric wording and routine administrative text are work you can specify, check and correct. Objectives, final grades and any call about a specific learner stay with you. Name what the freed hour buys, because the evidence on saving time is evidence about where the time goes.

## The short version

**1.** List the tasks from last week that produced a document you then edited. Those are your candidates.

**2.** Start with structured material you can check against your own specification: exit tickets, quiz items, worked examples, a second explanation of a hard concept.

**3.** Write down where the recovered hour goes before you recover it.

**4.** Time three recurring tasks for two weeks. Your sense of the saving is unreliable in a specific, documented way.

**5.** Keep objective-setting, [[summative-assessment|summative]] grades and individual student calls on your side of the line.

## Start with work you were going to rewrite anyway

The strongest number in this knowledge base comes from a [[rct|randomized trial]] summarized in [[ai-changing-teaching-workflows|How AI Is Changing Teaching Workflows]]. Two hundred fifty-nine science teachers using ChatGPT spent about 69% as much time on lesson preparation as the control group, a cut of roughly 31%, and blind expert reviewers found no detectable loss in quality. The teachers reallocated rather than banked it, moving the time into planning, marking and student-facing work.

A second dataset in the same work, more than 104,000 messages from over 15,000 educators, shows why the saving lands in odd places. The average prompt touches 1.7 categories at once, so a single request for a lesson plan comes back carrying differentiation and a quiz the teacher had not thought to ask for.

That is reallocation rather than reduction: what you gain from [[generative-ai|generative AI]] is the difference between a blank page and a draft specific enough to argue with.

## Match the task to what the tool does well

Quality is uneven in a pattern stable enough to plan around. Blind reviewers preferred AI versions of lesson conclusions and exit tickets 59.7% of the time over materials designed by professionals, and preferred AI material for high school content 59.2% of the time. The saving also grows where your confidence is lowest: teaching outside your own subject is the case with the widest gap between what the tool hands you and what you would have written alone.

Two categories run the other way. On elementary-level material, humans were preferred about 65% of the time for developmental appropriateness. Targeted [[scaffolding|scaffolds]] for [[multilingual-learning|multilingual]] learners and students in [[special-education]] came back neutral rather than wrong, which is worse than it sounds, since a neutral scaffold is one you rebuild from the start.

The test that predicts this before you spend an evening on it: can you state the specification precisely enough to check the output? An exit ticket on one objective, yes. Whether a paragraph suits a particular nine-year-old who reads two years behind her class, no.

## Give the recovered hour a destination

The clearest evidence that the saving is real, and that it does nothing on its own, comes from a large experiment across 178 schools and roughly 19,000 high school seniors on AI-automated essay feedback. Students whose feedback came from the system gained as much as students marked by people. The human graders cost about $0.85 an essay and added no measurable benefit on top.

In the AI classrooms, students had about 35% more one-on-one conversations with teachers about writing and wrote 30% more essays. Teachers' at-home work hours fell 20%, and the share reporting their time as "very insufficient" dropped from 23% to 9%. The largest [[learning-gains|learning gains]] landed on the most complex writing task, the kind AI is least able to evaluate, because the teacher now had hours to spend on it.

The caveat is the sentence to carry into the meeting. The bottom quartile showed no improvement. Students furthest behind gained nothing from a teacher who merely had more room in the day, so where the hours go is the whole argument, and [[equity-in-ai-education|equitable]] reallocation belongs in the plan from the start rather than in a review at the end.

## Measure the saving rather than feel it

Time saving is the claim instructors most reliably get wrong about themselves. Across three pre-registered studies with 2,691 participants, people underestimated how often they used AI and overestimated the time and effort it saved, reporting tasks as faster and easier while objective measures showed no difference. Using the tool earlier in a session predicted using it more later, so the error compounds inside a single afternoon ([[efficiency-gain-illusion-ai-overreliance]]).

What helps is [[metacognition|metacognitive]] calibration, and the cheap version is a log. For two weeks, note start and end times on three recurring tasks, one with the tool and two without. Expect one task with a large genuine saving, several with none, and a couple where the tool added a review step that ate the gain.

Effort and capability also come apart. A within-subjects study of 13 higher-education teachers found AI access cut the perceived cognitive effort of [[learning-design|learning design]] from a median of 6.33 to 3.33 (p = .004), while [[prompt-engineering|prompting]] training layered on top did not improve the quality of the designs ([[pishtari-teacher-ai-training-learning-design-2026]]). Less strain on a Thursday night is a real benefit. It is not evidence the designs improved.

## Keep objectives and final grades on your side

[[reichert-human-centered-llm-chatbot-design-teachers-2026|Participatory design work with six secondary teachers]] produced a boundary worth copying. They designed the system as a bounded expert: capable inside a narrow domain, working under supervision. They wanted help presenting content, generating practice problems and giving formative [[feedback]], and refused to hand over objective-setting or summative [[assessment]], insisting on override for ambiguous cases. What they protected is the core of [[teacher-role|the teacher's role]]: deciding what is worth learning and judging whether it was learned.

Grading is the task with the largest apparent payoff and the weakest portability. [[opraise-automated-marking-ai-assessment-2026|The OpRaise benchmark]] ran three frontier models against 761 authentic undergraduate psychology essays from 125 students at three UK universities, with 27 prompt configurations per model. Agreement with human degree bands ran from 35% to 65% depending on institution. Marks compressed toward the middle, so the strongest and weakest essays were graded least accurately, and the models agreed with each other far more than with human markers. The authors' central finding is that evidence gathered in one context does not transfer to another.

You can use [[automated-essay-scoring|automated essay scoring]] if you validate it on your own subject and students, and keep the decision with a person. [[ai-feedback-quality|AI feedback quality]] says what to check: speed and volume are not the same as usefulness, and the [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] judgment about which comment a student needs next stays with you.

## What instructors get wrong

**"The 31% is my number."** It is 259 K-12 [[science-education|science]] teachers on lesson preparation. The direction travels; the size does not. A seminar built on discussion may save less, and a lab course with safety constraints may save nothing on the part you most wanted help with.

**"Saving time is the goal."** The Brazil experiment is the counterexample. Hours came free and the bottom quartile gained nothing. The goal is what the hours buy.

**"A good draft means I can hand over the next step too."** The six teachers drew their line at objectives and summative judgment for a reason. Quality on an exit ticket says nothing about whether the tool can decide what the exit ticket should assess.

## What will come back at you

**"I tried it and the output was generic."** Most teachers in the research took the first result and edited it by hand rather than iterating with follow-up prompts. A second and third turn that supply your grade level, your reading, your last three quiz items and an example of what good looks like changes the output more than switching tools does. [[ai-literacy]] is the cheap investment, and [[prompt-engineering|prompt refinement]] is where it pays.

**"If I get faster, the time will be refilled with meetings."** Likely, and the most common way a saving disappears. Put the destination in writing where your chair can see it, before the space fills.

**"AI feedback shortchanges the students who struggle most."** The bottom quartile in Brazil gained nothing, and that is the strongest caution on this page. Pair the saving with a deliberate use. The 35% increase in one-on-one conversations is what those classrooms did with the hours, and that was a choice the teacher made.

**"Grading is where my hours actually are."** It is, and it is also where the tool is least portable across institutions. Validate locally or leave it alone.

**"This deskills the profession."** The Pishtari result is the honest version: perceived effort fell, and the added training did not improve design quality. Effort saved is not capability gained, and the six teachers' boundary is a reasonable place to hold the line while you learn which of your tasks is which.

## What to do this week

**1.** Write down the five tasks you did last week that ended in a document you then edited.

**2.** Pick the one with the clearest specification and draft it with the tool, timing both versions.

**3.** Put the recovered time into a named slot on your calendar: a conversation with a student, a second feedback pass, or reading a set of drafts properly.

**4.** Write your no-go list and keep it visible: objectives, final grades, anything that turns on one student's needs.

**5.** Keep the log for two weeks, then decide which two tasks are permanently yours to hand over.
