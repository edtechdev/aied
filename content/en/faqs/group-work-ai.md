You are writing the brief for a group assignment worth a third of the grade, and the sentence you need is the one about [[generative-ai|generative AI]]. Last term's version said "use permitted with acknowledgment," and what came back was coherent in a way that told you nothing: even marks, one voice, no record of who decided what, and a [[rubrics|rubric]] line on collaboration that every group met.

The answer is to decide AI's place per task phase rather than per course, make the group's negotiation about AI something they submit and you grade, sequence the work so it cannot be split into parallel chatbot sessions, and keep at least one component each member answers alone. All of it is task design, and none of it has been tested against a no-AI control, so you are making a design argument rather than applying a proven fix.

This page is the how: the brief, the statement, the sequence, the rubric, and the wording you can paste. [[ai-help-hurt-collaborative-learning]] is the why: what the research supports, how strong each claim is, and which pattern your group is in.

## The short version

**1.** Write the permission at the level of the task phase. Say which stages are AI-open, which are not, and why.

**2.** Require a short, jointly authored AI-use statement that records what the group decided not to do, and grade the reasoning.

**3.** Protect the stages where the group builds shared understanding: the framing, the disagreement, the reconciliation.

**4.** Choose the access configuration on purpose. One shared interface produces negotiated, visible use; private [[prompt-engineering|prompting]] produces flows you cannot see.

**5.** Keep one component each member answers for alone, and make it comprehension rather than authorship.

**6.** Read a group's restraint before you correct it. Declining AI can be deliberate [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulation]] or plain unfamiliarity, and the two call for different responses.

## Write the permission per phase, not per course

[[chen-zou-genai-group-assessment-agency-2026|Chen and Zou (2026)]] studied groups of three to five delivering a presentation worth 30% of the grade under an [[educational-policy-ai|institutional policy]] of "use only with explicit acknowledgment." GenAI was permitted for idea generation, language polishing, visual layout and scripting. Inside that single permissive policy, groups reached opposite conclusions with defensible reasoning on each side, and the task design shaped which conclusion a group reached. Fifty-two pre-service teachers in one course, with the authors asking that their patterns be read as possible responses to design rather than a distribution you should expect. A course-level rule does not determine what a group does.

So write the permission at the level of the artifact. Name the phases that are AI-open and the phases meant to be worked out without it, and tell students why the line sits there.

A version you can paste into a brief:

> This assignment has four phases. Phases 1 and 3 are AI-open: you may use generative AI for alternatives, feedback on your own draft, and language. Phases 2 and 4 are AI-closed: do them without AI and bring your notes. Wherever you use AI, the prompts go in the shared document in [folder] with the date and who ran them.

Three sentences, and each one removes a decision the group would otherwise make privately. The reason sentence is not decoration: Chen and Zou's groups reasoned their way to opposite conclusions, so the group that understands why a phase is closed is the group that can hold the line when the deadline presses.

Be honest about the warrant in the staff meeting. Neither review claims that withholding AI improves learning outcomes. The case rests on the structure of the trade-off — AI compresses the negotiation that produces shared understanding — not on a measured comparison.

## Make the negotiation a submitted artifact

Chen and Zou's central design argument is that the negotiation of acceptable GenAI use should itself become an explicit, assessable outcome. Teams should collectively justify and document how GenAI will and will not be used, rather than leaving the norm to peer pressure or perceived risk. That artifact exists only if the group actually talked, which is what makes it worth points: it converts an unenforceable rule into reviewable student judgment.

Three questions are enough, and they fit on half a page:

> 1. What did we use AI for, phase by phase?
> 2. What did we decide **not** to use it for, and why?
> 3. How did we check what it produced against another source?

Ask for the negative as well as the positive. A statement that records what the group ruled out tells you more than a list of tools, and it gives a group that disagrees somewhere to put the disagreement — usually the hard part already done. Question three is the one that distinguishes a group that used AI from a group that checked it.

Grade the reasoning, not the compliance. Two marks on the process line for a statement that names a real disagreement and resolves it beats five marks for a list of tools.

Do not assume the document reflects a shared understanding. [[genai-group-writing-strategies-2026|Korchak, Costley and Fanguy (2026)]] interviewed 10 postgraduate students in a scientific writing course where groups used GenAI through pre-planned strategies or open, individually driven interactions coordinated in shared documents. Their most pointed observation: students' accounts sometimes contradicted their own [[reflection|written reflections]], and one student denied having a group strategy while describing one in writing. A strategy that is not equally visible to every member is not reliably visible to you either.

## Sequence the work so it cannot be divided

The mechanism from the evidence page governs this one: if AI compresses negotiation, then the segments where a group builds shared understanding are the ones worth protecting, and a rubric line cannot protect them. Chen and Zou's rubric already required integration and coherence under a multicultural theme, each member's reflective insight tied to their own classroom contribution, and demonstrated group collaboration. Three groups still divided and conquered.

Change the sequence instead of the wording. A four-phase shape that resists division:

> **1. Frame (AI-closed).** The group writes the question, the audience and the success criteria in one shared document. Fifteen minutes, everyone present.
> **2. Generate alternatives (AI-open).** Members prompt separately or together, and paste every prompt and output into the shared document.
> **3. Decide (AI-closed).** The group argues the alternatives down to a position, records what it rejected and why, and names the strongest objection remaining.
> **4. Draft and defend (mixed).** Sections may be drafted with AI; the integration meeting is AI-closed, and each member explains a section they did not write.

Phase 3 is the one that pays. Chen and Zou's cooperation-oriented groups rebuilt their workflow as discussion, externalisation to GenAI, collective review and re-discussion, and that loop is what phase 3 institutionalizes. Their non-enacted groups partitioned work into discrete subtasks on individual platforms, which is what phases 2 and 4 alone would permit.

Put at least one stage where the whole team has to be in the same conversation, and make the output of that stage a deliverable — the rejected-alternatives note, the decision log, the negotiation statement. Anything not collected is optional in practice.

## Choose the interface on purpose

[[xu-genai-collaborative-space-2026|Xu et al. (2026)]] observed six groups of three working on a single shared ChatGPT-4 interface for roughly 45 minutes, and interviewed nine further students about asynchronous teamwork. With a shared, visible interface, teams co-constructed prompts, ran a recurring surface, evaluate and embed cycle, treated the chat as shared external memory, and openly negotiated AI's role. In asynchronous work, private prompting and output de-labeling produced opaque, privatized flows that raised the cost of maintaining a shared cognitive model.

The practical difference is whether the prompt is a group artifact. A shared document with the prompts and outputs in it gives you something to read and gives the group something to argue with. Private prompting gives each member a different assistant and no common record — which is a choice, whether or not you made it deliberately.

The evidence page carries the full contrast, including why the asynchronous case is worse and what a checkpoint does about it.

## Keep a part each member answers alone

Pair the group mark with an individual component: an [[oral-assessment|oral defense]] of their own understanding, or a written explanation of any section they did not draft. That is also the honest answer to the free-rider problem, because it grades comprehension rather than authorship, and comprehension is what a group mark was always a proxy for.

Three rubric lines that are no vaguer than the coherence line you already write:

> - **Individual comprehension.** Explains the reasoning of a section they did not draft, including one limitation of it.
> - **Process evidence.** Submitted AI-use statement names a rejected option and justifies the rejection.
> - **Integration.** The sections argue with each other; the group's position survives its own strongest objection.

[[llm-critical-thinking-teamwork-review|Martínez-Peláez et al. (2025)]] describe LLMs producing incomplete or incorrect responses, which pushes students to question, verify and improve the material. That exchange is what to listen for at the individual checkpoint: a student who can say what the tool got wrong and how they found out.

Detection is the wrong instrument for sorting individual contributions. None of these six studies tests detectors in group settings, and a group artifact is the worst case for one: several writers, one file, and a revision history that a shared account flattens.

## Form the groups on what members believe, not on who is friends

Whether AI becomes a shared reference point or a lookup depends partly on how much your groups already agree. In [[wang-genai-metacognitive-prompting-preconceptions-2026|Wang et al.'s (2026)]] study, fourteen pre-service [[teacher-role|teacher]] groups worked a 60-minute simulation inquiry with an on-demand [[metacognition|metacognitive]] chatbot. Groups whose members held different [[prior-knowledge|preconceptions]] routed AI use through conceptual talk; groups that already agreed ran a linear query-then-answer path and sustained negative evaluation of the output, which the authors partly attribute to prompts arriving after consensus had formed.

Two moves follow, and both are cheap:

- Run a short pretest on the core concept and mix the response patterns across groups, rather than letting students self-select into comfortable ones.
- In groups that converge quickly, keep [[self-regulated-learning|shared-regulation prompts]] on demand rather than pushed, because a nudge that arrives after agreement reads as interruption.

Treat it as a hypothesis rather than a rule. That is fourteen groups, one 60-minute task on a single topic, pre-service teachers in one country, and transcripts translated before coding.

## What instructors get wrong about this

A clear course policy settles what groups do. Chen and Zou watched groups reach opposite conclusions inside one permissive policy. The task, the rubric and the group's own reading of the risk decided it.

A collaboration criterion produces collaboration. Their rubric named integration, coherence and demonstrated collaboration, and three groups still partitioned the work and reassembled it. Criteria describe the product; sequencing produces the interaction.

Group work's real problem is free-riding, and AI makes it worse. No study here shows that AI has worsened free-riding, or that any intervention reduces it. The fairness complaint predates the tool.

More AI in group work means better collaboration. The benefits are real and the risk side is largely conceptual, but the specific harms are enough to justify protecting interaction rather than maximizing use.

A shared document means a shared process. Korchak, Costley and Fanguy found accounts that contradicted the written reflections. Look for the decision log, not the folder.

## The objections you will hear

**"I have 300 students. I cannot run oral defenses."** Then do not run 300 of them. Put the individual explanation in writing for everyone, and sample oral checks where the submitted work does not match earlier work. The point is that every member knows a conversation is possible.

**"I cannot grade process."** You are already grading a proxy for it and calling the result product quality. Grade the artifact that exists only if the process happened: the negotiated AI-use statement, the decision log, the short individual explanation. All three are bounded and checkable.

**"Isn't this a policy question for the department?"** The department can set the default. It cannot sequence your assignment. The same institutional policy produced different group behavior depending on the task, which puts the decision in your hands whether or not you wanted it there.

## Build the brief this week

**1.** Paste the phase wording into your next group brief and fill in the folder. Name two AI-closed phases and give a one-sentence reason for each.

**2.** Add the three-question AI-use statement, half a page, jointly authored, with marks on the reasoning.

**3.** Put phase 3 in the calendar as a single meeting with a deliverable: rejected alternatives, recorded in the shared document.

**4.** Add the individual comprehension line to the rubric, graded on a section the student did not draft.

**5.** Decide where the prompts live and write that into the brief. If the answer is "wherever each student keeps them," you have chosen private prompting without saying so.
