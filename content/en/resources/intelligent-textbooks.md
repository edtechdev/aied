The **Intelligent Textbooks** site is Dan McCreary's step-by-step guide to building *intelligent
textbooks* — online textbooks that go beyond static PDFs by adding search, site navigation, a glossary
of terms, a table of contents, quiz management, rendered formulas, social-media previews, link
checking, and an easy-to-visualize **concept graph** showing all the concepts in a course and their
dependencies. The guide argues that many courses today can benefit from high-quality online textbooks
with these features, and it shows how to build them using the [MkDocs](http://mkdocs.com/) build
system paired with the Material theme and generative AI to create and maintain the content.

The defining move is that the textbook is *authored in Markdown and generated by AI*, a concrete
instance of the [[vibe-coding|AI-assisted authoring]] pattern applied to [[curriculum-design|course
content]]: rather than
hand-maintaining a large site, you describe the course and let generative AI produce and maintain the
pages, with the toolchain handling navigation, search and the concept graph. A companion
[Claude Code Skills](https://dmccreary.github.io/ibook-skills/) library claims to automate over 90%
of the tasks required to build a level-2 textbook from a course description.

## A corollary resource: MicroSims

The natural companion to this guide is McCreary's **MicroSims** library at
<https://dmccreary.github.io/microsims/> (source:
[github.com/dmccreary/microsims](https://github.com/dmccreary/microsims)), which supplies the
interactive simulations an intelligent textbook embeds. A *MicroSim* (micro-simulation) is a simple
interactive simulation generated with AI to help teachers explain a concept, and it can be embedded in
an intelligent textbook or any site that accepts an `iframe`. MicroSims are distinctive for three
reasons: **AI-assisted generation** (standardized design patterns turn a natural-language description
of a simulation into a shareable asset), **universal embedding** (a single HTML `iframe` element drops
one into any page), and **transparent, modifiable code** (no black box — one click opens the sim in a
web editor, and a Creative Commons license lets most teachers use them without license fees). The term
was coined by Valerie Lockhart in 2023 after she found teachers and students could build simulations
with the p5.js JavaScript library with little or no training.

The project also publishes a JSON Schema for MicroSim metadata so AI tools can generate
searchable descriptors, with a faceted MicroSim registry on the roadmap. A research paper describing
the framework — *MicroSims: A Framework for AI-Generated, Scalable Educational Simulations with
Universal Embedding and Adaptive Learning Support* — is on arXiv
([2511.19864](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.19864)). Sample simulations include Bouncing Ball,
Projectile Motion, String Harmonics, Conway's Game of Life, Euler's Formula, and a stock-market
returns chart, each running live on the site.

## Who it is for

Instructors and instructional designers who want to build or maintain a course textbook, especially
in higher education and professional training, plus faculty developers and anyone using generative AI
to author educational content. It suits educators who are comfortable with a Markdown-and-Git workflow
(or willing to learn it) and who want the AI to handle the site plumbing — search, navigation, concept
graph — so they can focus on the pedagogy.

## Notes and caveats

This is a practitioner's guide and toolchain, not a peer-reviewed study — it presents a workflow and
its own educational rationale ("intelligent textbooks guide students through concepts on their quest
for knowledge") rather than evidence of learning outcomes. The MicroSim research paper is the closest
thing to empirical backing, and it is self-published on arXiv. Both projects are open source (the
intelligent-textbooks repo reports an MIT license and 38 stars; MicroSims reports a Creative Commons
license and 13 stars) and actively maintained. Concept graphs and glossary-first structure are
genuinely useful for discoverability, but the guide assumes a fairly technical authoring workflow —
MkDocs, Git, and generative-AI tooling — which may be a barrier for some faculty. As of ingestion the
registry for MicroSims is still only on the roadmap, so finding a specific existing simulation relies
on the site's own search and navigation.
