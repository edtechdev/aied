A free edited volume from Routledge (2027, DOI 10.4324/9781003780700, CC-BY-NC-ND) that treats generative AI as a condition assessment must work within rather than a problem it can solve. Eighteen chapters in three sections, edited from Deakin University's Center for Research in Assessment and Digital Learning, address teachers, program leaders, assessment designers and institutional policymakers.

## The argument the book makes

The framing claim, set out in Thomas Corbin's introduction, is that [[assessment|assessment]] after generative AI is a wicked problem in Rittel and Webber's sense. Competing purposes such as assurance, learning support and certification cannot be fully reconciled, and each attempt to fix one aspect reshapes another. The usual responses to uncertainty, meaning clearer policies, tighter rules and more precise designs, are therefore insufficient on their own. Variability between institutions, courses and tasks is an expected feature of the condition, not a temporary failure of coordination.

Three commitments follow. Make every design decision an explicit trade-off justified against the context, purpose and constraints of the task. Distinguish whether a task is meant to secure independence of production through invigilation, oral assessment or detection, or to judge contribution inside AI-entangled work. Treat the chapters as resources for situated judgment rather than as a model to adopt wholesale.

## Section I: conceptual and theoretical foundations

- **Assessment was already in trouble** (Boud, chapter 1). Generative AI exposed long-standing deficiencies rather than creating them: untrained assessors, unit-level organization, falsely precise grade scales and outcomes rarely assured. Move assessment from the course to the program outcome, protect fewer high-stakes tasks and build students' capacity to judge their own work.
- **Learning is collective, so assessment should be too** (Fawns, chapter 2). Students rarely learn in isolation from people or technologies, yet assessment rewards individual achievement. Design for contribution to a shared endeavor, sample work across situations rather than grading one artifact, and replace rule-and-detection integrity with dialogue about ethical practice.
- **Measurement comes before security** (Lodge, Bearman, Corbin and Dawson, chapter 3). All assessment is an inference from an observable product to unobservable learning, and AI breaks the assumption that the product is a reliable proxy. State what inference each task supports and whether AI has changed the construct before deciding format, permissions or criteria.
- **Originality is an impoverished concept** (Luo, chapter 4). Current policy treats any AI use as compromising original work, which reproduces an eighteenth-century model of solitary authorship. Redefine originality per task by asking whether AI use prevents the intended outcomes being demonstrated, and rewrite declaration language away from punitive framing.
- **Feedback is recognition, not just information** (Tai, Corbin and Flenady, chapter 5). Effective feedback confirms the social standing of learner and teacher, which a model cannot do. Reserve human interaction for recognitive moments, keep continuity with one marker across linked tasks, and offload automatable commentary rather than judgment.
- **Knowledge is hard to know** (McArthur, chapter 6). Higher education concerns contested, embodied knowledge that entails a knower, while text-matching software polices information instead. Stop presenting similarity reports as plagiarism detection, reframe ownership as responsibility for curation, and let interpretation remain the student's task even where AI use is permitted.

## Section II: empirical insights

- **Advice is itself a wicked problem** (Corbin, Bearman, Boud and Dawson, chapter 7). Workshops with eight academics showed expert proposals repeatedly reformulated into different problems, because teachers bear responsibility advisors do not share. Advise for clarity and options rather than closure, and treat workload as the binding constraint.
- **Program-wide assessment beats unit-by-unit assurance** (Nicola-Richmond and Dawson, chapter 8). A School of Health project found assuring learning in every unit creates single points of assurance failure. Across ten programs by December 2025, 18 assessment tasks had been changed to assure learning and 17 more were proposed, funded by 50 hours of project time per program in 2025.
- **Learning designers are assessment co-leaders** (Nguyen, Henderson and Heggart, chapter 9). Interviews with 11 learning designers across six Australian and New Zealand universities show their value in translational, emotional and relational labor rather than technical support. Embed them in assessment committees and protect time for codesign.
- **Students exercise agency across time** (Crawford, Bearman, Tai and Walton, chapter 10). Vignette work with 26 undergraduates shows GenAI opens immediate options for students under work, health and care pressure while narrowing longer-term enculturation. Design pacing and checkpoints rather than assuming steady engagement.
- **Originality behaves as texture** (Walton and Bearman, chapter 11). Policy demands an alchemical transmutation of AI-assisted work into something wholly the student's own. Shift from assuring the product to assuring the ways of working, which means assessors participate in unfolding work through oral defense and live revision.

## Section III: possible futures

- **Oral assessment is relational pedagogy** (Matthews, chapter 12). Sustainable assessment supplies the purpose and oral assessment the site, but only when enacted as dialogue rather than identity verification. Require argument and live interaction rather than read-aloud papers, and manage the reliability-versus-probing trade-off explicitly.
- **Humanisation is the yardstick** (Nieminen and Bearman, chapter 13). Debate centers on validity and future skills and ignores what assessment does to students as people. Allocate AI to corrective feedback, redesign self-assessment as an ongoing process, and audit security practices that suspect disabled and minoritised students disproportionately.
- **Assessment chooses which tools to include** (Swiecki, chapter 14). Tools are fundamental to acting, so permitting or banning them is always a design decision. Instrument the tools learners already use, capture prompts and revisions alongside products, and use replays for reflection rather than surveillance.
- **Aura needs a justification** (Pepperell, chapter 15). Restrictions on AI carry authority tied to provenance, and that authority holds only where friction serves learning. Justify every restriction by how learning works, tell students what it protects, and weigh the inclusion costs of mandatory real-time assessment.
- **Attribution replaces referencing compliance** (Eaton, chapter 16). Citing is a technical requirement entangled with a commodified publishing system, while attribution is an ethos of acknowledging intellectual lineage. Decouple grading from style minutiae and ask students who and what shaped their thinking.
- **Return to purpose rather than to control** (McGrath and Cerratto Pargman, chapter 17). A speculative vignette traces credential collapse by 2050 when universities cede assessment to corporate providers. Treat GenAI as emerging epistemic infrastructure and assess disciplinary knowledge, professional judgment and ethical awareness together.
- **Zoom out before you redesign** (Bearman, chapter 18). Three threads recur: learning must be more than acquiring information, doing your own work should mean responsibility rather than provenance, and scholarship helps by identifying what is mutable. If nothing can change this year, hold the purpose conversation with students and iterate next year.

## What recurs across the eighteen chapters

- **Validity first.** The most repeated move is to ask what a task infers and whether AI has changed the construct, before touching format, detection or rules.
- **Process over product.** Evidence ledgers, drafts, oral defense, replayed sessions and reflective accounts recur as ways to make work visible.
- **Friction is a design material.** Several chapters argue that struggle, error and uncertainty are what learning requires, and that removing them removes the reason for the task.
- **Relational assurance.** Interaction between teacher and student, sustained across time, is proposed as the check that detection cannot supply.
- **Equity is temporal and material.** Deadlines, caring loads, disability and access to paid models decide who can afford unassisted work.
- **Responsibility, not originality.** Attribution and curation replace purity tests as the standard for judging AI-assisted work.

## Where the book is thin

Most chapters are conceptual or theoretical, and several draw their evidence from the same small set of cited studies. Only a few report original data, and where they do the samples are modest: eight workshop participants in chapter 7, eleven interviewees in chapter 9, twenty-six students behind the vignettes in chapter 10, and the ten-program case study in chapter 8. Numbers quoted elsewhere belong to other people's research.

The volume is also strongly Australian and British in its policy references, and it argues more than it measures. Chapter 17 is deliberately speculative, chapter 3 warns that the second lane of the two-lane model is not yet understood, and chapter 8 is a single institution's implementation account. Costs, platform governance and K-12 sectors receive little attention.

## What you can do with it

Read the section that matches your role. Teachers will get the most from chapters 1, 4, 6, 11 and 16 on outcomes, originality, knowledge, working processes and attribution. Program leaders should start with chapters 3, 8 and 18 on inference, program-wide design and constraint mapping. Assessment designers and learning designers will find chapters 2, 5, 9, 12, 14 and 15 most actionable. Anyone building policy should read chapter 7 on why advice fails and chapter 17 on where surrender leads.

## Who it is for

University teachers and program leaders facing assessment redesign, [[assessment|assessment]] designers and faculty developers who advise them, learning designers working between academic and administrative domains, and institutional leaders setting policy. It suits readers who want a conceptual language for trade-offs rather than a template.

## Notes and caveats

Free to read and download under CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0, so the book can be shared with students and colleagues but not adapted or sold. Chapters are arguments, frameworks and case accounts rather than systematic reviews, so cite chapters individually. Reported figures belong to the studies the chapters cite unless the chapter says otherwise, and the editors' own empirical base is small.
