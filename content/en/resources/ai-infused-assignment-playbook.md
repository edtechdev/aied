The **AI Infused Assignment Playbook** is a practical guide from Sarah Lewis (Barry University,
social work) for instructors who want to integrate generative AI with intention rather than bolting
it onto existing assignments. It pairs a pedagogical foundation — learning goals, critical thinking,
student agency, transparency, and appropriate boundaries for AI use — with adaptable assignment
templates, design frameworks, prompts, examples and implementation guidance that instructors can use
across disciplines. The goal is explicitly not to prescribe one approach to AI but to help educators
design learning experiences in which AI use is purposeful, transparent, and aligned with what
students are expected to learn and demonstrate.

## What you can do with it

The playbook is organized around a thesis in Chapter 1 that learning is *network construction*, not
information accumulation — the [[cognitive-offloading|offloading]] question at the center of
[[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] debate — so the real question is whether AI supports or bypasses that
network-building work. Every assignment is built from five [[formative-assessment|formative]] design principles: **structured prompts, not vague
AI use** (students identify their purpose, relevant concepts, current understanding and the specific
help they seek *before* they are allowed to prompt); **critical evaluation of AI outputs** (students
document what they accept, modify and reject, and justify it); **documentation and transparency**
(prompts, AI-response excerpts, decision logs and rationales make use assessable); **metacognitive
reflection**; and **knowledge verification** via short closed-note checks.

Chapter 3 ships a fully worked sample assignment (AI-Enhanced Iterative Writing) plus sixteen
condensed template entries — AI Dialogue Reflection, AI Literature Review Assistant, AI Bias
Detection, AI Study Partner Reflection, AI-Assisted Self-Assessment, AI Debate Partner, AI Client
Simulation, AI Scenario Testing, AI-Facilitated Group Project, Peer Review with AI Comparison,
Professional AI Use Policy Development, and a Transparent AI Integration Portfolio. Appendix A
provides an AI–Human Collaboration Log for capturing the process. Faculty can take single
assignments à la carte or adopt a cluster as an AI-infused spine for a whole course.

## Who it is for

Instructors across disciplines, especially in higher education and professional programs (the
author writes from social work, and nursing/healthcare are named as parallel contexts), plus faculty
developers and those preparing accreditation evidence. It assumes students are already using AI and
that the responsible response is to bring that use into the open, structure it, and teach students
to use AI well rather than hope they avoid it.

## Notes and caveats

This is a practical guide, not an empirical study; its claims about student AI use rest on cited
external sources (BestColleges, Campbell, Digital Education Council, Flaherty) rather than new data.
The "network construction" framework and the "illusion of understanding" framing are drawn from the
wider literature (Wigmore et al.; Williamson et al.) and are presented as pedagogical rationale, not
tested outcomes of the playbook itself. The assignments are authored templates — none is reported
with effect sizes, so they should be treated as designs to adapt and evaluate locally. Published
2026-10-07, v1, on Zenodo under CC BY 4.0; free to download without an account. As of ingestion the
record shows only ~35 views and ~14 downloads, so it has seen little external use or peer review yet.
