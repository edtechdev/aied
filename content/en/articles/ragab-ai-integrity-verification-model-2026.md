> **Synthesis:** Ragab (2026) argues that [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]] now depends less on catching AI-assisted cheating than on verifying independently what a graduate can actually do. The study is a conceptual analysis built on a thematic, non-systematic review of literature on AI misuse, student motivation, online [[assessment|assessment]] risk and detection limits, and it proposes the AI Academic Integrity Verification Model: responsible [[educational-policy-ai|AI policies]], proportionate monitoring with human review, [[authentic-assessment|authentic assessment]], and secure supervised competency assessments at entry, at designated points of degree progression, at the end of each academic year and before graduation. Its case against relying on detection is the false-positive evidence — across 91 TOEFL essays by non-native English writers, seven GPT detectors averaged a 61.22% false-positive rate, 19% of essays were flagged by all seven and 97% by at least one. Ragab defines AI-assisted cheating as AI use that violates institutional policy, misrepresents independent student knowledge or effort, or conceals unauthorized assistance, distinguishing it from responsible use that supports learning without replacing required intellectual work. The model is offered for in-person and online institutions alike, and the author states that it requires empirical evaluation.

## Key Findings

1. **The model moves the question from authorship to capability.** Instead of trying to establish how every academic product was made, institutions verify what students themselves can demonstrate, which Ragab treats as the only sustainable basis for a credential.
2. **Verification is layered rather than singular.** The framework combines clear AI-use policies, proportionate monitoring, human review of any flag, authentic assessment design and independent competency verification, and technological indicators initiate human evaluation rather than determine misconduct.
3. **Supervised assessments are scheduled at transition points.** Students would complete secure competency assessments at entry where appropriate, at designated stages of degree progression, at the conclusion of each academic year and before graduation, with success a prerequisite for degree conferral.
4. **Exit examinations are necessary but explicitly not sufficient.** A single high-stakes examination cannot capture practical performance, sustained inquiry, collaboration or disciplinary judgment, so the model pairs it with oral defenses, supervised demonstrations, portfolios and performance-based assessment.
5. **Detectors produce false positives at a rate that disqualifies them as adjudicators.** Average false-positive classification of non-native English student essays was 61.22% across seven detectors, with 19% of essays flagged by all seven and 97% flagged by at least one.
6. **Behavioral analytics are indicators, not findings.** Right-clicking to reach plug-ins, copying assessment items into external tools and shifting focus away from the browser cannot independently establish dishonest intent, and unusual grade patterns justify review rather than a penalty.
7. **Misconduct is driven by incentive and normalization as much as by technology.** Grade and scholarship pressure, inadequate preparation, inconsistent policies and the perception that peers cheat without consequence all raise misuse, which is why Ragab pairs verification with policy clarity and ethics education.

## Why detection cannot carry the answer

Ragab's argument starts from a limitation rather than a scandal. Conventional plagiarism detection depends on textual overlap or recognizable linguistic patterns, and current output can resemble a student's established writing style and disciplinary language, so identification requires technical, contextual and process-based evidence rather than a binary classification. [[ai-detection|AI detection]] can therefore flag work but cannot adjudicate it — a point the paper supports with the false-positive data and with the observation that detection tools are overtaken by advances in generative systems.

The procedural answer follows directly. Institutional procedures should require corroborating evidence, transparent standards, human evaluation and an opportunity for students to explain their work, drawing on drafts, source use, writing history, oral follow-up and course-specific expectations. Ragab applies the same logic to [[remote-proctoring|online and remote assessment]], where unsupervised assignments and asynchronous schedules complicate authorship verification, and to learning-management-system analytics, where behavioral flags must be combined with human evaluation and due process. The principle is consistent: technology narrows the field of suspicion, and people decide.

## The model, layer by layer

The AI Academic Integrity Verification Model runs from coursework to degree conferral. Coursework and assignments sit under clear AI-use policies with proportionate monitoring; assignments, projects and assessments may be reviewed for patterns warranting inquiry, but a flag opens a human process rather than closing a case. Secure assessments then sample what the student can do, and standardized examination supplies the final independent check, so conferral depends on passing rather than on accumulating completed coursework. Ragab presents the framework as operating alongside established examinations such as the SAT, AP and MCAT while being designed around the outcomes expected at each level and in each field.

The author is careful about what the top layer can bear. Because a single examination cannot capture practical performance, collaboration or disciplinary judgment, exit assessment is one component in a broader system that also includes oral defenses, supervised demonstrations, portfolios and performance-based assessment. Institutions must align each method with program outcomes and evaluate its validity, accessibility, cost, security and disciplinary suitability before adopting it, which builds the [[assessment-validity|validity]] question into the implementation guidance.

## Motivation, normalization and the governance gap

The middle of the paper explains why policy alone will not hold. Students weigh grades, scholarships, admission to advanced programs and employment prospects against integrity concerns, and some feel inadequately prepared because of academic difficulties, ineffective study habits or insufficient instruction, which makes [[generative-ai|generative AI]] a convenient low-effort solution. Inconsistent policies, varying enforcement across courses and the perception that cheating is widespread weaken commitment to honesty. Peer observation does the rest: when students see peers using AI without consequences, misconduct becomes normalized, and weak enforcement can embed it in academic culture rather than marking it as a violation.

Ragab's governance conclusion is that policies must be principle-based and reviewed regularly rather than prescriptive and slow, with responsibility shared among students, faculty, administrators and technology specialists. This is where the institutional stakes appear: reputational damage from credible cheating reports can affect recruitment, faculty retention, accreditation and funding, and employers who cannot trust a transcript may substitute their own competency tests and supervised demonstrations.

## Equity and the price of verification

The equity section is the part a dean should read first. Monitoring systems should collect only what legitimate integrity purposes require, apply transparent retention and access rules, and avoid surveillance disproportionate to the assessed risk. Assessment and verification procedures should provide accommodation and should not disadvantage students because of disability, language background, technology access or location, and institutions should review outcomes for unequal effects with clear channels for correction and appeal.

Ragab extends the concern from the design of controls to the consequences of failure: inadequate institutional responses disproportionately affect students from historically disadvantaged groups who must compete for places and jobs against peers whose achievements do not reflect authentic learning. That makes verification a [[equity-in-ai-education|fairness]] measure as well as a quality measure, since the alternative to a trustworthy credential is employers and admissions offices substituting their own competency tests and supervised demonstrations. International credentials receive the same treatment — any additional competency assessment should rest on evidence-based and nondiscriminatory criteria rather than assumptions about a student's country of origin.

## What this means for practice

- **Write the permitted and prohibited uses before deploying any tool.** Ragab's sequence starts with defining permitted and prohibited AI use at program and course level, then aligning assessments with measurable outcomes and establishing misconduct-review procedures.
- **Treat every detector flag as the start of a human process.** Require corroborating evidence and an opportunity for the student to explain the work, since a 61.22% average false-positive rate makes automated penalties indefensible.
- **Stage the verification points, and pilot before scaling.** Add secure supervised assessment at entry where appropriate, at designated progression points, at the end of each year and before graduation, and make the final one a condition of conferral; Ragab's pilot indicators include student understanding of AI policies, reported violation rates, faculty workload, appeal outcomes and stakeholder confidence.
- **Do not let the exit examination stand alone.** Pair it with oral defenses, supervised demonstrations, portfolios and performance-based tasks, and evaluate each method's validity, cost and accessibility against program outcomes.
- **Review outcomes for unequal effects.** Disaggregate [[assessment|assessment]] performance and appeal outcomes and publish correction channels, because students whose language and technology access differ from the assumed norm are the most exposed to false accusation.

## Limitations

- The study is conceptual and literature-based: it reports no original survey, interview, experimental or institutional outcome data, the model has not been tested, and the review is thematic rather than governed by a formal protocol, so source selection reflects relevance to the author's themes.
- The reviewed literature spans different educational systems, disciplines and policy environments, which the author names as a limit on generalization across institutions.
- The detector evidence is borrowed, not generated: the 61.22%, 19% and 97% figures come from seven detectors applied to 91 TOEFL essays by non-native English writers, so they establish misclassification of that population rather than how often flags produce wrongful penalties.
- The paper names cost as a criterion for choosing verification methods but offers no estimate of what annual and exit supervised assessments would cost an institution or what administrative burden they would add.
- Rapid change in generative capability may date particular detection and prevention strategies, and the paper names no model version for the tools it discusses.
