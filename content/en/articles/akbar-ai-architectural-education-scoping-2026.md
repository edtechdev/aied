> **Synthesis:** Akbar and colleagues conducted a PRISMA-ScR [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|scoping review]] of AI in architectural and [[design-education|interior design education]], retaining 71 publications from 4651 Scopus records searched from January 2019 to August 2026. Nine themes cover definitions of AI, the distinctiveness of human intelligence, educational applications, efficiency, human–AI collaboration, the growing presence of AI, implementation challenges, technological limits and a human-centered framework. Empirical work in the set reports benefits for concept generation, [[visualization]], design exploration and some learning activities, while the conceptual literature stresses embodiment, contextual interpretation, ethical responsibility and oversight. The authors propose a framework connecting AI competencies with design verification, critical reflection and educator-led judgment. They repeatedly state that the framework is a conceptual synthesis, not a validated model, and that evidence for long-term effects on [[creativity]], critical thinking and professional judgment is limited.

## Key Findings
1. **The review mapped breadth rather than study quality.** It followed scoping-review guidance and did not formally appraise the quality of the included publications.
2. **Screening left a small evidence base.** From 4651 records, filters and keyword exclusions left 1549 for screening, and 71 publications were retained after title, abstract and full-text assessment.
3. **Nine themes organize the field.** The themes range from definitions of AI and the distinctiveness of human intelligence to efficiency, collaboration, challenges and a proposed human-centered framework.
4. **Reported benefits are task-specific.** Empirical studies describe gains in visualization, concept generation, design exploration and selected learning activities rather than broad changes in design competence.
5. **Creative and critical development remain under-evidenced.** The authors found limited evidence about long-term independent creativity, critical thinking and professional judgment.
6. **Studio practices are treated as irreplaceable.** Observation, drawing, model-making, material handling, site visits and face-to-face critique are described as forms of knowledge that generated images and instant answers do not supply.
7. **A human-centered framework is proposed.** It joins technological competence to verification, reflection and instructor judgment, but has not been tested in studios.

## What the review found

The Scopus search covered English-language peer-reviewed journals, conference papers and book chapters from January 2019 to August 2026. Filters and extensive keyword exclusions removed 3102 records, leaving 1549; a further 1478 were excluded during screening, leaving 71 studies for data extraction and thematic analysis. The authors note that the keyword exclusions may also have removed relevant interdisciplinary work on creativity and design pedagogy, and that review articles informed context but were not included in the final analysis.

The nine themes distinguish computational generation from human interpretation. AI is described as an evolving, interdisciplinary set of data-driven techniques, while human intelligence is characterized by consciousness, [[empathy]], intuition, [[ethics]], contextual knowledge and lived experience. In design education, the authors argue, a generated image can represent a material or spatial condition without the system having experienced it; originality and meaning depend on how designers interpret, modify and evaluate such output. Reflection and iterative experimentation are presented as the processes that turn generated content into architectural proposals. Because these distinctions are conceptual, the authors caution that they are not evidence that AI use necessarily weakens student creativity.

Empirical contributions in the corpus concern visualization, design exploration, feedback, [[learning-analytics|learning analytics]] and selected performance outcomes. The review also records efficiency and economic claims, including practice-side adoption figures that the authors explicitly say do not establish educational benefit. Challenges include bias, opacity, fabricated information, uncertain authorship, privacy, unequal access and dependence on commercial platforms. The authors argue that architecture heightens the stakes because design decisions affect safety, [[well-being]], cultural identity, environmental performance and resource distribution.

## The proposed framework and its evidence base

The review synthesizes its themes into a human-centered approach in which AI complements rather than replaces independent [[design-thinking|design thinking]]. Generative tools may broaden exploration, but students should evaluate outputs against the site, users and brief; analytical tools may compare environmental, material and technical alternatives, provided assumptions are verified; AI-supported feedback can aid interim reflection, while studio critique and final assessment continue to examine reasoning and justification. The authors propose that studio assessment document initial sketches, iterations, selected AI-generated outputs and decisions, and that oral presentations and reflective statements help distinguish individual contributions from computational assistance.

The framework connects [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]], [[computational-thinking|computational thinking]] and data awareness with observation, [[critical-thinking|critical reasoning]], ethics, empathy, cultural knowledge, material understanding, communication and reflection. In the authors' words, it is a conceptual basis for [[curriculum-design|curriculum development]] that requires empirical evaluation. The paper calls for comparative and [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal]] studio studies, using documented design processes, student reflections, [[rubrics]] and instructor evaluations, and for separate examination of generative tools versus tools for analysis, feedback and assessment.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Set design intentions through research, observation and initial exploration before allowing AI to test or refine alternatives.
- **Instructors.** Require students to justify how a proposal responds to site conditions, users and cultural context, not only how polished the render looks.
- **Assessment designers.** Ask for documented iterations and reflective statements so evaluation can consider originality, conceptual development and verification rather than final visual quality alone.
- **Curriculum designers.** Preserve observation, freehand drawing, model-making and site visits as core studio practices alongside AI competencies.
- **Administrators.** Establish transparent policies on disclosure, attribution, verification, [[privacy|data protection]] and acceptable use before scaling AI tools across studios.

## Limitations
- The review used a single database, English-language restriction, a defined publication window and extensive keyword exclusions, so relevant interdisciplinary studies may have been missed.
- Study quality was not formally assessed, and differences in methods and settings limit direct comparisons among the 71 publications.
- Several themes are conceptual or [[philosophy-of-ai-in-education|philosophical]] rather than empirical; their distinctions are not evidence of effects on learning.
- Included studies do not establish long-term effects on independent creativity, critical thinking or professional judgment.
- The proposed framework has not been empirically validated in architectural studio settings.
