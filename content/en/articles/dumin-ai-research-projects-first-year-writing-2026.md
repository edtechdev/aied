> **Synthesis:** Dumin (2026) describes a five-week group project in a first-year English composition course in which 28 students, working in groups of three or four, each took one [[llm|large language model]] — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Bing/Copilot — and applied rhetorical analysis to its output rather than to a human text. The project ran twice, six months apart, with two different cohorts, and the comparison between them is the paper's real contribution: the first group was more confident, more inclined to experiment across models and more worried about bias in the abstract, while the second group evaluated sources more systematically, was more cautious about its own skill, and was far more concerned with the editing burden and the loss of authorial voice. The author reads this as a maturing of [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] and an argument for rhetorical analysis as the bridge between literacy frameworks and an actual classroom assignment. The evidence is 28 students' reflections from one instructor's courses, with no comparison group and no measure of writing quality.

## Key Findings

- **Students are not the confident natives the narrative assumes.** Both cohorts were surprised at how easy the tools were to use, and several said they had never touched the assigned model before. One student wrote that Copilot was "very easy" and that the sources it relayed were impressive; the author treats this as evidence that assumptions about universal student adoption are wrong and that foundational instruction is needed even in a course full of first-year students.
- **Prompting was learned by doing, not by being told.** Frustration with formatting, especially the tendency to answer in bullet points, pushed students to change how they asked: one began requesting non-list answers explicitly. By the end, students described iterative follow-ups, rephrasing and multiple attempts, and several said practice rather than instruction had taught them that specific wording produces better output.
- **The two cohorts diverged more than they agreed.** Group 1 voiced broad concern about bias and misinformation and experimented across models; Group 2 tested its assigned model more systematically — a [[critical-thinking|critical evaluation]] of evidence, depth and timeliness — and raised source validity, information depth and timeliness. Group 2 also expressed more uncertainty about achieving consistent results, which the author reads as a more realistic appraisal rather than a loss of skill.
- **Authorial voice and editing time became the central worry in the second cohort.** Group 2 students described model text as stripping voice — one called it "a bland block or list of text," another said using the model for large portions of a paper "pretty obviously strips away the voice of the person writing" — and several noted that AI use could increase rather than reduce the time an assignment takes.
- **Integrity and dependence concerned both cohorts.** Students in both groups raised attribution, accidental plagiarism, adherence to instructor guidelines and the risk of [[cognitive-offloading|overdependence]] that would erode their own writing skills. The author reads this as internalized [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]] norms rather than compliance with a rule.
- **One cohort raised a concern the other did not.** Environmental costs of AI systems appeared in Group 1 reflections and were absent from Group 2 entirely, a difference the paper reports without explaining.
- **Requiring more interaction changed the assignment's shape.** In the second semester the author required at least five interactions with the assigned model and asked students to prompt for references and facts. Depth improved, but cross-model experimentation appears to have fallen, which the author names as a design tradeoff between breadth and depth of [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]].
- **Reflection surfaced the shift the instructor could not see in the output.** Students' individual reflections, not their group analyses, carried the evidence of changed thinking — the student who began thinking AI was "kind of useless and too hard to use" and ended saying "after using Claude I saw AI differently."

## How the study was done

The project was an exempt Scholarship of Teaching and Learning study (IRB #2023-108) run in 2024 in first-year writing courses at a midsized metropolitan university in the South. Students were in groups of three or four, and each group wrote a research question, prompted its assigned model, and completed a rhetorical analysis of the output. The author's metaphor for the task was that the research question is "the chip" used to get the model's reaction: the question mattered less than the interaction it produced. Groups had about five weeks and class time to work. Across the two semesters, 28 students consented to have their work included — 10 students in three groups on ChatGPT, six in three groups on Claude, three in two groups on Bing/Copilot, and nine in three groups on Gemini.

Consent was collected before the project and sealed in an envelope the instructor did not open until grades were posted, so students could opt out without affecting their grade or the classroom relationship. Data were the group rhetorical analyses and an individual post-reflection on what students learned about the models and how it might change their use of them. Analysis was a reading of the papers and reflections for patterns, organized by the four reflection questions: what was helpful or frustrating and whether that varied by program, how prompting changed, what skills were still developing, and what concerns students held about using models for writing. Only consenting students' responses were used, and they are reported by question rather than by model.

## What this means for practice

- **Start from tool mechanics, and make the analysis the assignment.** The finding that surprises most is how many first-year students had never used the assigned model. A short guided first contact, with permission to be unimpressed, costs one class session and replaces a lot of assumption. Students then produced the learning by interrogating output — its evidence, its framing, its word choices. An assignment that asks for the output instead gets a product and an integrity problem.
- **Schedule the editing stage explicitly.** The second cohort's central discovery was that using a model creates work: reconciling voice, cutting generic phrasing, checking claims. Naming that stage in the assignment, and grading it, teaches the thing students actually struggle with.
- **Choose depth or breadth on purpose.** One model per group for five weeks produces fluency and a feel for a specific system's habits; rotating models produces comparison. The paper's second-semester requirement of five interactions bought depth at the cost of breadth, and either is defensible if it is the stated goal.
- **Use the four reflection questions.** They are reusable as written and they produced the evidence the instructor could not get from the group deliverables: what helped, what frustrated, what changed in prompting, what still feels unfinished, what worries you.
- **The approach travels outside composition.** The author's example is a history class comparing the words a model uses for an event, "riot" against "massacre"; the object of analysis can be any disciplinary text the model generates.

## Limitations

- The paper states that model assignments were uneven in the first semester and rebalanced in the second, that the models themselves changed between cohorts, that each group pursued a different research question, and that the six-month gap gave the later group more exposure to AI discussion on campus.
- Each cohort also experienced a slightly different instructor approach, since the author revised the assignment between semesters, so cohort differences cannot be attributed cleanly to student development.
- The sample is 28 consenting students in one instructor's courses at one institution, analyzed by that instructor, with no second coder and no member checking.
- The data are reflections, not observations: the paper reports what students say they learned and intend to do, not what they did in later writing, and there is no measure of writing quality or transfer.
- The comparison between groups is descriptive. No statistical test, coded scheme or count of themes supports the claim that Group 2 evaluated more systematically; the differences are the author's reading of selected quotations.
- Because the assignment was required coursework, students knew their reflections were part of the course, which the author acknowledges as a risk that they wrote what the instructor hoped to read.
