> **Synthesis:** The treatment in this trial is structured use, not access. Wardat and Akour randomized Jordanian [[language-learning|EFL]] [[higher-ed|undergraduates]] across four universities into eight weeks of [[writing-education|academic writing]] instruction that either embedded [[generative-ai|ChatGPT]] in guided prompt–[[feedback|feedback]]–revision cycles or ran conventionally, with the same tasks, schedule and assessment in both conditions. The ChatGPT group gained about twelve points on a hundred-point scale; the control group gained about two. Adjusting for baseline left nearly the whole gap standing. A post-intervention model links perceived support from the tool most strongly to [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]], and less strongly to [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulation]] and [[motivation|motivation]] — associations collected after the fact, which the authors decline to read as mechanism. Twenty interviews describe how the tool entered the drafting cycle and where students set their own limits.

## Key Findings

1. **The writing advantage was large.** Eight weeks of structured use left the ChatGPT group roughly ten points higher on a hundred-point writing scale than the conventionally taught control group, though the two started level.
2. **Baseline differences cannot explain it.** The groups began within a fraction of a point of each other, and adjusting for the pre-test left nearly the whole gap standing.
3. **Both groups improved, unequally.** The ChatGPT group gained about twelve points over the eight weeks and the control group about two, so conventional instruction was not inert — it was simply much slower.
4. **Perceived support tracked critical thinking most closely.** In the post-intervention model the path to critical thinking was the strongest, followed by self-regulation, motivation and writing performance itself.
5. **Those associations are small and came after the fact.** Only the critical-thinking path reached medium strength and the rest were small, and every construct was measured once, at the end, so none of it can be read as mechanism.
6. **Interviews described a revision cycle, not a submission shortcut.** Nearly every interviewee described generating ideas and reworking drafts against feedback, and most described judging suggestions against course criteria before accepting them.
7. **Students named dependence themselves.** Nearly half raised dependence, originality or lost independent thinking, which the authors treat as a design constraint rather than an afterthought.

## An eight-week course, not a tool rollout

Three hundred undergraduates were recruited purposively from eight EFL writing classes at four Jordanian universities, two classes and one instructor per university, with proficiency at CEFR B1 or B2. After a pre-test they were allocated by computer-generated individual randomization to the experimental or control condition, 150 each, with allocation concealed in sealed opaque envelopes opened after pre-test data collection. Baseline equivalence was checked and held on gender, age group, academic year, proficiency level and pre-test writing scores.

Both conditions ran the same objectives, weekly tasks, schedule and assessment for eight weeks; the experimental group added one structured ChatGPT-4 session per week under instructor supervision, covering idea generation, planning, drafting, language refinement, feedback evaluation and revision. Instructors had attended a training workshop, and treatment fidelity rested on a shared intervention manual, prompt guidelines and assessment procedure. Essays were scored on four IELTS criteria by two trained raters blind to condition and occasion, with high inter-rater reliability (ICC = 0.917); scores were rescaled to 0–100. Because the intervention bundled tool use with instructor guidance, prompt training, critical evaluation and reflection, the authors attribute the writing difference to the package rather than to ChatGPT alone.

## What the interviews add to the model

Twenty experimental participants were interviewed for 30–45 minutes, stratified by post-test performance (seven low, seven medium, six high), and four 90-minute classroom observations were scheduled in weeks 2, 4, 6 and 8. Six themes emerged, and their distribution is the useful part: 19 of 20 reported using the tool to generate ideas and organize arguments, 18 of 20 valued immediate [[feedback|feedback]] on drafts, 17 of 20 described comparing suggestions against course materials and assignment criteria before accepting them, 15 of 20 described planning and monitoring their own process, and 17 of 20 reported gains in confidence.

Observations converged: students revised drafts repeatedly within a session, rewrote prompts when responses were unsatisfactory, and discussed suggestions with classmates and the instructor. The sixth theme cuts the other way. Nine of 20 worried that dependence would reduce originality and independent thinking, with one participant quoted as saying the tool "should help students think, not think for them" — the concern that makes [[cognitive-offloading|over-reliance]] a design problem rather than a side effect.

## Which claims the design can carry

Two claims live here and they have different warrants. The [[mixed-methods-research|explanatory sequential design]] keeps them apart. The [[rct|randomized comparison]] supports a causal statement about writing scores under these conditions. The structural model does not: critical thinking, self-regulated learning and academic motivation were each measured once, after the intervention, by five [[self-report-measures|self-report]] items, so the paths describe which students reported what at a single moment, and the authors repeatedly refuse the causal reading — indirect effects are labeled "model-based associations."

The construct measuring exposure is also perceptual rather than behavioral. ChatGPT-assisted learning was operationalized as perceived educational usefulness, administered to both groups; control students answered from general impressions while experimental students answered from eight weeks of direct use. A measurement-invariance check did not reject compositional invariance for that construct but was borderline for academic motivation, and the paper concedes that compositional invariance is not equivalence of response processes. Predictive performance beat an indicator-average benchmark but not a linear-model benchmark.

The reading these results support is [[constructivist|social-constructivist]] and [[sociocultural-learning|sociocultural]]: the tool acts as [[scaffolding|scaffolding]] inside an instructor-led cycle rather than as a text generator. The trial tests that package; it does not isolate the model.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors:** Keep the tool inside the writing cycle rather than at its edges. The reported uses were brainstorming, draft feedback, evaluation and revision, and each ran under instructor supervision in the experimental condition.
- **Curriculum designers:** Build the evaluation step into the assignment. Most interviewees described judging suggestions against criteria, which is the behavior that separated use from delegation here, and it needs a task structure — [[process-oriented-assessment|process evidence]] such as drafts, revision notes and an account of how AI was used — that rewards it.
- **Administrators:** Pair any rollout with [[academic-integrity|integrity]] and authorship guidance, since students raised dependence and originality unprompted and the intervention made student authorship of ideas and final text explicit.
- **Researchers:** Report the experimental and associational strands separately, as this paper does. A post-only self-report mediator cannot carry a mediation claim, whatever the path coefficients show.

## Limitations

- The trial ran eight weeks with no delayed post-test, so persistence of the writing advantage is unknown, and the authors say so directly.
- Critical thinking, self-regulated learning and academic motivation were measured only after the intervention by self-report; self-regulation's average variance extracted fell marginally below the conventional threshold, and the construct was retained on theoretical grounds.
- Randomization was at the individual level inside eight intact classes taught by four instructors, and the ANCOVA modeled no university, class or instructor clustering, so standard errors may be sensitive to unmodeled dependence.
- The intervention combined ChatGPT with instructor guidance, prompt training, critical evaluation, revision and reflection, so the effect cannot be attributed to the tool alone.
- The ChatGPT-assistance items went to both groups although only the experimental group had used the tool in the intervention, leaving cross-group comparability of that construct unresolved.
- The [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] strand drew 20 interviewees from the experimental group only and was coded by a single researcher, with peer debriefing but no second coder.
