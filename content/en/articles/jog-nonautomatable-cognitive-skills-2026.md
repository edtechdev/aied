> **Synthesis:** Jog (2026) reviews what should remain with the learner when generative AI can produce competent text. Higher-order critical thinking is the most frequently cited non-automatable skill, alongside authorial decision-making, contextual disciplinary judgment, ethical and epistemic agency, and metacognitive capacities such as self-regulation and reflective thinking. The review defines non-automatable not as what a model cannot perform at all, but as thinking that loses its educational value when handed over: work that carries accountability, depends on context and requires second-order self-regulation. Reported effects of generative AI are conditional. Unscaffolded use is associated with weaker source evaluation, argument depth and independent judgment, while tasks that require students to critique, revise and justify AI output are associated with maintained or improved reasoning. The review recommends integrating AI literacy into the curriculum and shifting assessment toward evidence of process.

## Key Findings
1. **"Non-automatable" is a claim about educational value.** The term does not mean AI cannot perform the surface task; it means delegating the thinking removes what the task is meant to teach.
2. **Higher-order critical thinking appears most often.** Evaluation, synthesis and informed judgment recur across business, economics, marketing and language-related disciplines.
3. **Other capacities cluster around it.** Authorial decision-making in academic writing, contextual disciplinary judgment, ethical and epistemic agency, and metacognitive skills such as self-regulation and reflective thinking are repeatedly named.
4. **Three features explain resistance to automation.** Accountability, dependence on situated knowledge, and second-order self-regulation: only the thinker can monitor and revise their own reasoning.
5. **Effects depend on task structure, not merely access.** Unscaffolded use was linked to poorer source evaluation and weaker argument; scaffolded critique, revision and justification were linked to retained or improved higher-order reasoning.
6. **Process evidence is the assessment response.** Iterative drafts, prompt logs, oral defenses, rubric-based critiques of AI output and case-based tasks are recommended in place of product-only assessment.
7. **Curriculum should integrate AI literacy.** It is framed as a higher-order competency combining critical evaluation, ethical reasoning and metacognitive awareness, with Bloom-style frameworks revised so the higher levels name what generative AI cannot do.

## What the review included
The narrative review searched Scopus, ERIC, Semantic Scholar and Google Scholar for literature "published between January 2020 and December 2025". The search was iterative and purposive rather than an exhaustive systematic protocol. Inclusion required a higher education setting, explicit attention to generative AI in teaching, learning or educational practice, and a focus on cognitive or metacognitive skills described as non-automatable or minimally automatable. The eligible pool contained forty studies; full texts were retrieved for twenty-four, while the remaining sixteen were excluded because complete versions were unavailable through open-access channels or the records held only bibliographic metadata.

The twenty-four included studies comprise thirteen secondary syntheses, seven empirical primary studies and four conceptual or position pieces. They span Asia, Europe, North America, Australia and the Philippines, and disciplines including business, economics, language, psychology, physiotherapy, medicine and research training. Each full text was read and analyzed thematically against three guiding questions: which capabilities are identified as non-automatable, what makes them resistant, and what follows for educational redesign.

## What makes a skill non-automatable
The literature uses the term in three distinguishable senses. The first is task complexity: generative AI competence degrades at higher levels of Bloom's taxonomy, so evaluation and creation are difficult to automate. The second is regulation: even when AI produces a competent output, only the learner can monitor, justify and revise their own reasoning. The third is normative: capacities such as authorship, judgment under uncertainty and professional accountability should not be delegated because delegation destroys their value as evidence of a person's competence. The review merges these into the "accountable learner-in-context", defining non-automatable cognition by who is accountable for, regulates and authorizes the work rather than by what AI cannot produce.

In academic writing, the important question is whether the student selected, evaluated and revised ideas for defensible reasons, not whether the text reads well. In professional education, an answer must be judged against a particular case rather than accepted because it appears plausible. The review cites an undergraduate psychology study in which some students asked to compare AI-generated text with a published study still failed to spot inaccuracies, showing that merely requesting comparison does not guarantee scrutiny. Prompt engineering is valued because it externalizes disciplinary reasoning, not because prompt writing is itself the transferable skill.

At assessment level, recurring design features are authentic tasks, open-ended problems, visibility of process and built-in self-assessment. One cited approach tests generative AI directly on the existing assessment, identifies where it succeeds and redesigns around the human cognition still required. In medical education this is illustrated by objective structured clinical examinations, portfolios, reflective writing and case-based discussions. The overall stance is constructive rather than restrictive: build tasks that require the cognition the degree is meant to certify, instead of building barriers around tool access.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Require prompt logs or a short account of how a response was checked and revised, so the iterative work of framing and interpreting queries becomes assessable.
- **Instructors.** Design activities in which students must critique, revise and justify AI output; the review associates those conditions with preserved or improved higher-order reasoning.
- **Assessment designers.** Test the existing assessment with generative AI, identify where it succeeds, and rebuild the task around the human judgment it cannot supply, using authentic, open-ended, process-visible formats.
- **Curriculum designers.** Embed AI literacy across the curriculum as critical evaluation, ethical reasoning and awareness of dependence, rather than offering a standalone tool tutorial.
- **Researchers.** Address the review's own call for longitudinal tracking, larger comparisons of traditional and AI-augmented instruction, and cross-disciplinary studies of retention and transfer.

## Limitations
- The search was purposive and narrative, not an exhaustive systematic protocol; no formal risk-of-bias assessment or quantitative synthesis is reported.
- Sixteen of the forty eligible studies could not be retrieved, so the synthesis rests on twenty-four full texts and may omit relevant work.
- Several included studies are preliminary or exploratory, with small samples, short interventions, short-term observations and self-reported data.
- Few studies examined long-term retention or transfer after AI exposure, and the evidence is concentrated in particular regions and disciplines, limiting generalizability.
- Measures of critical thinking vary across studies, making comparisons difficult; the authors describe the synthesis as provisional and unable to settle edge cases such as whether generative AI can teach values or creativity.
