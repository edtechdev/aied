> **Synthesis:** Chen, Yan and Zheng followed 12 Chinese Grade 7 students through a six-week inquiry project on the collapse of the Qin Dynasty, using Doubao alongside [[teacher-role|teacher]]-provided historical sources. Classroom observation, post-project interviews and retained [[student-ai-interaction|AI interaction]] logs were interpreted through an [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] framework adapted to history: [[prompt-engineering|prompting]] competence, information credibility, bias verification, and [[ethics]] and safety. Students used [[prior-knowledge|prior knowledge]] to formulate and refine questions, checked claims against textbooks and source excerpts, questioned whose perspectives were represented, and decided what to include in their explanations. The authors find that most students kept final interpretive authority for themselves, while one attributed greater authority to the system. Because participation was voluntary and prior historical knowledge was not independently measured, the practices describe this guided project rather than Grade 7 students generally.

## Key Findings
1. **Twelve students completed the project.** Fifteen initially volunteered; three left before the final interview or for scheduling and activity conflicts.
2. **Three data sources were triangulated.** Observations, retrospective interviews and chronological AI interaction logs were interpreted together rather than treated as interchangeable records.
3. **Historical questions drove AI use.** Students drew on prior knowledge, refined broad prompts and followed up on unfamiliar factors introduced by the [[conversational-ai|chatbot]].
4. **Evidence checking was visible.** Students checked claims against textbooks and source excerpts, and one challenged a factual claim using the Shiji material until the system corrected it.
5. **Perspective mattered as well as accuracy.** Students asked whether people from the former six states viewed unification differently and whether competing interpretations were represented fairly.
6. **Students set boundaries on assistance.** Several kept only supportable claims and asked for a framework rather than having the chatbot organize the whole explanation.
7. **Authority was not uniform.** Most students described themselves as final decision-makers, while one said the system was smarter and could produce a better explanation than the student.

## What the study measured

The case was one public junior [[secondary-education|secondary school]] in Chengdu, one optional six-week Grade 7 project on the rapid collapse of the Qin Dynasty, one teacher-led sequence and students' use of Doubao. Fifteen students volunteered, and 12 completed the project and interviews: seven female and five male students, with prior AI experience described as none, limited or regular. Data collection began on 11 November 2025, the project ran between November and December 2025, and individual interviews followed its completion.

Twelve sessions were observed, each about 60 minutes, giving roughly 12 hours of observation; all three authors took non-participant notes and consolidated them after each session. Interviews were conducted in Chinese, lasted approximately 60 minutes and comprised 12 core questions in six domains. The retained corpus contained 624 interaction turns, defined as a student prompt and its corresponding AI response, organized into 156 interaction sequences across the 12 participants. Turns per participant ranged from 32 to 78, with a median of 50; 92 sequences could be linked to identifiable classroom episodes.

Inductive coding of observations and transcripts produced 26 initial codes, then 10 preliminary codes and finally eight analytical codes grouped into three themes. Follow-up prompts were separately coded as clarifying, extending, challenging or redirecting. The adapted framework had four dimensions, and the authors stress that the themes and dimensions overlap rather than mapping one to one. In particular, AI bias verification refers here to students' attention to omitted or one-sided historical perspectives, not to their understanding of [[bias-mitigation|algorithmic bias]] in the model.

## What the themes showed

Students often began from what they already knew, as when S4 asked about causes of the Qin collapse other than tyranny, and then narrowed broad answers into more focused causal questions. Others pursued a factor introduced by Doubao, such as political conflicts after Qin Shi Huang's death, and returned to teacher-provided materials to continue the inquiry. The logs show both iterative refinement and extension beyond an initial question; the authors read these practices as prompting competence and inquiry extension within a historical discipline.

Evaluation involved both evidential checking and representational judgment. S1 described checking a claim about heavy taxation against the textbook and historical sources before using it. In an observed episode, S5 moved repeatedly between a Shuihudi Qin Bamboo Slips excerpt and Doubao's explanation of strict laws. In a logged exchange, S3 cited the Shiji account of Fusu and Huhai against a chatbot claim, after which Doubao corrected its response. Other students asked whether people in the former six states would have viewed unification as positively as the AI account suggested, and challenged a claim that historians generally agreed about harsh rule as the main cause. These episodes concerned the content and framing of historical accounts.

Strategic engagement centered on deciding what AI should contribute. S7 described keeping only claims supported by evidence and revising or omitting the rest, and an observed group linked an AI suggestion about administration to the Liye Qin Bamboo Slips and an article about expanded territory before revising its explanation. S12 asked Doubao to explain the connection between selected factors without adding new causes, and S8 declined an offer to organize reasons, requesting only a framework. The contrasting account from S9, who said Doubao was smarter and could produce a better explanation, shows that the authors' claim about student authority is a majority pattern, not a universal one.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Build inquiry tasks in which AI-generated claims must be checked against named sources before they enter a student's explanation.
- **Instructors.** Ask students whose perspectives a generated historical account includes and omits, not only whether its facts are correct.
- **Instructors.** Make students decide what assistance they want from a chatbot, including when to restrict it to a narrow supporting role.
- **Curriculum designers.** Adapt AI literacy frameworks to the evidential and interpretive demands of the discipline rather than teaching generic checklists alone.
- **Researchers.** Examine how the same student shifts between challenge and acceptance across interactions, since engagement with AI was not consistent.

## Limitations
- The optional after-school project used a small, self-selected sample of 12 students at one school, limiting transfer to other Grade 7 learners.
- Prior historical knowledge was not independently measured, so the study cannot show how much subject knowledge supported students' evaluations.
- Interview accounts were retrospective and subject to recall bias, although prompt wording and sequence were checked against logs where possible.
- Logs did not show whether students consulted physical sources or incorporated AI content into final explanations without triangulation.
- Students' questions about missing perspectives did not demonstrate understanding of algorithmic bias, and the online/offline arrangement made cross-environment links hard to capture.
