> **Synthesis:** Baş and colleagues surveyed 378 pre-service teachers at four Turkish state [[higher-ed|universities]] to ask whether [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] and ecological footprint awareness move together, and which parts of AI literacy carry any relationship. Both constructs were reported at high overall levels, although awareness of food and transportation and housing impacts was only moderate. Overall AI literacy showed a moderate positive association with ecological awareness, but the pattern was uneven: ethical and evaluative competencies were strongly linked to awareness while technical tool use was not. The authors interpret the result through an ethical-reflective competency bridge and argue that [[teacher-education|teacher education]] should connect digital and sustainability preparation instead of treating them as separate domains. The design is cross-sectional and correlational, so the finding describes association rather than cause.

## Key Findings

1. **Both competencies were reported at high overall levels.** Pre-service teachers described themselves as highly AI-literate and highly aware of their ecological footprint.
2. **Ecological awareness was uneven across domains.** Energy, water, and waste awareness sat in the high band, while food and transportation and housing sat in the moderate band.
3. **Overall AI literacy and ecological awareness were moderately associated.** The relationship was positive and statistically reliable within the participating sample.
4. **[[ethics]] carried the strongest link.** The ethical dimension of AI literacy showed the clearest association with ecological awareness, with evaluation next.
5. **Technical use was not significantly associated.** The dimension concerned with operating AI tools showed only a weak, non-significant relationship with overall ecological awareness.
6. **The ethics pattern survived a conservative correction.** Every ethics-by-ecological-domain association remained significant under Bonferroni correction; none of the use associations did.
7. **Gender differences were domain-specific.** Male participants reported higher technical AI confidence, while female participants reported higher awareness in food and transportation and housing.

## What the study measured

The study used a cross-sectional correlational survey with 378 pre-service teachers enrolled in faculties of education at four Turkish state universities. Sampling combined criterion and convenience procedures and was not probability-based. AI literacy was measured with a 12-item scale adapted into Turkish, covering Awareness, Use, Evaluation, and Ethics; ecological footprint awareness was measured with a 46-item scale covering Energy, Waste, Food, Water Consumption, and Transportation and Housing. Both constructs were self-reported on a single questionnaire. In the present sample, the AI literacy scale had a Cronbach's alpha of .75 and the ecological footprint scale .92.

The authors report an a priori power analysis for a moderate correlation (r = .30) with a two-tailed α of .05 and target power of .95, giving a minimum required sample of approximately n = 139. The achieved N = 378 yielded power greater than .99 for that effect and approximately .49 for a small effect (r = .10). Harman's single-factor test across all 58 substantive items found that the first unrotated factor accounted for 20.52% of the variance, below the 50% threshold used to flag [[self-report-measures|common method bias]].

On the seven-point AI literacy scale, the overall mean was M = 5.34 (SD = 0.70), inside the High band. Evaluation was highest (M = 5.46, SD = 0.94), followed by Use (M = 5.34, SD = 0.95), Ethics (M = 5.29, SD = 1.17), and Awareness (M = 5.26, SD = 0.88). On the five-point ecological scale, the overall mean was M = 3.57 (SD = 0.46). Energy (M = 3.91), Water Consumption (M = 3.88), and Waste (M = 3.74) fell in the High band, while Transportation and Housing (M = 3.10) and Food (M = 3.08) fell in the Moderate band.

## Which dimensions carried the relationship

Total AI literacy correlated with total ecological footprint awareness at r(376) = .350, p < .001, with a 95% confidence interval of [.258, .435]. At the sub-dimension level, Ethics was strongest (r = .443, p < .001), followed by Evaluation (r = .294, p < .001) and Awareness (r = .108, p = .037). Use was not significant (r = .098, p = .057). Looking across ecological domains, the strongest associations were with Energy (r = .361, p < .001) and Waste (r = .326, p < .001), and the weakest with Food (r = .168, p = .001) and Transportation and Housing (r = .148, p = .004).

Because the analysis reported thirty simultaneous correlations, the authors applied a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of α = .0017. Ethics was the only AI literacy sub-dimension whose correlations with all five ecological domains survived that correction; none of the Use correlations did. The authors treat this asymmetry as consistent with their ethical-reflective competency bridge, while stating that mediation and cross-domain transfer were not directly tested.

Demographic contrasts showed a similar dissociation. Male participants scored higher on overall AI literacy (t(376) = −2.392, p = .017, d = 0.29) and on Awareness, Use, and Evaluation, with no significant gender difference in Ethics (p = .157). Female participants scored higher on Food Awareness (t = 2.754, p = .006, d = 0.34) and Transportation and Housing (t = 2.085, p = .038, d = 0.25).

## What this means for practice

- **Teacher educators.** Build courses that connect ethical reasoning about AI with environmental awareness, since the ethical and evaluative sub-dimensions carried the observed association rather than tool use.
- **Instructors.** Make the environmental consequences of food systems and mobility more visible through [[project-based-learning|project-based]] or inquiry-oriented activities, the two domains where this sample's awareness was only moderate.
- **Curriculum designers.** Avoid treating digital competence and [[environmental-education|sustainability education]] as wholly separate curricular areas; the authors argue for connecting them within teacher preparation.
- **Instructors.** Extend [[ai-education|AI education]] beyond tool-oriented training to include critical, ethical, and societal implications, given that the Use sub-dimension was not significantly associated with ecological awareness.
- **Researchers.** Combine self-report scales with performance-based assessments or behavioral indicators in future work, since the present measures capture perceived competence rather than demonstrated skill.

## Limitations

- The cross-sectional correlational design cannot establish direction or mechanism, so the proposed competency bridge remains a theoretical interpretation rather than a tested causal path.
- Both constructs were measured by self-report; Harman's test reduces but does not eliminate concern about common method bias.
- The sample came from four Turkish state universities through non-probability sampling and is not representative of pre-service teachers in Türkiye.
- Sub-dimension reliability coefficients for the ecological footprint scale were taken from the original development study rather than computed for this sample.
- The study was only moderately powered (approximately .49) to detect an effect as small as the one observed for the Use sub-dimension.
