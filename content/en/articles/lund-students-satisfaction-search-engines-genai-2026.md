> **Synthesis:** Lund, Warren, and Teel (2026) survey 236 U.S. [[higher-ed|university]] students about their satisfaction with [[generative-ai|generative AI tools]] and with traditional search engines as academic information sources. Satisfaction with search engines was clearly higher (mean 3.12 on a 0–4 scale) than with AI (mean 2.11), and the two were negatively correlated, a weak negative relationship. K-means clustering separated a group heavily satisfied with search engines but dissatisfied with AI (41% of respondents) from a group moderately to highly satisfied with both (59%). Frequency of use predicted satisfaction, most strongly for AI, with a strong relationship between monthly use and AI satisfaction, and international and undergraduate students reported significantly higher AI satisfaction than domestic and [[graduate-education|graduate]] students. The authors read this as evidence that AI tools are settling into a complementary role in students' [[information-and-media-literacy|information seeking]] rather than replacing search engines.

*Electronic survey distributed through Qualtrics to students at U.S. universities between August and September 2025, with 236 valid responses. Principal components analysis of ten satisfaction items, k-means cluster analysis, and multiple regression on demographic predictors and prior-month use frequency.*

## Key Findings

- **Search engines beat AI on satisfaction.** Mean satisfaction was 3.12 (SD 0.80, median 3.25) for search engines against 2.11 (SD 1.00, median 2.17) for AI on a 0–4 scale — "often satisfied" versus "sometimes satisfied".
- **The two satisfactions move in opposite directions.** Students who were more satisfied with search engines tended to be less satisfied with AI, a weak negative relationship that was statistically reliable.
- **Use frequency tracks satisfaction, especially for AI.** Monthly AI use showed a strong positive relationship with AI satisfaction, and monthly search-engine use showed a moderate positive relationship with search-engine satisfaction; both relationships were statistically reliable.
- **Two student groups emerged.** Cluster 1 (41% of cases) reported low AI satisfaction (mean 1.08) and high search-engine satisfaction (3.48); cluster 2 (59%) reported moderate-to-high satisfaction with both (AI 2.82, search engines 2.88).
- **The dissatisfied-AI cluster skews domestic and graduate.** It contained 60.4% of domestic students but 17.6% of international students, and 64.7% of graduate students but 23.2% of undergraduates.
- **Satisfaction loaded as single constructs.** Principal components analysis retained one component each for search engines (eigenvalue 2.84, 52.66% of variance) and AI (eigenvalue 3.41, 68.27%), with loadings from .622 to .855.

## What satisfaction looks like across two sources

The study treats satisfaction as a measurable outcome of [[information-and-media-literacy|information seeking]] rather than a vague attitude. Ten items asked how satisfied students were with each source's ability to supply five kinds of information — job-related, coursework-related, studying topics, weather, and news. The components analysis found one dimension behind each set, meaning students answered about a source as a whole rather than distinguishing sharply among information types. That is itself informative: whatever drives satisfaction operates across contexts instead of being tied to one task.

Search engines came out ahead by a full point on the scale, a difference the authors call notable because it separates "often satisfied" from "sometimes satisfied." The negative correlation between the two satisfactions suggests a mild either/or dynamic: students who rate one source highly tend to rate the other lower. That could reflect differing expectations, or it could reflect that the two tools compete for the same tasks in students' workflows.

## Who is satisfied, and what that means

The cluster analysis turns the averages into populations. The larger group, 59% of respondents, is comfortable with both sources and reports moderate-to-high satisfaction with each. The smaller group, 41%, is heavily reliant on search engines and dissatisfied with AI. Composition matters: the second group contains most of the domestic students and most of the graduate students, while the balanced group contains 82.4% of international students and 76.8% of undergraduates. Whatever explains dissatisfaction with AI, it is not evenly distributed across the student body.

Frequency of use is the strongest predictor the study reports, and it works differently for the two sources. Using AI more often predicts substantially higher AI satisfaction (.58), whereas using search engines more often predicts only moderately higher search-engine satisfaction (.33). One reading is that AI satisfaction is still forming and is shaped by accumulated experience, while search-engine satisfaction is comparatively stable. For [[libraries|library and information services]], that suggests a window in which guided exposure could change how students judge these tools.

## Limits of the instrument

The survey is a convenience sample of 236 students, drawn largely from the natural and computing sciences and from graduate programs, so it over-represents technically inclined respondents and cannot describe all U.S. students. All measures are self-reports of satisfaction, not observations of search behavior or judgments of result quality. The cross-sectional design cannot establish whether use drives satisfaction or satisfied students simply use a tool more. And satisfaction is not the same as effectiveness: a source can be satisfying without being accurate, which is precisely the concern that [[ai-literacy]] instruction exists to address.

## What this means for practice

- **[[librarians]].** Treat satisfaction as a diagnostic worth measuring alongside use. Students who dislike AI may simply lack repeated, structured exposure, so offer guided practice rather than assuming settled preference.
- **Instructors.** Do not assume students prefer AI for every information task. Many still rate search engines higher, and the two groups in this study differ systematically by level and origin.
- **Instructional designers.** Build instruction that compares sources explicitly on accuracy and appropriateness, since satisfaction alone does not track quality.
- **Researchers.** Move beyond adoption counts to observed search behavior and outcome quality, and test whether satisfaction changes with sustained, supported use.

## Limitations

- The sample is a convenience sample of 236 students, heavily weighted toward natural and computing sciences and graduate students, so it is not representative of U.S. university students generally.
- All data are [[self-report-measures|self-reported]] satisfaction, which may diverge from actual search performance or from judgments of source quality.
- The cross-sectional design cannot establish the direction of the use–satisfaction relationship.
- The survey predates the most recent generation of AI tools, and satisfaction with a fast-moving technology may not remain stable.
- Satisfaction was measured on five information types that include non-academic categories such as weather and news, which may dilute the academic reading of the construct.
- Cluster membership is sensitive to the number of clusters chosen, and the two-cluster solution, while theoretically coherent, is one of several possible partitions.
