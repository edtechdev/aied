> **Synthesis:** Lewin and Farraj (2026) examine how first-year English majors in a selective Israeli program reasoned about [[generative-ai|generative AI]] tools while learning literary analysis under a rule-structured policy: one take-home essay permitted bounded tool use, while every other assessed writing task did not. Combining pre- and post-course interviews with a descriptive reading of graded papers and [[feedback|instructor feedback]], and using second-generation [[activity-theory-aied|activity theory]] as a sensitizing vocabulary rather than a causal model, the authors surface six themes. Students framed the tools as both mentor and crutch, justified acceptable use as bounded, inspectable and limited to micro-tasks such as lexical refinement or transitions, and treated interpretive claim-making, warranted analysis and writerly voice as non-delegable. The rule-structured contrast sharpened their awareness of effort, responsibility and what they called the value of struggle. Because no tool logs were collected, the study describes narrated boundaries; it establishes no effect of AI on writing quality.

## Key Findings

- **Students drew a line between help and cheating.** The same participants who described [[generative-ai|generative AI]] as a personal mentor also reported guilt and the feeling that assistance "feels like cheating," even when the course authorized it; permissibility was negotiated task by task rather than accepted wholesale.
- **Acceptable use stayed bounded and micro-task level.** Students reserved the tools for synonyms, transitions, spelling checks, bullet-point feedback and quick verification, and kept conceptual development, interpretation and evaluation for themselves.
- **They read AI feedback as surface-level.** Post-course accounts called tool feedback "on a superficial level" and "way too basic," focused on grammar and syntax rather than the analytic depth the assignment demanded.
- **Course feedback never stopped targeting interpretation.** Across Papers 1-3, instructor and teaching-assistant comments repeatedly pressed thesis specificity, evidential warranting and coherence, including on the AI-permitted paper.
- **Some students reframed struggle as constitutive of learning.** Accounts shifted from product-oriented goals toward mastery, ownership and what participants called the value of struggle, echoing [[productive-failure|productive failure]]; others stayed committed to efficiency.
- **The artifacts triangulate the interviews only descriptively.** Surface and conventional features looked more polished on the permitted paper while epistemic feedback persisted, but without tool logs the design cannot attribute any textual feature or grade difference to AI.

## A rule-structured course as the research site

The setting was a required first-year academic-writing course in a selective English-language and literature program in northern Israel, taught in two sections of approximately 20 students each. Eighteen first-year students, 45% of those enrolled across the two sections, consented to participate; their repertoires spanned Arabic, Hebrew and Russian, so the study sits at the intersection of [[writing-education|academic writing]] and [[multilingual-learning|multilingual learning]]. The course centered on close reading and literary interpretation, peer workshops, instructor and teaching-assistant feedback, and multiple drafts of three papers. GAIT use was expressly permitted for Paper 2, a take-home poetry-analysis essay, following instructional sessions on December 24 and 26, 2024 that introduced Microsoft 365 Copilot and Google Gemini 1.0. Papers 1 and 3 were GAIT-prohibited, as were the entry and final examinations. The second author, who had no prior acquaintance with the students, conducted all interviews via Zoom before instruction began and again after final grades were issued; each lasted approximately 30-60 minutes and was transcribed verbatim. The design makes the policy itself the contrast: one sanctioned episode of tool-assisted writing set against a semester of prohibited use.

## Activity theory supplies the vocabulary, not the proof

The authors adopt Engeström's second-generation activity theory as a sensitizing framework, using subject, object, tools, rules, community, division of labor and outcome as an analytic vocabulary applied after initial inductive coding rather than as codes fixed in advance. Four tensions organized the accounts: tools-rules (permissible help versus cheating), tools-object (surface polish versus interpretive aims), subject-tools (authorial voice versus generic output), and tools-division of labor (AI guidance versus human mentoring). Participants also showed what the authors call object-aligned calibration, relating decisions about assistance to the aims of literary-critical writing. The paper is explicit that this is a pattern in narrated accounts, not a demonstrated mechanism or a reconstructed activity system, and the authors frame their contribution as an extension of activity-theory work to a genre where contradictions involving [[learner-identity|voice, authorship and evaluation]] are especially vivid.

## What students delegated to AI, and what they kept

The first theme captures the mentor-or-crutch tension: some students described targeted, tutor-like dialogue, while others experienced reliance as a substitute for their own decision-making. One student's AI-assisted paper received a high grade (91), yet on the final examination without AI she "faced an original problem with the vocabulary" and got 60, a comparison the authors treat as a perception of uneven performance rather than evidence of cause. The second theme traces insecurity and boundary-setting around voice; the third records perceived limits, with feedback described as mechanical; the fourth shows students increasingly treating AI as a paratutor for micro-tasks while reserving conceptual mentoring for people. What they kept was consistent: interpretive claim-making and responsibility for stance. The accounts align with other qualitative work on student rationalization and [[bounded-reliance-ai-writing-feedback-2026|bounded reliance]], and with studies of [[academic-erasure-complexity-ai-writing-2026|complexity loss under AI-supported writing]].

## Surface polish versus the epistemic core

The artifact record adds an independent descriptive layer. In Paper 1, texts typically showed emerging interpretive ideas but unstable argument, and [[feedback]] pressed arguability and proof. Paper 2 sometimes displayed more conventional register, sentence-level fluency and structure, and two students earned strong organization and argument ratings (25/30 in organization, mid-50s/60 in argument), yet instructor comments continued to target misalignment between thesis and body, weak justification chains and questionable evidence selection. Paper 3, a 250-300-word in-class analysis of an unseen text without internet access, repeated the demand for thesis specificity; one student's style rating fell from 10/10 on Paper 2 to 3/10, and relatively strong work also appeared without AI, with revised submissions earning grades of 91 and 94. The convergence is in the focus of [[assessment]], not proof that AI caused any feature. Throughout, [[rubrics|rubric ratings]] were not directly comparable across assignments, and the authors present the [[reflection|reflective]] turn in student accounts as increased articulation of values rather than proven improvement in competence.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Make argument, evidence and interpretive responsibility explicit in [[rubrics|rubrics]], model prompts that ask tools for diagnostic feedback on a draft containing the student's own ideas, and require transparent documentation of permitted use.
- **Faculty developers.** Treat a single permitted episode as a teachable structure rather than a loophole to close: the contrast made effort and authorship visible to students, and bounded exposure produced [[reflection]] that blanket permission did not.
- **Administrators.** Do not infer learning effects from surface polish. Grades and style ratings moved independently of the feedback's epistemic focus, so any policy claim about AI improving [[writing-education|writing]] needs direct [[educational-measurement|measurement]] of warranted reasoning.
- **Researchers.** Pair interviews with consented interaction logs and version histories, keep perceptions and observed practice distinct, and test whether boundaries drawn in literary analysis transfer to other disciplines and [[educational-policy-ai|policy]] regimes.

## Limitations

- Interviews are self-reports of practice and values; without prompt histories or tool-use logs the study cannot determine which suggestions students received or accepted, or how particular passages were produced.
- The study is confined to one program and a self-selected group of eighteen students, limiting transferability.
- The three assignments differed in genre, length, drafting arrangements, assessment criteria and AI permissions, so rubric ratings cannot isolate AI effects, test [[transfer-of-learning|transfer]], or support claims of regression.
- The course instructor was a researcher; the authors mitigate role conflict through independent interviewing and de-identification, but the dual role remains.
- Post hoc ChatGPT use was limited to alternative wording and clarity checks and generated no codes or findings, but it means the analysis was not entirely AI-free.
