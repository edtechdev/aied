> **Synthesis:** Yuan and colleagues describe a cybersecurity program at one Chinese university rebuilt around Outcome-Based Education for the AI era. Starting from job competencies for intelligent-security roles, the team reverse-designed the [[curriculum-design|curriculum]] and added AI attack–defense content, a progressive four-stage practical sequence, and a five-part support system built on industry–education collaboration. The paper reports program-level indicators: 186 experimental projects, 11 talent cultivation bases, approximately 2100 [[graduate-education|graduates]], competition awards, employment figures, and an average employer satisfaction rate of 96.2% from 158 valid responses collected over 2021–2025. The authors explicitly state that a randomized design was not feasible, that the indicators are indirect observational proxies, and that no standardized pre–post skill tests were run. The results support correlation between the reform and reported outcomes, not causal attribution.

## Key Findings
1. **The curriculum was reverse-designed.** The team began from AI security job competencies and derived learning objectives, course content, practical training, and assessment from them.
2. **Practice was staged.** A progressive system moved from real-world scenarios to hands-on operations, practical training, and real combat exercises.
3. **Support had five parts.** Industry–education integration, AI-specific courses, intelligent platforms, [[educational-development|faculty development]], and an evaluation guarantee formed the proposed support system.
4. **AI content was concrete.** The AI Security Applications module included [[machine-learning|machine learning]] security, deep learning for intrusion detection, intelligent malware analysis, and AI-based attack and defense mechanisms.
5. **Program indicators were reported.** The university described 186 experimental projects, 11 joint cultivation bases, competition results, employment outcomes, and employer feedback.
6. **Causality was not established.** The authors call the indicators indirect proxies and note the absence of standardized pre–post assessments of individual competence.

## What the model requires

The program sets objectives for traditional offensive-and-defensive practitioners and intelligent-security specialists. Using OBE backward design, the team researched job requirements in cybersecurity, security, and AI security with enterprise engineers and faculty, then broke competencies such as AI vulnerability discovery and intelligent threat detection into learning objectives threaded through curriculum, training, and assessment. A four-dimensional quality monitoring system involves students, peers, enterprise AI experts, and employers, with findings fed back into course content, methods, and faculty training.

The practical teaching sequence begins with realistic scenario demonstrations on network ranges and virtual simulation platforms, where conventional and AI-driven attack principles are taught together. Classroom exercises then move from basic AI-assisted scanning and vulnerability discovery to machine learning-based intrusion detection and AI-driven encrypted traffic analysis. School–enterprise cooperation adds [[project-based-learning|project-based]] training with partners including Hangzhou Anheng Information Technology Co., Ltd. and Gansu Haifeng Information Technology Co., Ltd., covering assessment, intelligent vulnerability remediation, and incident response. The final stage uses competitions such as the Great Wall Cup, NetDing Cup, and StrongNet Cup and assurance tasks for events including the National University Games and the Canton Fair.

The support system combines diversified university–enterprise cooperation, an experimental course structure with modules for Network Protocol Analysis, Operating System Security, Application Security, Network Attack and Defense, and AI Security Applications, and three specialized laboratories. The AI module uses [[open-source]] tools including Scikit-learn, TensorFlow, and PyTorch and public datasets including USTCTFC2016, CIC-IDS2017, and CIC-Bell-DNS-EXF-2021. Faculty development includes recruiting AI security professionals and sending incumbent faculty to corporate R&D frontlines. Quality assurance specifies competency indicators at each stage and uses evaluation by students, peer teachers, and external experts.

## What the reported outcomes show

The college reports developing five provincial-level first-class courses and establishing an interdisciplinary core curriculum centered on “Cybersecurity + AI.” Working with the Tuoge Platform, it developed simulation platforms for five core courses and integrated modules for intelligent attack and defense, AI forensics, and data security; 186 experimental projects had been established. Three provincial-level teaching and research teams and three provincial-level teaching competition awards are reported, and the Information Security Laboratory and Cybersecurity Laboratory were recognized as provincial-level Experimental Teaching Demonstration Centers. The reform initiative was adopted and promoted by eight local universities, benefiting over 10,000 students annually.

Over the past five years, the college established 11 talent cultivation bases with more than 10 government departments and enterprises. Among graduates, 60.18% remain employed in Gansu Province and other western regions. The college reports approximately 2100 undergraduate graduates, 53 national-level academic competition awards, 520 provincial-level awards, seven National Cybersecurity Scholarship recipients, and a 35% employment rate at leading enterprises such as QiAnXin and in public security, national security, and other government agencies. Average employer satisfaction reached 96.2%, based on 158 valid responses collected over 2021–2025. The survey [[rubrics|rubric]] covers theoretical knowledge, practical cybersecurity skills, AI security operational capability, teamwork, and professional literacy, with respondents from security firms, public security-related institutions, and state-owned organizations. The college also reports targeted training for 600 local cybersecurity practitioners.

These figures are observational. The paper states that the reform was implemented across the whole program, so a randomized controlled design was not feasible; improvements coincided with the phased rollout but may also reflect labor market shifts, laboratory investment, and national competition policies. Multi-source evidence is described as consistent with the model being effective, yet supporting correlation rather than causal attribution. The authors also note that they have not performed standardized pre–post skill tests to quantify individual gains in AI security ability.

## What this means for practice
- **Curriculum designers.** Reverse-map job competencies into measurable objectives before adjusting individual courses, following the OBE logic described in the paper.
- **Instructors.** Use staged practical tasks that move from scenario demonstration to hands-on exercises, enterprise projects, and competition or event-based work.
- **Institutions.** Build dual-qualified teaching teams with enterprise experts and maintain platforms that approximate intelligent attack–defense scenarios.
- **Administrators.** Collect multi-source feedback from students, peers, enterprise experts, and employers, while labeling employment and award data as indirect indicators rather than competence measures.
- **Researchers.** Develop direct competence assessments and pre–post evaluations before claiming that a program caused improved AI security capability.

## Limitations
- **Single institution.** Empirical evidence comes primarily from one regional university, and broader multi-institutional validation is needed.
- **No causal design.** The program-wide rollout precluded randomization, and the authors describe only correlation between the reform and observed outcomes.
- **Indirect outcomes.** Employment, awards, and employer feedback do not directly measure students' AI–cybersecurity competence.
- **No pre–post skill data.** Standardized assessments of individual gains were not available at the time of writing.
- **Resource constraints.** The authors cite high hardware costs and rapid technology iteration as limits on high-end training resources, plus a small pool of AI cybersecurity experts.
- **Collaboration mismatch.** Insufficient depth of university–industry AI collaboration is attributed to differing benefit expectations between universities and enterprises.
