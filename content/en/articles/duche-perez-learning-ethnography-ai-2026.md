> **Synthesis:** Duche-Pérez and colleagues (2026) propose a pedagogical model for teaching ethnography with conversational generative AI in university courses. The model organizes learning into six formative moments: preparation before fieldwork, AI-based rehearsal, peer role-play, a field encounter students must undertake themselves, post-field analysis and writing, and evaluation with mandatory revision. Its central claim is epistemic rather than empirical: a generated passage has a different status from a claim traceable to field relationships, and students must learn to say what an output supports before they use it. The authors refine "false ethnography" to mean false provenance, unwarranted empirical attribution, and a lack of accountability to interlocutors rather than synthetic text as such. Activities keep an unaided student attempt, instructor scaffolding, comparison with generated output, structured debriefing, and justified revision. The model is offered for pilot implementation, not as a tested intervention; the article reports no empirical data, with no sample, instrument, or measured student outcome, and its design decisions remain open to classroom evaluation.

## Key Findings
1. **Six formative moments structure the proposal.** Preparation, AI rehearsal, peer role-play, the field encounter, post-field analysis and writing, and evaluation with revision are presented as a sequence whose ordering and suitability remain provisional.
2. **An unaided attempt comes first.** Students write an initial question or interpretation before seeing generated material, so instructors can examine reasoning that might disappear once a fluent answer is available.
3. **Generated dialogue is rehearsal, not testimony.** A simulated refusal can help a student practice respecting a boundary; it provides no evidence that people at the proposed site would refuse or would do so for the reason the model supplied.
4. **Three settings make the same decision visible.** In an AI rehearsal a pause is a textual cue, in peer role-play a classmate can explain it, and in a field encounter its timing and circumstances may remain genuinely unresolved.
5. **False ethnography is a provenance failure.** Presenting generated speech, observations, or interpretations as findings from fieldwork is the error; a weak interpretation with an identifiable basis is a different, correctable problem.
6. **Traceability accompanies, but does not replace, accountability.** An analysis log can record sources, prompts, versions, and decisions, yet it cannot settle whose experience a category captures or whether a participant could contest the representation.
7. **Consent must cover the specific processing.** Permission for an interview or recording does not establish permission for AI-assisted summarization, analysis, translation, or reuse; an equivalent non-AI route must be available.

## The six moments in concrete terms

The article develops the model through a selective conceptual synthesis guided by four questions: how students learn ethnographic judgment, what gives an ethnographic claim authority, how digital environments shape field relationships, and when synthetic material is justified in methodological training. Situated learning informed the emphasis on independent interpretation and revision; debates on ethnographic authority informed the requirement to trace claims to field materials. The authors describe these as design decisions rather than findings from classroom trials.

In the first moment, students draft research questions and initial dimensions of observation without AI, then ask the model to critique the draft or suggest alternatives. A question such as "Why have young people lost their traditional customs?" is used to show how wording can assume a change, treat young people as a homogeneous group, and introduce the researcher's category before participants have spoken. The model can flag leading wording or abstract terms, but its judgments about tone depend on textual patterns and the context supplied.

Rehearsal and peer role-play are deliberately contrasted. In rehearsal, a chatbot can be prompted to request clarification whenever the interviewer uses an abstract term; the transcript then shows the precise turn at which the interviewer narrowed or widened the exchange. In peer role-play, students rotate through interviewer, interlocutor, and observer roles, and the observer records a specific exchange such as a follow-up that repeated the interviewer's own term after the speaker had chosen a different one. The field encounter is designated non-delegable: AI may not generate fieldnotes, interview evidence, or reflexive records.

For post-field analysis, students code passages and write a memo before AI proposes categories. The teaching example turns on the phrase "we take turns": one student may code rotation of responsibilities while a model suggests shared authority. Students retrieve surrounding fieldnotes to ask what was allocated, who set the order, and whether anyone could decline. Analysis logs record the source passages, material submitted, processing conditions, prompt, system and version, date, generated suggestion, and the decision to accept, modify, or reject it. The final moment adds an oral defense and mandatory written revision; a completed log or a confident defense is explicitly not treated as a validated measure of judgment.

## The worked teaching example

A 90-minute activity asks when a researcher may describe a category as native or locally meaningful. The instructor introduces the concept, students write an independent memo from a market fieldnote, the class examines the inference together, students request a competing interpretation, both readings are compared with the record, and students revise and justify the claim. The activity is divided into Minutes 0–10 for the concept, Minutes 10–25 for the independent memo, Minutes 25–40 for examining the inference, Minutes 40–50 for generating a competing reading, Minutes 50–70 for comparing both with the fieldnote, and Minutes 70–90 for revision. A fieldnote recording "helping the family" does not by itself sustain a claim about "traditional solidarity," still less a "collectivist Andean worldview." The assessable submission consists of the initial memo, the AI output, a comparison note, and the revised interpretation.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Begin with an unaided student draft so that a later generated suggestion can be compared with a reading you can actually inspect.
- **Instructors.** Ask students to identify which words occur in the field record, which concepts they introduced themselves, and which claims the model added.
- **Instructors.** Treat a generated dialogue as declared rehearsal and forbid its conversion into testimony about real participants.
- **Researchers.** Verify quotations against the original record and state the evidence that could challenge a category before treating it as an ethnographic finding.
- **Institutions.** Establish which systems may process participant material, what consent covers, and an equivalent route for students who decline AI processing.

## Limitations
- The model is a selective conceptual synthesis, not a systematic review or an empirical evaluation; it has not undergone classroom implementation, comparative assessment, or longitudinal evaluation.
- No sample, instrument, or student outcome is reported, so the six moments, their ordering, and the proposed combination of debriefing, oral defense, and revision are untested.
- The authors state that a complete analysis log can document every revision while revealing little about whether a student recognized its consequences for an interlocutor.
- The proposal is written for university ethnographic methods, anthropology, and qualitative methodology courses; its feasibility across field settings, languages, and processing infrastructures requires pilot study.
- Whether the activities strengthen ethnographic attentiveness and judgment is explicitly left as a question for evaluation in the classrooms and field settings where they are used.
