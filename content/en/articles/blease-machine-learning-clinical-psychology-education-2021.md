> **Synthesis:** Blease and colleagues (2021) surveyed postgraduate clinical psychology and psychotherapy students at Basel four months before ChatGPT existed, and the baseline is the point: 97% had heard of [[machine-learning|machine learning]], 46% intended to learn about it for mental health care, and students estimated that 0.52% of their program was spent on it. They moderately agreed it belonged in the curriculum (median 4 on a five-point scale). The qualitative themes show why the gap mattered — students expected AI to change diagnosis, therapy and the profession itself, and had almost no formal preparation for any of it.

## Key Findings

- **Awareness without education.** 97% of respondents had heard the term "machine learning"; 73% (n = 27) intended to enter a mental health profession; 46% intended to learn about AI/ML as it applied to mental health care.
- **Students put the curriculum share at half a percent.** Their own estimate of time spent on AI/ML education was 0.52% of the program.
- **They wanted it taught.** On a five-point scale students moderately agreed (median = 4) that AI/ML should be part of clinical psychology and psychotherapy education.
- **Four themes framed the impact.** Qualitative analysis of free-text comments produced four themes on how AI/ML would affect mental healthcare, running from diagnostic support to threats to the therapeutic relationship.
- **Response rate limits the claim.** 37 of 120 eligible students responded (31%), so this is a pilot picture of one program rather than a sector survey.

## What the study did

A mixed-methods pilot: an online survey of postgraduate students in the Master's program at the University of Basel's Faculty of Psychology, combining Likert items on familiarity, curriculum coverage and attitudes with free-text comments coded into themes. The timing matters for interpretation — data collection closed in early 2021, so the object of the questions was machine learning in mental health care (risk prediction, therapy apps, decision support) rather than the conversational agents that arrived later.

That distance is analytically useful. The paper documents the state of the curriculum before generative AI made the question urgent, which makes it a baseline against which later surveys — including the 2026 latent-class study of psychology students and psychotherapy trainees — can be read.

## Why a pre-2022 baseline still matters

Most current work on AI in clinical training asks how students feel about chatbots. This study asks a prior question: whether the professional curriculum treats computational methods as part of clinical competence at all. The 0.52% estimate answers it, and the answer explains a pattern that later studies keep rediscovering — trainees who are interested, under-prepared, and aware that the tool will shape their practice.

For curriculum designers the paper's contribution is the gap it names. Psychologists are being asked to interpret algorithmic outputs, evaluate therapy apps and explain risk models to patients, and the program that should teach this devotes a rounding error of its time to it. The authors' recommendation is not a course on how models work but integration into existing clinical teaching, so that AI questions arise where clinical questions already do.

## What this means for practice

- **Curriculum designers:** audit the actual hours, not the module catalogue. Student estimates are the honest measure of what a program teaches.
- **Clinical educators:** teach evaluation of algorithmic tools inside existing case-based teaching rather than as a separate technical module.
- **Programmes:** treat pre-2022 baselines as evidence. Surveys from that period show the curriculum gap before it became a policy topic.
- **Instructors:** the four themes students raised are usable as a discussion structure — diagnosis, therapy relationship, professional identity and risk.

## Limitations

One program, 37 respondents, 31% response rate, self-selected. Students estimated curriculum time rather than reporting documented hours. The data predate generative AI, so nothing here speaks to chatbots or LLM-based clinical tools directly. Free-text coding is reported thematically without inter-rater reliability statistics.
