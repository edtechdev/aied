> **Synthesis:** Onaolapo (2026) argues that mandatory AI disclosure fails because institutions ask researchers to declare something that can cost them reputation while doing nothing to protect them from that cost. The paper reads the gap between [[self-report-measures|self-reported]] AI use and actual disclosure not as mass noncompliance but as a predictable response to a badly designed rule. It names the likely mechanism — AI users are judged less competent and less motivated — and argues that the exposure falls hardest on early-career researchers and those whose standing is still contested. Because editors can require a declaration but only institutions can decide how it is treated in hiring, probation and examination, the paper assigns ownership to [[higher-ed|university]] leaders. It proposes stating the consequences of disclosure, distinguishing kinds of AI use, and stopping reliance on detection tools. The article reports no empirical data of its own: it argues from cited studies rather than from a sample, instrument, outcome or test of any policy it discusses.

## Key Findings
1. **Disclosure mandates are nearly universal but rarely honored.** The paper reads persistent silence in published papers and submissions as evidence of a rule design problem, not of unusual dishonesty in academia.
2. **The cost of declaring falls on the person declaring.** Experimental workplace evidence cited in the paper shows that AI users are marked down on competence and motivation; academic publishing has named related effects as AI shaming and the punishment of honesty.
3. **The cost is uneven, but the pattern is not simple.** Second-language writers face both a competence penalty for declaring and biased detectors that may flag them anyway, while regional disclosure differences run against a neat rich–poor account.
4. **The problem belongs to institutions, not journal editors.** A journal can ask a question on a submission form; an institution controls supervision, probation, promotion and examination, where the same declaration carries far higher stakes.
5. **Say what disclosure will mean.** Institutions that require a declaration should state plainly that disclosed language or drafting assistance will not count against a researcher's contribution.
6. **Distinguish kinds of use.** Language editing, structural drafting, data analysis and idea generation should not be collapsed into one declaration, because the mildest use is then read against the most serious.
7. **Detection is not a substitute for trust.** Detectors work on finished text and cannot tell what a tool was used for or whether the author kept intellectual control; the proposals are starting points for evaluation, not tested interventions.

## The gap as the paper frames it
The Perspective opens from a familiar policy instrument: declare AI use, and transparency follows. It then assembles evidence from several sources to argue that this expectation fails in practice. Large-scale counts of journals and published papers show widespread disclosure policies alongside very rare declarations. Data from submissions to publisher journals after disclosure questions became compulsory show similarly low rates, while surveys find much higher self-reported use. The paper warns that these sources measure different populations in different ways, so the distance between them is not a precise gap estimate; only the direction is consistent across publication-level, submission-level and survey evidence.

From that direction, it draws an inference about policy rather than people. When nearly everyone in a group that otherwise follows publication requirements ignores a rule, the paper argues, the more plausible explanation is a rule that asks people to hand over costly information without offering anything in return. Ambiguity makes silence more attractive: policies contradict one another, and when the requirement and the penalty are both unclear, saying nothing is the choice whose consequences an author can predict.

## Why the paper treats silence as a design problem
The mechanism is reputational. Cited experiments in workplace settings found that people who used AI both expected harsher judgment and received it, on competence and on motivation. The paper concedes that those experiments were not studies of peer review, but argues that the inference travels: in scholarship, where reputation rests on perceived intellectual independence, using AI reads as a sign of weaker ability underneath. Publishing scholars have described the same dynamic as AI shaming and as the punishment of honesty. Other cited work disputes the moral framing, noting that shame implies wrongdoing where policy intends to describe good practice. The paper treats that disagreement as evidence that the field has not agreed what a disclosure signals.

Exposure, it argues, varies with how secure a researcher's credibility already is. A senior scholar at an established institution risks little by declaring language assistance; an early-career researcher, [[graduate-education|doctoral]] candidate or scholar at an institution still building its reputation has more to lose from the same sentence. The author also notes that detectors often misclassify writing by non-native English speakers while getting native-speaker writing right, creating a trap: declare and risk a competence judgment, or stay silent and risk being flagged anyway. One finding complicates the fairness story — authors from some regions were more likely to disclose than others — and the paper declines to assert a clean asymmetry, saying regional patterns are not yet well understood.

## The proposal in concrete terms
Three commitments follow, all described as institutional rather than technical. First, institutions that require disclosure should state how it will be treated in probation, promotion, doctoral examination and internal research assessment; a short assurance that declared assistance will not count against a contribution costs nothing and removes the strongest reason for silence. Second, disclosure should separate language editing, structural drafting, data analysis and idea generation. The paper draws on a proposed intentional-and-substantial test and on a distinction between surface editing and co-production, while noting that colleagues argue routine language support should be voluntary. Third, detection tools should not act as an informal backstop; where used, their output should start a conversation, not settle a question, and staff should be told what it cannot do.

The deeper claim is that detection cannot replace disclosure: a detector estimates whether finished text looks machine-generated, but cannot say what the tool was used for or whether the author kept control. The paper closes by noting that none of its three commitments has been tested — no study yet shows that reassurance statements, functional separation or institutional protection increase disclosure. Norms are forming now, it argues, and will be set less by policy wording than by what visibly happens to the first people who comply.

## What this means for practice
- **Institutional leaders.** Publish a plain statement of how declared AI assistance will be treated in probation, promotion and examination before asking researchers to declare it.
- **Administrators.** Separate language editing, drafting, analysis and idea generation in disclosure forms so that the mildest use is not read against the most serious.
- **Faculty developers.** Tell staff explicitly what detector output can and cannot establish, and require that a flag opens a review rather than a penalty.
- **Instructors.** Discuss with doctoral candidates and early-career researchers what declaring assistance will cost them, and record concerns where institutional guidance is silent.
- **Researchers.** Treat the three commitments as hypotheses: evaluate whether reassurance and functional separation change disclosure behavior and perceived fairness.

## Limitations
- The article is a Perspective and reports no empirical data; it has no sample, instrument, outcome or analysis of its own.
- The figures and mechanisms it cites come from different journals, disciplines, countries and time periods, which the paper itself says prevents a precise estimate of the disclosure gap.
- Its clearest experimental evidence comes from workplace settings rather than peer review, so the competence-penalty mechanism is inferred for academic publishing.
- The three proposed commitments are reasoned responses, not tested interventions; no study cited shows that they increase disclosure or reduce asymmetric exposure.
- The paper leaves unresolved both the disagreement over whether AI disclosure carries moral shame and the messy regional evidence on who declares.
