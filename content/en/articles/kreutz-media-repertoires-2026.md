> **Synthesis:** Kreutz (2026) argues that [[generative-ai|generative AI]] should not be counted as one more medium added to a learner's toolkit, but treated as a technology that reconfigures the relationships among the media practices a learner already uses. Building on the media repertoire approach of Hasebrink and colleagues, the paper specifies a learning-related media repertoire — bounded by learning relevance rather than by media type, and spanning formal, non-formal and informal settings — and proposes three dimensions along which generative AI may restructure it: how knowledge is acquired, how epistemic activities are distributed between learner and system, and how the functional relationships between media change. It then reframes AI-related inequality as the *AI gap*: differences in learners' opportunities to access, appropriate and benefit from generative AI, produced across three recursively related levels — the societal media environment, the media ensembles of schools, families and peer groups, and the individual repertoire. The article reports no empirical data: it is a conceptual framework whose examples are illustrative.

## Key Findings

1. **The unit of analysis is the configuration, not the medium.** Media repertoire research moved from cataloguing which media a person uses to analyzing how an individual's media practices relate — complementary, overlapping, substitutive, sequential, or simultaneous — so a repertoire can change without a medium being added or dropped.
2. **Three levels are recursively linked rather than stacked.** Individual repertoires, the media ensembles of social domains such as schools and families, and the societal media environment each shape the others; a school's platform rules and a family's resources are as consequential as the technology itself.
3. **The learning-related repertoire is bounded by relevance, not by design intent.** A medium belongs to it when it becomes relevant to a person's learning, which lets search engines, video platforms, messaging services and generative AI sit alongside learning management systems and textbooks.
4. **Generative AI occupies three roles at once.** It is simultaneously an element of the repertoire, a dialogical partner that supports iterative questioning and refinement of ideas, and an intermediary that summarises, contextualizes or connects material from other sources.
5. **Structural change runs along three dimensions.** Knowledge acquisition shifts in sequence and frequency, as learners open with AI and turn to textbooks or specialist sites later for verification; epistemic activities are delegated, shared or retained; and media relationships reconfigure through new complementarities, convergences, substitutions and sequences.
6. **Frequency of AI use is not evidence of structural change.** The paper insists the dimensions are an analytical frame for looking at shifts in function, sequence and distribution over time, not established effects, and that presence alone should not be read as transformation.
7. **Inequality becomes a property of repertoire structure.** The [[digital-divide|AI gap]] is not merely unequal access or unequal competence: it is unequal opportunity to build a repertoire in which AI is combined with other sources and with social support for verification.

## Why individual competence is the wrong center of gravity

The paper's target is the framing that dominates current work: [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]], competencies, [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]], efficiency and governance, each studied as an encounter between an autonomous learner and a tool. Kreutz accepts that competencies matter, then argues that an exclusive focus on them makes the social and institutional shaping of practice invisible. The same application behaves differently at home, among peers, under a teacher's guidance, or inside an institutional platform, and the empirical literature the paper reviews reports heterogeneous use patterns that task-level studies cannot explain. Hence the shift from whether a technology helps or harms learning to how practices, sources and people are arranged around it.

## What the framework asks a study to observe

The three dimensions are written as observational instructions rather than hypotheses. For knowledge acquisition, watch the order in which sources are consulted and whether reference works move from orientation to verification. For epistemic activities, record which tasks a learner performs alone, which are delegated wholly or partly, and which are shared with AI or with people — [[cognitive-offloading|cognitive offloading]] is treated as a distribution of responsibility, not a deficit. For media relationships, look for functional convergence between AI and search, substitution of explanatory sites or translation tools, and new sequences such as AI output transferred into a word processor and then checked against a textbook. The paper's own illustration is a classroom presentation: information seeking changes, writing and summarising are redistributed, and a new link forms between the AI tool and the presentation software, all in one task.

## Two contrasting repertoires, offered as illustrations

Kreutz sketches an advantaged configuration — reliable AI access combined with established habits of consulting textbooks, specialist sources and teachers or peers to verify and contextualize output, so AI can open a task while evaluation stays with the learner — against a disadvantaged one in which access exists but guidance and complementary sources do not, leaving generated answers as the only available reference. The paper is explicit that these are analytical illustrations rather than validated types. What they carry is the claim that [[equity-in-ai-education|equity]] lives in the arrangement of practices, which is why [[information-and-media-literacy|media literacy]] is re-specified as the capacity to navigate relationships between media and sources, to judge which epistemic activities may be delegated, and to acknowledge what AI-supported knowledge production cannot settle.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Ask students to narrate the sequence of a task — which source came first, what was checked against what — and design at least one task per unit that cannot be completed inside a single interface, so comparing a generated answer with a textbook or a peer's reading stays a practiced move.
- **Course and curriculum designers.** Treat [[student-ai-interaction|student-AI interaction]] as one element of a repertoire that includes the library, the textbook, the LMS and each other, and check that the course still teaches the relations between them.
- **Faculty developers.** Audit the repertoire a program assumes rather than the tools it licenses: guidance, complementary sources and social support for verification are the conditions under which [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulated use]] becomes possible.
- **Administrators and policy makers.** Provisioning access does not close the AI gap; the framework locates the difference in guidance, institutional rules and the availability of sources to check output against.
- **Researchers.** Use the three dimensions as a coding frame for [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal]], biographical or comparative work, and report configurations rather than correlations between use frequency and outcomes.

## Limitations

- **No empirical data.** The paper is conceptual and states plainly that the framework has not been validated; there is no sample, no instrument, and no measured outcome.
- **The dimensions overlap by design.** They are complementary analytical perspectives rather than mutually exclusive categories, so a single episode can register on all three and coding will require judgment.
- **The examples are illustrative.** The contrasting repertoire configurations and the classroom-presentation case are analytical illustrations, explicitly not representative or empirically established types.
- **Scope across phases and subjects is untested.** Whether the framework transfers across age groups, educational phases, subject areas and institutional settings remains open, as does its behavior under rapid changes in AI functionality and access conditions.
