> **Synthesis:** Thirty-four first-year students in a compulsory musicology unit at the Sydney Conservatorium of Music used *Mystic Melodies*, a [[game-based-learning|gamified]] adaptive-difficulty AI quiz, after a lecture on Music and the Supernatural. They broadly agreed they understood the topic (M = 3.74, SD = 0.67), but their judgments of the app scattered: engagement 3.06 (SD = 1.15), comparison with traditional tutorials 2.74 (SD = 1.19), future use 2.76 (SD = 1.30). The 21 students who would recommend it and the 13 who would not reported similar topic understanding (3.81 vs. 3.62, p = 0.420) while differing sharply on future use (3.52 vs. 1.54) and AI perception (3.67 vs. 1.69). Open-ended comments, read through disciplinary thinking, locate the stronger evidence in resistance: objections to right-or-wrong quiz logic, to learning "outsourced to a computer," and to feedback that rewards copied keywords. The authors conclude that students may accept AI as optional review but resist it as a substitute for expert-led, interpretive teaching.

## Key Findings

1. **Perceived topic understanding was the one point of consensus.** Students rated their understanding of the lecture theme at M = 3.74 (SD = 0.67), with 23 agreeing or strongly agreeing, 10 neutral, and 1 disagreeing — against standard deviations of 1.05 to 1.30 on the app-evaluation items.
2. **Every other aspect of the app divided them.** Engagement averaged 3.06 (SD = 1.15), enhanced understanding 3.12 (SD = 1.23), game features 2.94 (SD = 1.20), feedback 3.15 (SD = 1.21), and AI perception 2.91 (SD = 1.22); the lowest-rated items were comparison with traditional tutorial instruction at 2.74 (SD = 1.19) and future use at 2.76 (SD = 1.30), each with 14 respondents disagreeing or strongly disagreeing.
3. **Adaptive hints were the most positively received feature.** Hints drew M = 3.41 (SD = 1.05), a median of 4, and 20 respondents agreeing or strongly agreeing against 4 disagreeing.
4. **Willingness to recommend split the sample but did not track learning.** Twenty-one students would recommend the tool and 13 would not, and the groups' topic-understanding ratings were 3.81 vs. 3.62 (p = 0.420, adjusted p = 0.420), a difference the authors call not statistically significant.
5. **Recommendation tracked the experience of the app instead.** The largest gaps were future use (3.52 vs. 1.54), AI perception (3.67 vs. 1.69), and enhanced understanding (3.86 vs. 1.92); Mann–Whitney U tests with Holm correction found significant differences on engagement, enhanced understanding, feedback, adaptive hints, game features, future use, AI perception, and comparison with traditional tutorials (adjusted p < 0.05).
6. **The disciplinary-fit evidence sits mostly in the complaints.** 9 out of 34 respondents made comments coded under disciplinary fit; the authors say six offered the clearest explanatory insight; only one comment explicitly linked the app to [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]] and interdisciplinary interpretation, while others objected to correct-or-incorrect judging, to "outsourced" expertise, to inhibited independent thinking, and to keyword-copying the AI failed to catch.
7. **The authors' design answer is supplement, not substitute.** They recommend positioning the app as optional or semi-structured review outside class time, limiting in-class use to a brief demonstration or debrief, and adding open-ended prompts and feedback that distinguishes surface keyword matching from interpretive understanding.

## The intervention and the sample

The study ran in Semester 1, 2025, inside *This Is Music*, a compulsory first-year unit at the Sydney Conservatorium of Music that treats music as an interdisciplinary field and changes theme weekly. The intervention fell in week 9 (28th April–2nd May), when the theme was Music and the Supernatural; students attended a one-hour lecture and then completed the quiz during a one-hour tutorial. *Mystic Melodies* ran on Cogniti and was powered by [[generative-ai|ChatGPT]]-4o. Its design combined short-answer questioning, a quest narrative in which correct answers generated mythic gifts and teaching points while incorrect answers withheld gifts and explained why, optional "hint" requests for [[adaptive-learning|adaptive difficulty]], and a [[formative-assessment|formative assessment]], concluding after ten questions.

323 students were enrolled in the unit. The intervention and survey ran in six tutorial groups comprising approximately 90 students who were invited to participate, and participation in both was optional. Thirty-four valid responses were analyzed, approximately 37.8% of students invited. No demographic or background variables were collected, so the sample is an anonymous convenience sample from one cohort in one institution.

The instrument was a [[self-report-measures|five-point Likert survey]] from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree), developed for this study rather than adapted from a validated scale, with items derived from the intervention's design features. It covered overall evaluation plus open-ended questions on effective aspects, improvements, and recommendation. Understanding items were treated as self-reported perceptions, not measures of [[learning-gains|learning gain]].

## What the numbers show

Topic understanding sits apart from everything else: mean 3.74, median 4, interquartile range 1, one dissenting respondent. The app-evaluation items cluster nearer the midpoint with far wider spread, and both comparative items fall below the midpoint: 11 respondents preferred the app to traditional tutorial instruction and 11 expected to use similar apps in future, against 14 who did not.

Splitting by recommendation sharpens it. The 21 who would recommend and the 13 who would not were close on perceived understanding (3.81 vs. 3.62) and little else: engagement 3.71 vs. 2.00, feedback 3.76 vs. 2.15, adaptive hints 3.81 vs. 2.77, game features 3.48 vs. 2.08, preference over traditional tutorials 3.29 vs. 1.85. Every intervention-related comparison survived Holm correction. The authors caution that recommendation is itself a global judgment, so group differences are associations among related perceptions, not independent evidence, and the understanding item had lower variance. Still, the direction suggests students were not rejecting the material as too hard; they were judging the mode of learning.

A satisfaction survey quoting the 3.74 mean could call the quiz successful, yet students who understood the topic could still regard it as a poor substitute for a tutorial.

## Why the resistance looked disciplinary

The [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] analysis produced several themes — feedback and teaching points, engagement and gamification, revision and review, AI limitations, and disciplinary fit — but the paper concentrates on the last because it alone offers a potential explanation for the central [[quantitative-research|quantitative]] pattern. The frame comes from disciplinary-thinking research: Wineburg on historical thinking as a learned practice, Shulman on signature [[pedagogy|pedagogies]], Clarence on the specificity of disciplinary knowledge. On that view, [[humanities-education|music humanities]] students learn to connect sound to cultural meaning, judge interpretations, and recognize what counts as evidence, not merely to recall repertoire facts.

9 out of 34 respondents made comments coded under disciplinary fit, applied both where concerns were explicit and where they could reasonably be inferred, since first-year students may lack the vocabulary to name them. The clearest positive case, Student 28, called the tool "particularly effective for enhancing critical thinking and deepening understanding by offering diverse perspectives on topics like the intersection of music, culture, and the supernatural" — the only comment explicitly linking the tool to critical thinking and interdisciplinary interpretation.

The weight lies in the objections. Student 13 asked for "more opinion based response questions" and for the system to "converse with the response in an engaging way that initiates further inquiries" instead of judging answers correct or incorrect, which the authors read as identifying a mismatch between quiz logic and the humanities tutorial. Student 15 objected to learning "outsourced to a computer that has no understanding of music or art." Student 25 said AI "does not allow you to learn effectively and think independently." Student 27 valued feedback split into "Assessment, Explanation, and Teaching point" but caught the AI accepting an answer that copied the question's key words.

Student 26 supplies the design conclusion in miniature: the tool was "an interesting tool to offer a different approach to revise lecture material," but "I don't think it can replace traditional tutorials." Across these comments, the authors argue, students were evaluating not only [[usability-research|usability]] but whether the interaction fit the inquiry they associate with music humanities — interpretation, dialogue, expertise, independent thought. They also insist the lens is limited: disciplinary fit is one plausible explanation among several, not a demonstrated dominant one.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors in humanities units.** Do not make a quiz app the central or compulsory tutorial activity for everyone; the authors recommend optional or semi-structured review outside class time, with class time reserved for discussion and interpretation.
- **Learning designers.** Build open-ended and opinion-based prompts, make [[ai-feedback-quality|feedback]] explicitly interpretive rather than only corrective, and test whether the system can distinguish a copied keyword from an argued answer.
- **Program leads reading evaluation data.** Report app-experience items separately from perceived topic understanding; a high understanding mean alongside 2.74 for comparison with traditional tutorials describes a cohort that felt it learned but did not endorse the mode.
- **Researchers.** Treat recommendation groups as analytic categories rather than student types, and pair self-report with objective or artifact-based measures.

## Limitations

- The data come from one survey, one cohort, one institution, and one activity in one topic.
- Thirty-four valid responses represent approximately 37.8% of the approximately 90 students invited across six tutorial groups, and participation was optional.
- No demographic or background variables were collected, including age, gender, and prior experience with AI, so prior attitudes cannot be ruled out.
- All outcomes are self-reported perceptions collected immediately after the activity, with no objective learning measure and no pre/post comparison.
- There was no comparison or control condition, so the study cannot say whether other tutorial activities would have produced different responses.
- The disciplinary-fit evidence is thin — 9 out of 34 comments, only one explicitly positive — and the authors state it does not show disciplinary thinking was the dominant basis for evaluation; the instrument was not validated.
