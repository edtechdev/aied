> **Synthesis:** This [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|systematic review]] asks whether digital literacy frameworks still fit an AI-saturated world. Screening four databases for validated instruments published between January 2014 and February 2025, the authors assemble 69 digital literacy frameworks and 11 [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] frameworks — 80 in all — and read them through an ecological lens at three levels: the school microsystem, the socio-economic macrosystem, and the historical chrono-system. What they find is a field split by role and by region. Student frameworks cluster on information, communication, safety and creation; educator frameworks spread across pedagogy, learner empowerment and assessment. Developed regions weight communication and collaboration, developing regions weight [[problem-solving|problem solving]] and operational use, and copyright and licenses appear in no developing-region framework. The chrono-level answer is that AI has expanded digital literacy from safety toward [[ethics|AI ethics]], elevated higher-order thinking, and recast communication to include [[human-ai-collaboration|human–AI interaction]] — while metacognitive regulation remains thinly specified.

## Key Findings

1. The review analyzes 80 validated frameworks — 69 digital literacy (67 database-derived plus two reports) and 11 AI literacy — from a PRISMA screen of 35,036 without-AI and 441 with-AI records, covering publications from January 2014 to February 2025.
2. Of the 69 digital literacy frameworks, 28 target students or citizens and 42 target educators (one counted in both roles); student frameworks most often carry information and data literacy (n = 40), communication and collaboration (n = 20), and safety and ethics (n = 19).
3. Educator frameworks distribute differently, with teaching, learning and pedagogy (n = 38), empowering learners (n = 25) and [[curriculum-design|curriculum]] and assessment (n = 25) leading, and information and data literacy (n = 31) and technology literacy (n = 28) the prominent competencies.
4. Mapped onto DigComp 2.2, framework coverage concentrates on information and data literacy (frameworks n = 24), communication and collaboration (n = 19) and safety (n = 19), and less on digital content creation (n = 16) and problem solving (n = 15).
5. Across economies, communication and collaboration was covered by 83.3% (n = 10) of developed-region frameworks against 60.0% (n = 9) of developing-region frameworks, while problem solving was slightly more frequent in developing (60.0%, n = 9) than developed (50.0%, n = 6) regions.
6. DigComp 2.2 amends 73 novel AI examples: communication and collaboration 31.51% (n = 23), problem solving 27.40% (n = 20), information and data literacy 19.18% (n = 14), safety 12.33% (n = 9) and digital content creation 9.59% (n = 7).
7. Among the 11 AI literacy frameworks, only Zhong and Liu's (2025) KAT framework does not treat ethics as an explicit dimension; technical knowledge spans all five DigComp content areas, while metacognitive regulation and higher-order thinking remain under development.

## How the review selected and appraised its frameworks

The search ran across ERIC, Web of Science, ProQuest and Scopus, with two parallel branches — one carrying a [[generative-ai|generative-AI]] term block, one without — using Boolean strings in title, abstract and keywords. The without-AI branch returned 35,036 records and the with-AI branch 441. After duplicate removal and screening against eligibility criteria that required questionnaire or survey designs and samples of N ≥ 100, 162 records reached quality appraisal. Appraisal followed the COSMIN checklist on a three-point scale (0 = no evidence of reliability or validity; 1 = reliability or validity but not both; 2 = both), retaining only non-zero records with multi-item coverage. Thirty-nine were excluded; from 123 retained records, 67 unique frameworks plus two reports (ACARA, 2020; Fraillon et al., 2023) yielded 69. The with-AI branch retained 11 unique frameworks. Dual coding reached Cohen's κ = .89 to .92 across the three research questions, and DigComp 2.2 served as a shared reference taxonomy for cross-framework mapping rather than a normative standard.

## What the validated frameworks contain

Inductive content analysis of the 69 digital literacy frameworks produced eleven element categories, stratified by role: 28 frameworks for students or citizens and 42 for educators, with one framework counted in both. For students, the five most emphasized categories align closely with DigComp: information and data literacy (n = 40), communication and collaboration (n = 20), safety and ethics (n = 19), digital content creation (n = 13) and problem solving and [[creativity]] (n = 13), followed by technology literacy (n = 15). Educator frameworks distribute their elements more evenly and systemically: teaching, learning and pedagogy (n = 38), empowering learners (n = 25), curriculum and assessment (n = 25) and [[educational-development|professional development]] (n = 8), with information and data literacy (n = 31) and technology literacy (n = 28) as the leading competencies. The authors note that educator-facing frameworks treat [[assessment|assessment design]] as part of the role, while cultural inclusiveness appears in only one educator framework and attitudes toward ICT in 7 of the 42. Student-centered frameworks emphasize discrete individual skills, whereas educator-centered frameworks feature systemic capacities such as planning, design and management of digital resources.

## The digital divide, reframed for AI

The macro-level question is the [[digital-divide|digital divide]]. Mapping the 28 student and citizen frameworks onto DigComp 2.2's five competence areas, information and data literacy leads (frameworks n = 24), followed by communication and collaboration (n = 19) and safety (n = 19), then digital content creation (n = 16) and problem solving (n = 15). Comparing frameworks by World Bank FY2026 income classification, information and data literacy was covered in 86.7% (n = 13) of developing-region frameworks and 91.7% (n = 11) of developed ones; communication and collaboration in 60.0% (n = 9) against 83.3% (n = 10); and problem solving slightly more in developing (60.0%, n = 9) than developed (50.0%, n = 6) regions. Copyright and licenses were represented by no developing-region framework. The authors read the pattern through [[sociocultural-learning|sociocultural]] theory as developed regions emphasizing integrative capacities while developing regions privilege operational skills that AI can increasingly automate — descriptive differences within the reviewed sample, not evidence of regional deficiency. Sensitivity checks removing higher-education-only instruments left the communication and problem-solving patterns intact, though information and data literacy and safety were less stable.

## How AI reshapes digital literacy

At the chrono-level the authors map DigComp 2.2 onto the 11 AI literacy frameworks. DigComp 2.2 adds 73 novel AI examples, concentrated in communication and collaboration (31.51%, n = 23), problem solving (27.40%, n = 20) and information and data literacy (19.18%, n = 14), with less attention to safety (12.33%, n = 9) and digital content creation (9.59%, n = 7). The conceptual expansion is threefold: digital safety widens into AI ethics, [[metacognition|higher-order thinking and metacognitive components]] gain prominence, and communication and collaboration are reinterpreted to include human–[[student-ai-interaction|AI interaction]]. Technical knowledge is the most prominent AI literacy element, spanning all five DigComp content areas; AI ethics is second, and critical evaluation third, concerned with appraising outputs for accuracy, reliability, completeness and bias. Less consistently represented are metacognitive regulation, [[critical-thinking|higher-order thinking]] and educator-specific human–AI collaboration, which the authors describe as still under development. Two of the eleven frameworks target educators, and the AI-[[tpack]] extension models how content, [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] and AI-technological knowledge combine.

## What this means for practice

- **Curriculum designers.** Treat DigComp 2.2 as an adaptable reference, not a global [[benchmark]]. The review finds frameworks differ systematically by region and role, so competence lists should be localized to infrastructure, language and governance conditions rather than imported wholesale.
- **Faculty developers.** Build educator-facing frameworks around AI as a collaborative partner rather than a passive tool. Only two of the eleven AI literacy frameworks target educators, and the AI-TPACK extension points to composite knowledge — particularly AI-technological pedagogical knowledge — as the professional development target.
- **Assessment designers.** Shore up validity where generative AI decouples observable performance from durable learning: design AI-resilient tasks with transparent staged processes and real-time verification so completed work still evidences mastery.
- **Policymakers.** Watch the copyright and licensing gap. No developing-region framework represented it, and AI-generated content sharpens disputes over authorship and ownership, so digital literacy policy and copyright law need to move together.

## Limitations

- The review covers publications from January 2014 to February 2025; the ≤2023 and >2023 split used for the period comparison is a lag-based descriptive proxy following the late-2022 release of ChatGPT, since framework design, validation and publication typically lag tool release.
- The macro-level comparison rests on a modest corpus of validated student and citizen frameworks stratified by participants' country or region and World Bank FY2026 income classification; most are instruments validated in specific educational settings rather than national-level frameworks, and socio-economic conditions vary within countries.
- Restricting inclusion to validated frameworks may overlook emerging competences absent from current instruments, such as [[prompt-engineering|prompt engineering]]; the authors note that Levy-Nadav et al. (2024) was excluded because it offers [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] insight without empirical validation in target populations.
- The branching search did not partition the literature as intended: the with-AI branch yielded only 1 of the 11 frameworks uniquely, while 42 AI-related records entered the pool from the without-AI branch, so the branch distinction should not be read as an analytic contrast between generative-era and earlier framework work.
- Because AI literacy is treated as the umbrella construct, the review may under-represent frameworks published under narrower adjacent labels such as [[machine-learning]] literacy or algorithmic literacy.
