> **Synthesis:** Lee, Ha and Lee ask how a teaching-focused private university can move from isolated AI courses to systematic AI convergence education. At a mid-sized Korean university they combined four components: analysis of [[educational-policy-ai|national AI education policy]] and domestic and international cases, a complete census of 67 program units and 1646 courses across 55 non-AI-specialist majors using a four-type typology, a needs survey of department heads and program chairs in which 27 of 67 responded, and rapid prototyping of a staged curriculum framework reviewed by five experts over two rounds. Explicitly AI-articulated courses accounted for only 1.5% of the curriculum (25 courses in 13 of 55 majors), while 193 provisionally identified candidate courses (11.7%) sat in 46 of the 55 majors yet were absent from curriculum documentation, learning outcomes and progression pathways. Two-thirds of responding leaders endorsed the change while naming infrastructure and institutional support as the decisive conditions. The study presents a diagnosis and a design, not an evaluation: latent candidates require syllabus-level verification, and no learning, [[equity-in-ai-education|equity]] or enrollment effects were measured.

## Key Findings

- **An articulation gap.** Twenty-five courses (1.5%) explicitly articulated AI, against 193 (11.7%) with plausible AI-integration potential — nearly eight times as many, spread across 46 of 55 majors.
- **Adoption is early and uneven.** Only 13 of 55 majors offered even one explicitly AI-articulated course; explicit provision clustered in teacher education (9 of the 25), health sciences and culture and arts, while three-quarters of majors had none.
- **Latent potential varies by field.** Design and media carried 30.3% Type 4 courses and theology and Christian studies 22.9%, against 1.9% in teacher education, where explicit articulation was already furthest along.
- **Leaders endorse, conditionally.** Two-thirds (66.7%) called major-level AI convergence necessary; their foremost concerns were [[institutional-ai-infrastructure|infrastructure]] (59.3%), administrative and financial support (48.1%) and unverified learning effects (44.4%).
- **Conversion beats addition.** 55.6% preferred converting existing courses over creating new ones (37.0%), and 59.3% favored AI modules inside the practice track.
- **Infrastructure is the gating condition.** 77.8% named AI platform and software-license support as the most needed action, ahead of faculty training and consulting (55.6%).
- **Design changed under review.** Expert critique pushed ethics and assessment standards into Phase 1, added a pilot phase, and created an operation-level conversion path for licensure-bound majors.

## Diagnosing the whole curriculum

The diagnostic instrument was a complete census of the university's [[curriculum-design|curriculum master tables]] for 2026: 67 program units, of which 12 with an AI or information-technology identity were tabulated separately, leaving 55 majors and 1646 courses. Courses were classed as AI-linkable against three conservative criteria — operations that use AI or data tools, content that covers AI and its disciplinary application, and assignments that require generative AI, data analysis or programming — and then sorted into a four-type typology: tool- and practicum-centered courses, convergence project and problem-solving courses, discipline-specific AI application courses, and a fourth type of latent candidates whose AI potential cannot be confirmed from documents alone.

Two coders established reliability: after a calibration round on 30 items, the second coder blind-coded a stratified sample of 325 courses, reaching 90.2% agreement and Cohen's κ = 0.82. Of the 32 disagreements, 25 fell at the boundary of the provisional fourth type. Across the curriculum, 25 courses (1.5%) were explicitly articulated and only 13 of 55 majors offered one; explicit provision was concentrated in teacher education, where 4.3% of courses (9 of 209) were AI-articulated against 1.1% elsewhere. The share of latent courses differed significantly across major clusters (χ²(14) = 133.40, p < 0.001, Cramér's V = 0.29), running from 1.9% in teacher education to 30.3% in design and media. Comparing the 2025 and 2026 revision cohorts added a limited longitudinal signal: acting arts moved from no explicitly AI-articulated course to five, and newly established programs launched with AI courses embedded.

The authors draw four structural conclusions: AI-linked courses sit at the level of individual courses with little inter-major cooperation and no university-level architecture; AI use is absent from titles and documents, so the institution cannot manage what it cannot see; common infrastructure and support are missing; and provision skews toward practicum and project courses rather than foundational theory.

## What program leaders said

The [[self-report-measures|needs survey]] went to the full population of 67 department heads and program chairs and drew 27 responses (40.3%), gathered online over February 2026 through ten items on necessity, concerns, current operation, preferred forms and required support. Two-thirds (66.7%) judged major-level AI convergence necessary, 29.6% were neutral and one respondent called it unnecessary. Their concerns were operational rather than philosophical: educational infrastructure such as devices, platforms and licenses (59.3%), insufficient administrative and financial support (48.1%) and uncertainty about learning effects (44.4%), followed by insufficient AI competency among instructors and learners (37.0%). Slightly more than half (55.6%) reported that their major ran no AI-linked courses.

On design, converting existing courses (55.6%) and extracurricular programs (55.6%) beat establishing new courses (37.0%); discipline-specific AI literacy and data insight ranked first among design elements (59.3%). For completion, AI modules inside the practice track led (59.3%), well ahead of a convergence minor (14.8%) or double major (7.4%). And 77.8% asked first for AI platform and software-license support. The pattern, the authors argue, locates the decisive condition in university-level common infrastructure rather than in individual instructor will.

## From diagnosis to the CROSS AX framework

Read as [[change-management|institutional change]], the design response rejects two alternatives — a stand-alone AI major and AI taught in depth as content — in favor of a CROSS AX professional whose disciplinary expertise is crossed with AI application capability. Three stages carry students from [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] to applied capability: AX-Foundation (common courses in [[generative-ai|generative AI]] use, data-based decision-making, and AI literacy and ethics), AX-Bridge (existing core courses converted rather than replaced) and AX-Field Lab (capstone work on authentic field problems). Because many majors cannot rename nationally standardized courses, expert review added a dual conversion path: title-level conversion, and an operation-level path in which at least two of methods, assignments and assessment embed AI, documented through a syllabus checklist and an AX tag in the curriculum table.

A five-phase roadmap sequences the institutional work from 2026 to 2030: Foundation Building establishes [[institutional-ai-infrastructure|shared platforms]], licenses, ethics and assessment standards, incentives and governance; Pilot Application runs two to four departments mixing confirmed readiness with latent capacity; Operational Diffusion scales to twenty-five programs and certifies excellent courses; Full Diffusion and Quality Management extends to all majors with portfolio-based certification; Sustainable Ecosystem institutionalizes industry-linked projects, career support and shared regional provision. Five experts (mean 23.4 years of experience) reviewed the drafts in two written rounds. In the first round the framework's universality (I-CVI = 0.40) and the roadmap's feasibility (I-CVI = 0.60) fell below criterion and drove the revisions; in the second round, with the same panel, all ten criteria reached I-CVI = 1.00. The authors read that as confirmation that the concerns were addressed, not as independent validation.

## What this means for practice

- **Administrators.** Sequence the investment before the mandate: shared platforms, licenses, incentives and assessment standards come first, because conversion without infrastructure reproduces the fragmentation the diagnosis found.
- **Designers.** Start from the institution's own curriculum master tables with a document-based coding pass. It costs little and separates what is already explicit from what is merely latent, which changes the feasibility calculus.
- **Faculty developers.** Build operation-level conversion for licensure-bound majors, where methods, assignments and assessment can change even when course titles cannot.
- **Instructors.** Frame AI convergence as disciplinary problem solving rather than extra content, matching the leaders' preference for discipline-specific AI literacy and data insight over tool training.
- **Researchers.** Verify the latent category at syllabus level and evaluate learning effects as converted courses run; both are named next steps, and the study supplies no outcome evidence of its own.

## Limitations

- A single-institution case: the typology, framework and roadmap are transferable instruments whose generalization is untested elsewhere.
- The census read curriculum documents, so fourth-type potential is provisional and requires syllabus-level verification.
- The survey reached 27 of 67 program leaders (40.3%); the modest response and small number preclude inferential statistics and leave non-response bias open, and respondents are not the wider faculty or students.
- This is a diagnosis and a design, not an evaluation: no learning-effect evidence exists yet, the very uncertainty leaders ranked among their leading concerns.
- Four of the five expert reviewers came from the case institution, and the second round used the same panel, so the uniform ratings confirm internal consensus rather than independent judgment.
- Equity, student learning, enrollment and institutional sustainability were not measured; the arguments built on them are hypotheses for implementation research.
