> **Synthesis:** Al-Harthi and colleagues (2026) ask what leads pre-service teachers to use AI responsibly, surveying 618 fourth- and fifth-year students at one Omani university and testing a mediation model in which perceived institutional support for AI builds [[self-efficacy|AI self-efficacy]], which in turn supports [[ethics|digital citizenship practices]]. Institutional support was positively associated with both confidence and conduct, and the indirect path through confidence was statistically significant, consistent with partial mediation. The confidence pathway was real but modest — institutional support explained 2.9% of the variance in AI self-efficacy against 40.6% of the variance in citizenship practices — and participants rated citizenship practices highest (mean 4.150 on a five-point scale) and institutional support lowest (3.516). The study moves institutional AI support research beyond adoption and performance toward a professional and ethical outcome, and it answers a noted evidence gap in Arab Gulf contexts, where national AI agendas now name responsible use as policy. For [[teacher-education]], the lesson is that [[educational-development|professional development]] workshops alone will not convert knowledge into conduct.

*Cross-sectional survey of 618 pre-service teachers at one public university in Oman, analyzed with covariance-based structural equation modeling; the modeled paths are statistical associations rather than demonstrated causation.*

## Key Findings

- **Institutional support was associated with both confidence and conduct.** Perceived institutional support for AI was positively associated with AI self-efficacy and with digital citizenship practices.
- **Confidence carried part of the link.** The indirect association from institutional support through AI self-efficacy to digital citizenship practices was statistically significant, consistent with partial mediation.
- **The confidence path was modest.** Institutional support accounted for 2.9% of the variance in AI self-efficacy but 40.6% of the variance in digital citizenship practices.
- **Support was the weakest-rated construct.** Participants reported the most moderate perceived institutional support (mean 3.516), below AI self-efficacy (3.942) and digital citizenship practices (4.150).
- **Citizenship was measured as practice.** Six items captured information verification, respectful interaction, and equitable participation rather than the full multidimensional construct.
- **The context is underexplored.** The sample came from one public university in Oman, a region underrepresented in [[higher-ed|higher education]] AI research.

## Two theories, one mediation model

The paper grounds its model in organizational support theory and social cognitive theory. Organizational support theory reads institutional provision as investment in pre-service teachers' development, which invites constructive engagement with the practices the institution endorses. Social cognitive theory supplies the mechanism: support provides mastery experiences, vicarious models, and persuasion from which efficacy beliefs develop. Perceived institutional support therefore occupies the environmental corner of the model, AI self-efficacy the personal corner, and digital citizenship practices the behavioral corner. Perceived support was measured with four items covering resources, training, responsible integration, and explicit policy; AI self-efficacy with five items; and digital citizenship with six practice-oriented items, all on a five-point scale. The authors argue that formal instruction often fails to convert knowledge into conduct, citing a cluster-randomized digital citizenship curriculum that improved students' knowledge and self-efficacy more consistently than their behavior. That knowledge-practice gap is the problem the mediation model is built to explain, and it is why the study treats institutional conditions rather than individual deficit as the lever.

## How much of the path runs through confidence

The mediation was partial: institutional support predicted citizenship practices both directly and indirectly, and both estimates were positive and statistically significant. The indirect effect was small relative to the direct one, and the model explained only 2.9% of the variance in AI self-efficacy against 40.6% of the variance in citizenship practices. The authors read this as evidence that confidence is one pathway among several, and they name [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]], ethical awareness, policy clarity, institutional trust, and perceptions of usefulness and ease of use as unmeasured candidates. Measurement was a further constraint. Convergent validity was strongest for AI self-efficacy, whose average variance extracted reached .569, while the values for perceived institutional support (.471) and digital citizenship practices (.485) fell slightly below the .50 benchmark. Common method variance could not be ruled out, although Harman's single-factor test explained 35.59% of item variance.

## An underexplored context

The study was conducted at a single public university in Oman with 618 fourth- and fifth-year pre-service teachers, a whole-population recruitment that yielded a 69.8% response rate. Oman matters for two reasons. Its National Program for Artificial Intelligence and Advanced Digital Technologies, approved in September 2024 and extending through 2026, combines sectoral adoption with a human capital agenda that includes training graduates, educating teachers and school students, and introducing AI-related curricula. The sample itself was diverse within the college: 205 participants (33.2%) studied Arabic language and humanities, 90 (14.6%) English language education, 172 (27.8%) science, mathematics, and technology-related programs, and 151 (24.4%) other programs including art and physical education. Its General Policy for the Safe and Ethical Use of Artificial Intelligence Systems foregrounds fairness, transparency, accountability, inclusiveness, privacy, and safety. The authors also note that higher education technology adoption research remains concentrated in Asia, North America, and Western Europe, leaving the Arab Gulf underrepresented. Because the constructs were adapted into Arabic through forward- and back-translation, the findings describe an understudied combination of institutional ambition and cultural context rather than a generic model.

## What this means for practice

- **[[teacher-role|Instructors]].** Treat digital citizenship as practiced conduct, not a policy statement: build information verification, disclosure of AI-assisted work, and data protection into tasks.
- **Faculty developers.** Pair [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] teaching with structured practice and technical assistance, because confidence carried only a modest share of the path.
- **Administrators.** Coordinate infrastructure, training, ethical guidance, and [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] opportunity rather than relying on isolated workshops.
- **Institutions.** Set explicit expectations for disclosure, authorship, algorithmic bias, equitable access, and human oversight, and reinforce them through authentic assessment.

## Limitations

- The cross-sectional design restricts causal interpretation; the mediation findings are statistical indirect associations and cannot establish temporal order.
- All three constructs were [[self-report-measures|self-reported]] in one questionnaire, so common method bias and social desirability bias cannot be ruled out despite anonymity and Harman's test.
- Convergent validity was marginal for two constructs: average variance extracted was .471 for perceived institutional support and .485 for digital citizenship practices, below the .50 benchmark.
- The six-item digital citizenship measure captured the practice-oriented Skills and Practice component without the full multidimensional breadth of critical digital citizenship.
- The sample was fourth- and fifth-year students at a single public university in Oman, which limits generalizability across institutions, program levels, and cultural settings.
