> **Synthesis:** The first wave of a new panel survey finds that undergraduates use AI extensively for tasks that appear to help learning and for tasks that appear to undermine it. Respondents describe a large gap between their own misuse of AI and what they believe their peers do. They broadly agree that AI harms writing and makes take-home work less fair, while also valuing it for real-time conceptual explanations, practice quizzes and study guides. Most Dartmouth respondents expect AI to change the nature of jobs rather than eliminate them, and most report having taught themselves the AI skills they use. The authors argue that rules and teaching should vary by discipline. All of these are self-reports from a working paper, not measured learning outcomes.

## Key Findings
1. **Helpful and harmful uses coexist.** Students report frequent use both for explaining concepts and generating study aids and for drafting essays, copying text with little editing and finding problem set answers without understanding them.
2. **The self-peer gap is large.** Respondents report much more misuse by peers than by themselves, and the gap persists in an anonymous sample.
3. **Writing and take-home work draw the most concern.** Strong majorities agree that AI harms writing and that out-of-class work has become less fair, with many feeling pressure to use AI to keep up.
4. **Students report clear learning benefits.** They value real-time answers to conceptual questions, practice quizzes, study guides and faster progress on research projects.
5. **Job change dominates job loss.** Most Dartmouth respondents say AI is changing the nature of jobs rather than eliminating them.
6. **Discipline shapes the picture.** Views on learning, harm and career value differ by division, so the authors argue that rules must vary by context.
7. **Skills are self-taught but teaching is wanted.** Most students taught themselves, yet many ask for formal AI training in courses and workshops.

## What the survey measured
The first wave ran from June 2026 through September 2026 across three groups: 1,201 current Dartmouth undergraduates, 332 incoming Dartmouth first-years in the class of 2030 surveyed before arrival, and 1,003 U.S. college students from the College Pulse panel at four-year colleges and universities. Each group received a similar but not identical survey, tailored to the institution most recently attended. Dartmouth students were emailed and offered a \$20 gift card; those surveys ran on Qualtrics, while College Pulse fielded the national survey.

The Dartmouth sample is 21 percent Pell eligible, matching the institution-wide rate, but skewed toward female students (56 percent versus 50 percent in the population) and international students (26 percent versus 13 percent). Respondents checked tasks they use AI for and tasks they believe peers use it for, then rated statements about learning, assessment, guidance and careers. The harmful-use questions were deliberately explicit. These are self-reports and perceptions about [[student-ai-interaction|student AI use]], not observed behavior or learning gains.

## What the responses show
For potentially harmful uses, Table 2 shows a consistent self-peer gap among Dartmouth undergraduates. Some 17.0 percent say they draft essays or papers with AI while 63.4 percent say peers do; 5.4 percent admit copy-pasting text with little editing or reflection versus 37.6 percent for peers; 9.5 percent report finding problem set answers without understanding them versus 41.6 percent for peers; and 43.2 percent report summarizing assigned readings versus 75.5 percent for peers. The prose rounds these to "Only 17 percent," "5.4 percent" and "Nine-and-one-half percent."

The authors caution against reading the gap as simple dishonesty. It does not shrink in the anonymous College Pulse sample, and focus groups produced an even mix of not wanting to self-incriminate and overestimating peer cheating. They cite meta-analytic evidence that underreporting of sensitive behavior runs at about 40 percent for the most sensitive acts, and note that scaling self-reports by a factor of 2x still leaves a large gap. They also cannot rule out reports of the worst peer behavior observed rather than a typical peer.

For potentially helpful uses, Table 3 reports 52.5 percent of Dartmouth students checking grammar themselves versus 69.2 percent for peers, 66.4 percent versus 72.3 percent for explaining difficult concepts, and 56.5 percent versus 66.4 percent for generating practice quizzes or study aids; the prose gives these as "Fifty-three percent," "Two thirds" and "Fifty seven percent." Students are less likely to use AI as a confidant. The conclusion frames real-time conceptual explanations as a way to get office-hours-style questions answered when faculty are unavailable.

On writing and assessment, Table 4 reports 92.3 percent agreement among Dartmouth respondents [1,136] and 84.3 percent among U.S. college students [1,003] that AI use for writing can harm writing skills, a difference of 7.9 (t = 4.5); the abstract says "Ninety-two percent of students agree or strongly agree that use of AI is harmful to writing." Agreement that take-home work is increasingly unfair is 69.9 percent and 69.6 percent, and 39.9 percent and 38.7 percent feel they must use AI because peers do. Stated instructor rules guide use for 75.0 percent of Dartmouth students and 71.2 percent of U.S. college students. Table 5 has 48.1 percent of arts and humanities students, 70.1 percent of scientists and 67.1 percent of social scientists agreeing that AI helps students understand concepts more deeply.

Table 8 shows 55.8 percent of Dartmouth students and 56.6 percent of U.S. college students believing AI is reducing jobs in their field, versus 33.4 percent and 31.6 percent who think it is creating jobs. But 84.1 percent of Dartmouth respondents agree that AI is changing the nature of jobs without necessarily eliminating them, stated in the abstract as "Eighty-four percent"; among incoming students it is 87.5 percent. Strong AI skills are seen as important by 74.0 percent of Dartmouth students and 54.9 percent of U.S. college students, and by 56.3 percent of arts and humanities students versus 77.7 percent of scientists and 78.8 percent of social scientists.

Finally, Table 12 reports 85.8 percent of Dartmouth students saying they mostly developed their AI skills outside formal coursework, described in the text as 86 percent, versus 79.3 percent of U.S. college students. Only 52.7 percent of Dartmouth students say faculty gave meaningful guidance on effective and ethical AI use, and 38.3 percent say the institution is adequately preparing them for AI in a future career. In Table 13, 15.0 percent unprompted raised a for-credit course or required workshop on AI use, 23.2 percent asked for more technical applications and 23.5 percent for help writing better prompts. The authors read this as strong demand for [[curriculum-design|curriculum integration]] and [[ai-literacy|AI skills teaching]].

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Use class time and office hours for human-to-human discussion of key concepts, since students already report using AI for real-time explanations and routine research support.
- **Instructors.** Make permitted and prohibited AI uses explicit on each assignment: the survey reports that stated rules guide how most students use AI.
- **Instructors.** Consider proctored, paper-and-pencil assessments where out-of-class work is central to a course, given the reported concerns about take-home fairness.
- **Curriculum designers.** Build formal AI skills instruction into courses and workshops rather than leaving students to teach themselves, and ask students to evaluate AI output against other sources.
- **Administrators.** Set AI guidance at the discipline and course level rather than with a single blanket rule, since attitudes toward harm and career value differ sharply by field.

## Limitations
- This is a working paper and has not been peer reviewed.
- All measures are self-reports of behavior, attitudes and perceived peer behavior. Self-presentational bias is a live concern: students may underreport their own misuse, and the paper notes that the self-peer gap survives anonymity and may reflect overestimation of peers.
- The paper measures no actual learning outcomes and no verified cheating rates; "unfairness" and "harm" are respondent perceptions, not experimental estimates.
- The Dartmouth sample comes from one selective institution and is skewed toward female and international students relative to its own population. The College Pulse panel covers four-year college students, not all undergraduates.
- Free-response questions drew smaller subsamples, such as the 294 incoming students who described high-school AI rules and the 764 who commented on institutional guidance.
- This is only the first wave of a panel. The employer survey is described as still in the field, and the authors state that future waves will probe the self-peer gap more precisely. Dartmouth College funded the survey costs.
