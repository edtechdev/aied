> **Synthesis:** A cross-sectional survey of 420 undergraduate and [[graduate-education|postgraduate]] sport management students in Türkiye examined how perceived digital competence relates to [[ai-literacy]] and related competencies. Aggregate scores were positively associated, but the five digital competence dimensions showed a differentiated pattern across core AI Literacy, Create AI, AI [[self-efficacy]] and AI Self-Competency, with local effects ranging from negligible to small. Students who reported formal [[ai-education|AI education]] scored higher only on core AI Literacy after correction for multiple comparisons, while students reporting AI tool use scored higher on four outcomes. No gender differences survived correction. The authors emphasize that these are unadjusted associations from one convenience sample and do not show that any competency causes another.

## Key Findings
1. **One convenience sample in Türkiye.** Students from five universities completed an online questionnaire during the 2025–2026 academic year.
2. **Two multidimensional instruments were used.** Digital competence was measured with 29 items and AI-related competencies with 34 items covering four domains.
3. **The aggregate association was positive but incomplete.** Higher perceived digital competence accompanied higher overall AI-related competence, leaving most of the variance unexplained at the aggregate level.
4. **The dimension-level pattern was uneven.** Different digital competence dimensions correlated with different AI-related domains, and the significant local effects were small.
5. **Formal education was selective.** After correction, only core AI Literacy differed between students who did and did not report formal AI education.
6. **Reported AI use was more widespread.** Students reporting AI tool use scored higher on the overall AI measure, Create AI, AI Self-Efficacy and digital content development.
7. **No gender differences remained.** All Holm-adjusted comparisons across the AI-related and digital competence outcomes were nonsignificant.

## What was measured

Responses were collected between 23 June and 27 July 2026. From 432 returns, 12 incomplete submissions were removed, leaving 420 participants: 240 male (57.1%) and 180 female (42.9%). Most were aged 18–21 (56.7%), followed by 22–24 (32.6%) and 25 or older (10.7%); 23 postgraduates made up 5.5% of the sample. Formal AI education was reported by 102 students (24.3%) and AI tool use by 306 (72.9%). Institutional affiliation was not recorded at respondent level, and a response rate could not be calculated.

The Turkish COBADI® scale assessed five digital competence dimensions on a four-point scale: Information and [[statistics-and-data-science-education|Data Literacy]], Digital Content Development, Communication, Problem-Solving, and University Virtual Tools and Social Communication. The 34-item Meta AI Literacy Scale was translated into Turkish and measured core AI Literacy, Create AI, AI Self-Efficacy and AI Self-Competency on a 0 to 10 scale. Both instruments showed acceptable reliability and fit in this sample, with alpha coefficients from 0.801 to 0.965 and average variance extracted values from 0.567 to 0.848. The Turkish version was not independently cross-culturally validated.

## What the associations showed

The aggregate COBADI® score was positively associated with the aggregate MAILS score (β = 0.438, p < 0.001), accounting for 19.2% of variance (R² = 0.192, adjusted R² = 0.190), a medium effect (f² = 0.238). The five digital dimensions jointly accounted for 24.1% of variance in AI Literacy, 21.8% in AI Self-Efficacy, 13.7% in Create AI and 12.6% in AI Self-Competency. All HTMT values were below 0.85, and Harman's single-factor test gave 31.4% of variance to the first factor.

After false discovery rate correction across 20 pathways, the largest observed coefficients were Problem-Solving for AI Literacy (β = 0.286, 95% CI [0.181, 0.398], FDR-adjusted p < 0.005, f² = 0.064), Information and Data Literacy for Create AI (β = 0.194, 95% CI [0.088, 0.307], FDR-adjusted p = 0.005, f² = 0.029), Digital Content Development for AI Self-Efficacy (β = 0.247, 95% CI [0.143, 0.356], FDR-adjusted p < 0.005, f² = 0.049), and Communication for AI Self-Competency (β = 0.184, 95% CI [0.081, 0.292], FDR-adjusted p = 0.005, f² = 0.028). The authors warn that these were not formally compared, so no dimension can be called stronger than another. Significant local effects ranged from f² = 0.016 to f² = 0.064.

After Holm correction, the formal-education groups differed only on AI Literacy (Cohen's d = 0.357). The AI tool-use groups differed on MAILS Aggregate (Cohen's d = 0.393), Create AI (Cohen's d = 0.461), AI Self-Efficacy (Cohen's d = 0.462) and Digital Content Development (Cohen's d = 0.365). All 11 gender comparisons had Holm-adjusted p values of 1.000, with effect sizes from |Cohen's d| = 0.029 to 0.148.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Treat general digital skills and AI-specific competence as related but distinct when planning sport management coursework.
- **Instructors.** Consider [[discipline-specific-aied|discipline-specific]] tasks in event planning, fan [[learning-analytics|analytics]] and digital content production as contexts for future evaluation, while recognizing that this study did not test their effectiveness.
- **Curriculum designers.** Avoid assuming that a short formal AI course changes more than AI Literacy; the survey cannot identify which instructional features mattered.
- **Researchers.** Measure the type, purpose and frequency of AI use instead of a binary user/non-user item, and test these associations with [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal]] or intervention designs.

## Limitations
- The cross-sectional design does not support causal claims; students with stronger competencies may have been more likely to use AI tools.
- Convenience sampling through student groups and academic staff limits generalization, and duplicate participation could not be independently verified.
- Formal AI education and AI tool use were single binary [[self-report-measures|self-report]] items with no detail on duration, quality, tool type or purpose.
- Postgraduates were only 5.5% of the sample and were analyzed with undergraduates, with no separate sensitivity analysis.
- The Turkish MAILS was evaluated in the same sample used for the substantive analyses, without separate pilot testing, cognitive interviewing or independent cross-cultural validation.
- Some conceptual overlap between COBADI® Problem-Solving and MAILS AI [[problem-solving|Problem Solving]] may have contributed to the observed associations, and [[primary-education|primary]] models were not adjusted for covariates.
