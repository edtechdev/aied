> **Synthesis:** Mirpour and Behdarvandirad built and validated two three-item scales for the motivational question [[generative-ai|generative AI]] has made unavoidable: whether students still value learning material themselves when a tool can produce the work, and whether they believe they can learn without it. Across 882 US [[higher-ed|undergraduates]], the two constructs separated cleanly in factor analysis (CFI = .993, RMSEA = .041), held across traditional-age and [[adult-learning|adult learners]], and predicted effort and persistence beyond conventional task value and [[self-efficacy|self-efficacy]]. Students who used AI more scored lower on both, and just over half of AI use's negative association with effort ran indirectly through them, with no direct path remaining. The authors' practical claim is that [[motivation|motivation]] in AI-rich contexts is the variable to measure, not the amount of AI use.

## Key Findings

- **Two constructs, not one.** A two-factor model beat a one-factor model and an orthogonal two-factor model, so valuing learning in AI contexts and feeling capable without AI are distinct dispositions rather than two names for confidence.
- **Both scales are short and psychometrically adequate.** Three items each, with internal consistency at α = .80 for learning value and α = .72 for learning self-efficacy.
- **They measure the same thing across traditional-age and adult learners.** A partial scalar invariance model held across 469 traditional-age and 407 adult students.
- **More AI use goes with less of both.** Learning value correlated with AI use at r = −.30 and learning self-efficacy at r = −.32, while both correlated only moderately with their classical counterparts.
- **The constructs predict effort and persistence, not grades.** Adding them explained more variance in effort expenditure and persistence intention, but added nothing for GPA.
- **AI use's association with effort runs through the motivational beliefs.** Once both constructs entered the model, the direct AI-use path fell to non-significance, with just over half its total association carried indirectly.
- **The items are narrow and specific.** They ask whether learning the material oneself is worthwhile when tools can answer quickly, and whether one can rely on one's own ability when tasks are challenging.


## How the scales were developed

The authors wrote item pools for both constructs and collected survey data from 882 undergraduates at a US university, with roughly balanced traditional-age and adult representation. Three items with mixed loadings or weak associations were dropped before the main analyses, leaving six items. The sample was split, with exploratory factor analysis on 431 students and confirmatory factor analysis on 445.

The two factors correlated at .74 in the EFA and .85 at the latent level in the CFA, which is what makes them distinct constructs rather than two names for the same disposition. Fit was robust CFI = .993, TLI = .988, RMSEA = .041 and SRMR = .024, and both competing models fit worse (Δχ²[1] = 16.23, p < .001 against one factor; Δχ²[1] = 105.10, p < .001 against orthogonal). Partial scalar invariance across student type held at CFI = .995 (Δχ²[3] = 1.11, p = .774). Criterion validity came from hierarchical regressions controlling classical task value, classical self-efficacy, student type and AI use, which added ΔR² = .022 (p < .001) for effort and ΔR² = .015 (p < .001) for persistence but ΔR² = .003 (p = .311) for GPA.

## Why this reframes the AI-use debate

Institutional concern about generative AI usually targets the volume of use, and this study's central claim is that volume is a poor instrument. AI use carried no association with GPA and almost none with persistence once the two motivational constructs were in the model, and its whole apparent effect on effort ran through them. Students who value learning less when a tool can do the task, and who doubt they can learn without it, both use AI more and invest less; the causal direction is not established, and the authors say so.

The measurement logic also shifts where the questions go. An institution adopting a tool or a policy can administer six items before and after and see whether the change moved how students value their own learning, which is a different and more decision-relevant signal than counting prompts.

The second half of the argument is about direction of concern. If the value of learning and confidence in one's own capability are what predict effort, then the goal is not less AI use but [[self-efficacy|self-efficacy]] and task value that survive AI use — a claim the [[motivation|motivation]] literature has long made about autonomy-supportive conditions.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors:** ask what a student believes about learning without the tool, not just how much they use it. These six items are short enough to run at the start and end of a course.
- **Instructors:** design tasks that require unaided explanation or retrieval, so the belief that one can learn without help has evidence behind it.
- **Faculty developers:** use the two constructs to frame AI guidance. Framing AI as a tool that saves work while leaving learning untouched addresses the value side; framing it as something students cannot manage without addresses the efficacy side.
- **Administrators:** measure motivational correlates before and after an AI policy change rather than relying on usage logs. The scales are brief enough for an institutional pulse survey.
- **Researchers:** use the scales where AI-contextualized motivation is a mediator; the exploratory path model suggests AI use's effect on effort is largely carried by them.

## Limitations

- The design is cross-sectional, so the erosion and self-selection readings of the AI-use association cannot be distinguished; the authors name this as the study's most important open question.
- All measures are self-reported, including effort expenditure and persistence intention; no behavioral or grade-based criteria beyond self-reported GPA were used.
- LSE-AI's average variance extracted was .46, below the conventional .50 threshold, which the authors attribute to the three-item length.
- The sample is 882 undergraduates at one US institution, and replicating in other cultural contexts is left to future work.
- Task value's cost dimension is not represented in the brief LV-AI scale, so the scale does not capture the cost side of the value construct.
