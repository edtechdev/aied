> **Synthesis:** Williams and Prishtina (2026) critique [[chng-ai-instructional-design-2023|Ch'ng's (2023) study]], *How AI Makes its Mark on Instructional Design*, which they describe as a mixed-methods study of 300 educators and students claiming successful AI integration across the five ADDIE phases for online course design. Their method is a [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|systematic review]] of literature published between 2019 and 2024 plus a phase-by-phase deconstruction, and their verdict is more affirming than adversarial: Ch'ng's sample size and mixed design are counted as strengths, and the assembled literature is read as converging on the same benefits. The objections concern what the study measures. Return on investment is a cost figure, and the authors argue that [[assessment-validity|validity]] needs evidence it cannot supply; resource availability varies enough across institutions to limit generalizability; and every ADDIE phase leaves out a condition of use — tool access in analysis, educator training in design, curriculum fit in development, connectivity and software compatibility in implementation, and creativity and critical thinking in evaluation. What remains is a set of cautions for [[learning-design|learning designers]] rather than a refutation, and the authors say so.

## Key Findings

- **The critique accepts Ch'ng's headline result and disputes its reach.** The critique reports that Ch'ng's study combined quantitative surveys with qualitative interviews across 300 educators and students, finding that AI tools enhanced [[personalized-learning|personalized learning]], improved engagement and produced better academic outcomes, and it treats the sample size and the mixed design as genuine strengths.
- **Return on investment is the measurement problem the critique names first.** Using ROI as the primary measure of success, the authors argue, may not capture the qualitative benefits of AI integration, which is a claim about what the study can conclude rather than about what it found.
- **Resource availability is the generalizability problem.** Institutions differ in budget, infrastructure and staff expertise, so a design that works where tools and support are already in place cannot be assumed to transfer, and the critique asks for that variation to be considered explicitly rather than noted in passing.
- **Each of the five ADDIE phases is read as leaving out a condition of use.** Analysis excludes tools some educators cannot access; design does not address educators who lack AI training; development does not consider integration into existing curricula; implementation overlooks connectivity, software compatibility and user training; and evaluation does not explore AI's limits on creativity and [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]].
- **Two biases are flagged for anyone replicating the work.** Selection bias, if later samples skew toward students with reliable technology access, and response bias, if participants report overly positive views of AI tools through social desirability or shallow engagement, either of which would inflate the apparent effect.
- **The supporting literature is presented as convergent rather than mixed.** Crompton and Burke's review of 138 peer-reviewed articles published between 2016 and 2022, Bhutoria on personalized learning across three educational contexts, Seo and colleagues on learner–instructor interaction, and Akgun and Greenhow on ethics are all read as reinforcing Ch'ng's conclusions, with the ethical cautions repeated in each.
- **The recommendations are for institutions, not for the model.** The authors propose a framework for nuanced integration built on ethical considerations, continuous feedback and collaboration between educators and AI systems, plus professional development, equitable access as a policy requirement, and future studies that report effect sizes, run longitudinally and follow [[universal-design-for-learning|Universal Design for Learning]].
- **The critique's own conclusion is that the original stands.** Ch'ng's study is called a pivotal contribution and an exemplary study, and the value claimed for the critique is that it keeps those conclusions actionable by attaching the conditions under which they hold.

## How the study was done

The method is a [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|systematic review]] presented as a meta-analysis. The authors searched SpringerLink, ResearchGate and academic journals for work on AI in instructional design using keywords including "AI in instructional design", "AI and pedagogy", "ADDIE model" and "ethical considerations in AI", and restricted inclusion to peer-reviewed journal articles, book chapters and conference papers published between 2019 and 2024 that addressed AI in instructional design, higher education or online learning. Full-text availability was a condition of inclusion, and opinion pieces and editorials were excluded. Ten studies appear in the appendix table, among them Ch'ng (2023), Crompton and Burke (2023), Bhutoria (2022), Seo et al. (2021), Akgun and Greenhow (2021), Meng et al. (2019) and Xu and Ouyang (2021).

Extraction used a coding framework covering AI applications, instructional design models including ADDIE and the Successive Approximation Model, ethical considerations, emerging roles, methodologies and key findings, and a separate thematic scheme supported the qualitative synthesis and is reproduced in a third appendix. The evaluation of Ch'ng's study was organized around four questions: alignment with foundational instructional design theory, practical implications, the new roles AI introduces, and ethical implications. No pooled statistic is computed. The section headed as a quantitative synthesis compares studies and notes where their conclusions agree, and the phase-by-phase analysis is the authors' reading of what Ch'ng's account includes and omits.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructional designers:** treat a vendor or pilot claim of efficiency as a question about measurement. The critique's most portable point is that ROI answers a cost question and cannot answer whether the learning experience improved, so a design decision needs a second criterion — completion, revision quality, or the judgment of the people who teach with the output.
- **Administrators:** budget the support, not just the license. The phase-by-phase list is effectively an implementation checklist: tool access, training, curriculum fit, connectivity, software compatibility and evaluation of higher-order outcomes, each of which fails somewhere in the account and none of which is a property of the AI tool itself.
- **Faculty developers:** the recommendation that survives is ordinary professional development on ethical use, tool capability and collaborative design, which is the same conclusion the faculty-survey literature reaches from the other direction.
- **Researchers:** report effect sizes and follow cohorts over time. The authors note that the reviewed studies rarely quantify AI's effect and almost never track it beyond a single term, which is why a five-phase account of what AI could do in design still rests on self-report.

## Limitations

- The paper presents itself as a meta-analysis but reports no pooled effect, no confidence interval and no statistical synthesis, so its "quantitative synthesis" is a narrative comparison of findings and the strength of the pattern it describes cannot be read off the text.
- Ten studies, drawn from two databases plus academic journals and restricted to 2019–2024 peer-reviewed work, is a small base for claims about a field, and the authors acknowledge that the reviewed studies may not represent the diversity of educational contexts.
- The critique describes Ch'ng's design inconsistently — mixed-methods surveys and interviews in the summary of the methodology, "rigorous experimental methodologies" later in the same analysis — which matters because the methodological verdict rests on that description.
- Much of the critique affirms rather than tests the original: the same paragraph that lists the study's weaknesses also calls its conclusions consistently supported, so the objections function as conditions on the findings rather than as challenges to them.
- The authors state that the rapid evolution of AI means some insights may quickly become outdated, a limitation that applies with more force to a review whose newest included study is from 2024 than to a primary study with a fixed dataset.
