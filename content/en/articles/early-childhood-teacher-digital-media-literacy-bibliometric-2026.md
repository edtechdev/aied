> **Synthesis:** Janegizova, Mankesh and Taubaeva (2026) map the research literature on digital and [[information-and-media-literacy|media literacy]] in pre-service [[early-childhood-elementary-ai-education|early childhood]] teacher preparation: 86 publications from 2012 to 2025 drawn from Scopus and Web of Science, analyzed with Bibliometrix for productivity, collaboration, citation impact and keyword structure. The field is growing at 18.41% a year, is concentrated in Spain (27 publications, 31.4% of the corpus), and is only weakly international, with 8.14% of publications co-authored across countries. Digital competence and early childhood education sit at the center of the conceptual structure. Media literacy is present but loosely connected to it, and AI topics appear in the corpus without yet forming a thematic cluster of their own. The paper is a description of a field rather than a test of anything, but its gap statement is specific: [[teacher-education|pre-service teacher preparation]] is being reshaped by AI faster than its research base has organized around it.

## Key Findings

1. **The literature is small and accelerating.** 86 documents across 59 sources and 228 authors grew at 18.41% a year over 2012-2025, with 13.77 citations per document on average.
2. **Spain dominates the corpus.** 27 of 86 publications (31.4%) come from Spain, with the United States next, and only 8.14% of publications are internationally co-authored.
3. **Digital competence is the central construct.** Keyword co-occurrence places digital competence and early childhood education at the core of the field's conceptual structure.
4. **Media literacy is adjacent rather than integrated.** It appears as a related strand linked to, but not folded into, the digital competence literature.
5. **AI has not yet formed a cluster.** AI-related topics are present, including among the most-cited works, but remain infrequent and scattered across themes.
6. **The most influential studies measure self-perceived competence.** Several top-cited papers report training gains in self-perceived digital competence rather than in observed practice.
7. **The field's own diagnosis is a readiness gap.** Studies in the corpus find positive attitudes alongside moderate skills and insufficient knowledge, so access and enthusiasm do not convert into pedagogically sound use.

## What a map of this literature shows

Bibliometrics answers a question the individual studies cannot: what the field has actually organized itself around. Here the answer is digital competence, with media literacy as a neighbour and AI as a newcomer. That distribution matters for early childhood specifically, because the adult in the room mediates what a young child encounters, so a teacher's capacity to judge a digital resource is not a generic skill but a developmental one — it requires knowing what a four-year-old can do with a tool and what the tool does to the interaction. The corpus's most-cited studies focus on whether pre-service teachers feel competent, and several report gains after short training interventions in self-perceived competence. That is a weak proxy for the judgment the introduction describes, and the gap between self-report and observed practice is the same one the broader [[teacher-ai-competency|teacher AI competency]] literature documents.

## Where AI fits, and where the gap is

The paper's most useful observation is structural rather than quantitative: AI content enters this literature through the digital competence frame rather than as its own research program. Some of the most-cited work engages AI directly, but the keyword network does not yet show a cluster, which means there is no shared vocabulary, no settled instrument, and little accumulation of findings across studies. For a field preparing teachers who will supervise AI use with children, that is a real problem, since the competencies involved — judging generated content, explaining it to a child, deciding what a tool should be allowed to observe — are not fully covered by a digital competence framework built around ICT integration. The concentration in a handful of countries and the thin international collaboration compound it, because early childhood practice is culturally specific and a competence model developed in one system does not straightforwardly travel.

## What this means for practice

- **Teacher educators.** Audit your early childhood program for the AI-specific competencies that a digital competence framework does not cover — judging generated content with a child's developmental needs in view, and explaining what a tool is doing.
- **Programme designers.** Require evidence of observed practice rather than [[self-report-measures|self-reported]] competence, since the corpus's most-cited interventions measured the latter.
- **Researchers.** Build shared instruments for AI-related competence in early childhood preparation, because the absence of a thematic cluster reflects an absence of common measures.
- **Administrators.** Look past the field's growth rate: a literature concentrated in a few countries and thinly collaborative is a thin evidence base for a curriculum decision.

## Limitations

- The corpus is 86 publications from two databases (Scopus and Web of Science Core Collection), so journals indexed elsewhere and non-English work are largely invisible.
- Bibliometric indicators describe productivity and citation patterns, not study quality; a highly cited paper can carry weak evidence.
- The analysis stops at 2025 to avoid an incomplete year, so the most recent AI-related work is not represented.
- Keyword co-occurrence reflects authors' and indexers' vocabulary choices, so a topic can be present in the literature without appearing as a cluster.
- The thematic interpretation of the ten most-cited publications was coded by the authors, and no reliability check for that coding is reported.
