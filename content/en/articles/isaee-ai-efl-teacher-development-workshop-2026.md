> **Synthesis:** Isaee, Barjesteh, Attia and Elsawy (2026) document a two-day [[professional-training|professional development]] workshop on [[generative-ai|generative AI]] for 62 faculty members, graduate students, teacher trainees and administrators at the Ayatollah Amoli Campus of Islamic Azad University in Amol, Iran — a self-selected group from universities across Mazandaran Province. It used backward design in three phases: an opening panel, hands-on work producing grammar drills, IELTS prompts, lesson plans and role-played AI conversations, then a roundtable; 28 attendees were interviewed during five-to-twelve-minute breaks. Three themes came back: efficiency and workload relief, [[academic-integrity|integrity]] worries about plagiarism and [[cognitive-offloading|overreliance]], and access and equity gaps between urban and rural institutions. The authors grant that the agenda was built around efficiency, ethics and [[equity-in-ai-education|equity]], so the themes probably reflect the design as much as unprompted priorities, and that nothing measures time saved, learning, or classroom use. Read it as a design case for [[global-south|Global South]] teacher development, not evidence that AI training changes practice.

## Key Findings

- **Sixty-two attended; 28 were interviewed.** Faculty, graduate students, teacher trainees and administrators registered voluntarily from universities across Mazandaran Province; interviews were opportunistic and brief, five to twelve minutes during breaks.
- **The workshop was built backward from outcomes.** Two days at four hours each, from 21 February 2024, ran an opening panel, hands-on grammar drills, IELTS prompts, lesson plans and role-played conversations, then a roundtable.
- **Efficiency was the enthusiastic theme.** Interviewees described role-play dialogues in minutes rather than hours, instant comprehension questions and leveled speaking prompts, calling AI an assistant that never tires.
- **Integrity concerns centered on plagiarism and overreliance.** Participants worried that copied ChatGPT text would stop students thinking for themselves, that homework would displace learning, and that unchecked inaccurate outputs could pass.
- **Access and equity split urban from rural institutions.** Interviewees contrasted Tehran students reaching advanced tools through VPNs with rural peers lacking stable internet, devices or Wi-Fi, and said any initiative must close that gap.
- **Hands-on use mostly streamlined existing practice.** The organizers' impressionistic reading placed most participants at substitution and augmentation on [[samr-model|SAMR]], graduate students at modification, and none at redefinition; no instrument was administered.

## The workshop and its participants

The event ran two days, four hours a day, at the Ayatollah Amoli Campus of Islamic Azad University after ethics approval, with invitations circulated through departmental mailing lists, notice boards and professional networks across Mazandaran Province; all 62 registrants were accepted. Organizers sorted them into faculty, graduate students, teacher trainees and administrators to balance small-group work. About two-thirds were women, mean age was 31.0 years (SD = 6.4), and experience in teaching or study ran from one to twenty years. The authors note everyone came by choice: a self-selected group organized by role, not a stratified sample of Iranian [[english-education|English]] teachers, and the 28 interviewed are a subset of the 62.

## The challenges participants named

Three constraints recurred. Infrastructure came first: internet access is often unstable, several international platforms remain partially restricted under sanctions, and interviewees contrasted metropolitan students reaching advanced tools through VPNs with rural peers who lacked a stable connection or a working device. Assessment culture came second: large, exam-oriented classes leave teachers reluctant to assign work they cannot verify, and participants said [[educational-policy-ai|institutional guidance]] was missing. Integrity came third: plagiarism was already a live problem, and participants expected generative tools to make it easier while giving them no shared standard for assistance versus substitution. The organizers answered infrastructure with offline demonstrations and [[open-source|open-source]] alternatives, and integrity with an ethics panel that also reassured skeptical senior faculty — accommodations, not fixes.

## The opportunities and what teachers produced

The most concrete material is the output. Hands-on sessions produced grammar drills, IELTS writing prompts, leveled lesson plans and role-played AI-assisted conversations, supported by [[prompt-engineering|prompt-engineering]] guides, sample AI-based lesson designs, case studies and ethics reference sheets. Interviewees valued speed: role-play dialogues in minutes rather than hours, instant comprehension questions, speaking prompts matched to student level. The organizers read that as streamlining existing practice, with AI-supported [[personalized-learning|personalization]] appearing only among graduate students and nobody describing redefinition. Prompt design emerged as the transferable skill, and culturally appropriate prompts and group projects as the localized opportunities the discussion landed on. None of it was tested: the blended sequence of AI-assisted drafting followed by supervised or process-traced assessment was discussed and never tried in a classroom.

## What a reflexive single-workshop report can and cannot support

The authors treat their own position as data: both organizers designed and led the workshop, sat in on every interview, and coded the transcripts themselves; reflections were written after initial coding so they would interrogate rather than confirm emerging themes, and two outside professors verified the coding. Reporting follows COREQ, and the risks are named directly: socially desirable answers, agenda-shaped themes, no claim of saturation. That candor sets the ceiling: a [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] description of one event can show how a designed professional-development experience was received and what vocabulary participants reached for; it cannot show time saved, changed teaching or learning. As a record of what was planned and produced it is useful to anyone designing similar training — transferable design, not effect — the caution running through [[limitations-in-aied-research|single-site AI education research]].

## What this means for practice

- **Faculty developers.** Design backward from outcomes and put ethics and access on the agenda from the first session; participants raised both anyway, and senior faculty arrived skeptical.
- **Faculty developers.** Prepare offline demonstrations, screenshots and open-source alternatives before the day, so restricted platforms or unstable connectivity cannot strand the hands-on segment.
- **Administrators.** Publish a clear acceptable-use policy and check connectivity before mandating AI use; participants named unclear guidance and rural infrastructure, not teacher willingness, as the binding constraints.
- **Instructors.** Use AI for drafting, brainstorming and practice, then keep higher-stakes evaluation in supervised, oral or process-traced form — the blended design that answers both efficiency and integrity concerns.

## Limitations

- One workshop at one campus with self-selected attendance, so nothing generalizes to Iranian [[teacher-ai-competency|EFL teachers]] or to AI-focused professional development elsewhere.
- Only 28 of the 62 attendees were interviewed, in opportunistic breaks of five to twelve minutes, with no saturation claim and no count of who held which view.
- The themes mirror the workshop's agenda of efficiency, ethics and equity, and the organizers did the interviewing, coding and interpretation themselves, with no inter-coder agreement statistic.
- Perceptions only: no measure of time saved, workload, teaching quality, student learning or later adoption, and no follow-up, so the resources handed out may never have been used.
- [[self-report-measures|Self-report]] throughout, with interviews in Persian analyzed in English translation by one author and verified by another; participants checked neither transcripts nor findings.
