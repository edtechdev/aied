> **Synthesis:** Lin, Zhao, Gurung, Meng, and Koedinger (2026) compared learning from tutor-training lessons generated by a retrieval-augmented LLM pipeline (curated research sources + structured lesson template) against carefully human-authored lessons in a randomized eight-condition between-subjects experiment (N = 208) on scenario-based training for middle-school math tutors. Participants studying the human-created lesson scored higher on post-test performance (0.793 vs 0.730 mean proportion correct), **but only in the no-feedback condition**: when immediate explanatory feedback was provided, the lesson-source difference disappeared, and feedback itself had no overall main effect. The authors read the interaction as evidence that the human-vs-LLM gap in generated instructional materials is partly a *scaffolding* gap — generated lessons are competitive when the learning environment supplies the explanatory feedback the lesson itself does not. One tutoring micro-skill, one lesson format, one RAG pipeline: the study does not establish general transfer or test whether LLM-assisted authoring saves development time without quality loss.

## Summary

High-impact tutoring improves student learning, but scaling tutoring programs requires large numbers of novice tutors, and developing high-quality tutor-training lessons is a key bottleneck. This study asked whether a scenario-based tutor-training lesson generated by an LLM using a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline — conditioned on curated research sources and a structured lesson template — supports post-instruction performance as well as a carefully refined, human-authored lesson, and whether immediate explanatory feedback moderates any difference between creation sources.

The experiment used a randomized eight-condition between-subjects design. Participants completed a source-matched pre-instruction practice assessment, studied a tutor-training lesson created by the same source (human-created or LLM-created), and then completed two fixed-order post-lesson assessments (one human-created, one LLM-created). Conditions crossed lesson source (human vs LLM), assessment source, and feedback (immediate explanatory feedback vs none). The focal content was a scenario-based lesson on a single tutoring micro-skill: using polite and autonomy-supportive language in online middle-school mathematics tutoring. Final analysis included 208 participants — 110 in the human-created lesson conditions and 98 in the LLM-created conditions.

## Key Findings

- **The human-created lesson won on average — conditionally.** Post-test performance (mean proportion correct across the two post-lesson assessments) was higher after the human-created lesson (M = 0.793, SD = 0.215) than the LLM-created lesson (M = 0.730, SD = 0.215), a difference of 0.063 (d ≈ 0.29).
- **Feedback erased the source difference.** The human-created advantage appeared only in the no-feedback condition (0.842 vs 0.698, a 0.144 gap); with feedback provided, no statistically reliable lesson-source difference was detected. The source × feedback interaction was significant (F(1, 204) = 7.423, p = 0.007, η²p = 0.035).
- **Feedback had no overall main effect.** The authors are explicit that the interaction "should not be interpreted as evidence that feedback generally improved post-test performance" — feedback moderated the *relative* performance of the two lesson types without raising either absolutely.
- **Assessment-source asymmetry.** The human-created lesson condition performed better on the human-created post-lesson assessment, while the LLM-created post-lesson assessment showed a small descriptive difference favoring the LLM-created lesson condition — with fixed assessment order, the study could not establish a general transfer advantage.
- **The RAG pipeline as a contribution.** The generation workflow — curated research sources plus a structured lesson template producing a complete scenario-based lesson — is offered as a reproducible method for topic-grounded, justifiable lesson generation, with the authors noting the design decisions RAG requires (what sources to index, retrieval granularity, controlling retrieval noise).
- **No detected AI use by participants.** The authors screened responses for generative AI use and excluded none on that basis.

## What this means for practice

- **Educational technology developers.** RAG-conditioned lesson generation over curated sources is a viable pipeline for producing usable tutor-training materials — but pair generated lessons with feedback mechanisms, since the human advantage concentrated precisely where feedback was absent.
- **Program designers (tutoring scale-up).** If generated training materials are adopted for novice-tutor scale-up, budget for the explanatory feedback layer: the study suggests the quality gap between generated and human-authored lessons is not fixed, and scaffolding can close it.
- **Instructional designers.** One lesson format, one micro-skill, one pipeline: treat the result as a conditional, not a verdict — the study explicitly does not test whether LLM-assisted authoring reduces development time or cost without reducing instructional quality.
- **Researchers evaluating generated content.** The assessment-source asymmetry (each lesson type advantaged on its own assessment) is a design warning: fixed-order, source-matched assessments can confound lesson quality with assessment familiarity — a methodological point for anyone replicating human-vs-LLM content comparisons.
- **Tutor-training designers.** Scenario-based practice on specific micro-skills (here, polite autonomy-supportive language) is a tractable training unit; prior evaluations of the approach report pretest-to-posttest improvements, though scenario work may strengthen one component of professional reasoning (e.g., diagnostic justification) without improving others (judgment accuracy).

## Limitations

- One tutoring micro-skill (polite, autonomy-supportive language in online middle-school math tutoring), one lesson format, and one RAG-based generation pipeline: the authors state the conclusions cannot generalize beyond this configuration.
- The fixed assessment order (human-created then LLM-created post-lesson assessments) confounds transfer conclusions with order effects; the study does not establish whether learning from either lesson type transfers to the other's assessment.
- The feedback finding is an interaction, not a main effect — the study provides no evidence that immediate explanatory feedback improves post-test performance overall, only that it changes which lesson type performs better.
- No direct test of the efficiency claim: whether LLM-assisted authoring reduces development time or cost without reducing instructional quality remains unexamined — the authors name this as future work.
- Scenario-based training may improve some components of professional reasoning (justification quality) without improving others (judgment accuracy), a pattern from prior work the authors cite as a caveat on what this training format can move.
- An analysis-sensitivity note: the Sequence result differed under alternative handling of no-attempt responses, indicating conclusions about sequence were sensitive to that analytic choice.
