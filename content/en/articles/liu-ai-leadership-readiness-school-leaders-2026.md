> **Synthesis:** Liu and Nazgul Seifullinqyzy (2026) ask what separates [[k-12|school]] leaders who are ready to implement AI from those who merely value it, surveying 763 school leaders in China (n = 389) and Kazakhstan (n = 374). Understanding of concrete AI-enabled teaching scenarios was the strongest antecedent of perceived instructional value, and digital leadership [[self-efficacy|self-efficacy]], organizational support, and leadership support for adoption were the strongest correlates of implementation readiness. A four-profile [[latent-profile-analysis|latent profile analysis]] separated high understanding–high readiness (31.8%), value-positive–near-average readiness (37.1%), risk-sensitive cautious (15.7%), and low understanding–low readiness (15.3%). The largest importance–competence gaps concerned [[ethics|ethics]] and risk governance, teacher development, and content quality audit, while Chinese respondents scored higher on most constructs and Kazakhstani respondents reported higher risk concern without significant cross-country differences in the associations. The study reframes AI readiness as a coordination problem for [[teacher-role|school leaders]] rather than a literacy deficit, with direct consequences for [[educational-development|leadership development]] and [[governance|AI governance]].

*Cross-national [[quantitative-research|quantitative]] survey with measurement-invariance testing, latent profile analysis, and standardized recursive path modeling; every construct is [[self-report-measures|self-reported]] and the design is cross-sectional.*

## Key Findings

- **Readiness is not the same as valuing AI.** The largest profile (37.1%) paired favorable instructional appraisal with near-average readiness, and perceived value had only a small direct association with implementation readiness.
- **Coordination capacity led the readiness correlates.** Digital leadership self-efficacy, organizational support, and leadership support for adoption showed the largest direct associations with implementation readiness.
- **Understanding scenarios fed value, which fed support.** Leaders who understood concrete AI-enabled teaching scenarios valued them more, and that value was associated with willingness to authorize and resource adoption.
- **Risk concern behaved differently from the other constructs.** It was higher among Kazakhstani respondents and was negatively associated with leadership support, but showed no direct association with readiness.
- **Four profiles, not a single average.** The retained solution separated high understanding–high readiness, value-positive–near-average readiness, risk-sensitive cautious, and low understanding–low readiness groups.
- **Ethics and risk governance topped the development gaps.** Leaders rated future importance above current competence most strongly for ethics and risk governance, teacher development, and content quality audit.

## Valuing AI is not the same as being ready to run it

The largest pooled profile, value-positive–near-average readiness, included 37.1% of leaders (n = 283) and combined favorable appraisals with near-average confidence, support, and readiness. The path model gives that pattern a precise interpretation: perceived instructional value had a small direct association with implementation readiness, while digital leadership [[self-efficacy]], organizational support, and leadership support for adoption showed much larger direct associations. Leaders may therefore recognize useful classroom applications before they have the confidence, resources, or support arrangements needed to coordinate school-level adoption. The finding aligns with [[technology-acceptance-model|technology acceptance]] research on perceived usefulness and facilitating conditions, but adds the school-level work that follows: coordinating teachers across subjects and grade levels, scheduling professional learning, allocating technical and financial resources, and assigning responsibility for reviewing AI-generated content. Readiness, in this account, is a [[change-management|change-management]] capacity rather than a judgment about the technology.

## Four profiles, one readiness gradient

The four-profile solution separated a risk-sensitive pattern from the low-understanding, low-readiness pattern. High understanding–high readiness included 31.8% of leaders (n = 243) with elevated scenario understanding, value, self-efficacy, support, and readiness; value-positive–near-average readiness included 37.1% (n = 283); risk-sensitive cautious included 15.7% (n = 120), combining elevated risk concern with lower value, support, and readiness; and low understanding–low readiness included 15.3% (n = 117). Profile membership differed by country (χ2(3) = 32.24, p < 0.001, Cramer's V = 0.206): the high-understanding–high-readiness group held 40.62% of Chinese respondents but 22.73% of Kazakhstani respondents, while the risk-sensitive cautious group held 11.83% and 19.79%. The authors read this as a readiness gradient in which risk concern changes its practical meaning: cautious leaders may need review protocols, staff guidance, and clear responsibility for risk decisions rather than more enthusiasm.

## The competence gaps leaders report

Across seven competence domains, leaders rated future importance above current competence everywhere, and the largest gaps concerned ethics and risk governance (G = 1.75), teacher development (G = 1.58), and content quality audit (G = 1.56); the smallest concerned AI foundations (G = 0.89). The profile-specific rankings point to different emphases within that shared pattern. Low-understanding–low-readiness leaders reported the largest gaps in digital change management (2.52), teacher development (2.23), and scenario judgment (2.17). Risk-sensitive cautious leaders reported digital change management (2.19), teacher development (2.04), content quality audit (1.72), and ethics and risk governance (1.71). Even high-understanding–high-readiness leaders reported a substantial ethics and risk governance gap (1.57). The pattern suggests that [[teacher-role|teacher development]] and [[governance|risk governance]] belong at the center of leadership preparation, while scenario judgment is the entry point for leaders who are still building instructional understanding.

## Why the cross-national comparison stays cautious

Measurement invariance testing supported comparing the recruited samples: the full-scalar model met prespecified fit-change criteria (CFI = 0.9957, TLI = 0.9950, RMSEA = 0.0078) and served as the primary basis for cross-sample comparison, and a partial-scalar sensitivity model releasing three intercept constraints improved fit (CFI = 0.9982). Chinese respondents scored higher on scenario understanding, instructional value, leadership support, self-efficacy, and readiness; Kazakhstani respondents scored higher on risk concern. Yet bootstrap comparisons of the direct and indirect associations found no statistically significant cross-country differences, with direct-path p values from 0.2089 to 0.9689. Policy agendas, language ecology, and professional-development infrastructure were not modeled as explanatory variables, and the sensitivity analysis put the minimum detectable coefficient difference at 0.13–0.26, so small differences cannot be ruled out.

## What this means for practice

- **Administrators.** Build the coordination capacity that tracked readiness most closely: digital leadership self-efficacy, organizational support, and leadership support for adoption.
- **Faculty developers.** Put ethics and risk governance, teacher development, and content quality audit at the center of leadership preparation, with practice in assigning responsibility for [[privacy]] and accuracy.
- **Policymakers.** Match support to profile: risk-sensitive leaders need governance tools and clear escalation procedures, while low-readiness leaders need scenario judgment and change-management preparation.
- **Researchers.** Test whether readiness profiles persist over time and whether the competence gaps close after targeted professional learning.

## Limitations

- The design is cross-sectional and the sample was recruited through open networks without a closed sampling frame, so no response-rate denominator exists.
- The Chinese, Kazakh, and Russian instruments were reconciled item by item, but the procedure omitted formal back-translation, cognitive interviews, and a separately registered pilot.
- Risk concern had low internal consistency (α = 0.581, ω = 0.582) and AVE of 0.260, so the risk findings are the least stable in the study.
- Country differences in policy, language ecology, and professional-development infrastructure were described but not modeled, and the minimum detectable coefficient difference was 0.13–0.26.
