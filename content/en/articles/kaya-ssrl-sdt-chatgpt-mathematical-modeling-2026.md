> **Synthesis:** Kaya and Yildiz Durak (2026) use a three-round Delphi panel of 10 experts, plus a field implementation with 40 preservice mathematics teachers working in five groups, to build a classification of 18 ChatGPT-4o-mediated mathematical modeling activities. Each activity is labeled against the four features of [[sociocultural-learning|socially shared regulation]] (transactive, deeply [[metacognition|metacognitive]], collectively agentic, socio-historical/contextualized), the collective-level needs of [[self-determination-theory|self-determination theory]] (autonomy, competence, relatedness), a [[math-education|modeling competency]], and the [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] role ChatGPT-4o plays in it. Consensus moved from 8 of 28 candidate activities in round one to 18 of 23 in round three, with merges and removals between rounds. The authors report the tool supported competence and autonomy but was limited on relatedness, and that some groups accepted ChatGPT-4o's output without [[critical-thinking|critical evaluation]].

*Three-round Delphi study with an expert panel of 10 (three subject-matter academics, three educational technology scholars, four mathematics teachers) and an implementation phase with 40 preservice mathematics teachers in five groups of eight, conducted in Turkey. Consensus threshold set at 80%, meaning at least 8 of 10 panelists selecting "agree" or "strongly agree".*

## Key Findings

1. **The deliverable is a classification tool, not a measured outcome.** Eighteen ChatGPT-4o-mediated mathematical modeling activities survived three Delphi rounds, each labeled for its regulatory character, its collective [[motivation|motivational]] contribution, a modeling competency, and the role ChatGPT-4o plays in it.
2. **Consensus was hard-won and moved across rounds.** Round one reached consensus on 8 of the initial 28 activities, with three merged into one and two unreplaced-by-consensus activities swapped for a single new one. Round two evaluated 17 activities and reached consensus on 2, merging two and removing one. Round three reached consensus on 18 of 23 and removed 5.
3. **Regulation and motivation are classified together, per activity.** Every activity carries labels on all four socially shared regulation features and all three collective needs, so the same activity can be strong on shared monitoring and weak on relatedness.
4. **ChatGPT-4o was reported strongest on competence and autonomy, weakest on relatedness.** The authors state the tool was effective in supporting competence and autonomy yet limited in meeting the need for relatedness, consistent with their reading that students still experience [[generative-ai|generative AI]] as impersonal.
5. **Twelve distinct pedagogical roles were assigned**, among them knowledge provider, modeling engine, inquiry prompter, strategy coach, reflective [[prompt-engineering|prompting]] and reflection scaffold, argumentation partner, and feedback provider.
6. **Field data qualified the design.** Groups positioned ChatGPT-4o as a thinking partner and strategy developer, and high-quality feedback supported more active engagement among [[teacher-education|preservice teachers]] with low self-confidence. But some groups accepted its information without critical evaluation, and in others interaction was shaped mainly by individual members rather than shared.
7. **Activities cluster by function rather than spreading evenly.** Some support access to contextual knowledge (#1, #2), others model construction and testing (#5, #6, #13), and the remainder evaluation of solution processes (#14, #16, #18).
## Three rounds, one tool

The panel worked in writing and without contact with one another. Round one ran open-ended, asking panelists to evaluate activities for content coherence, theoretical validity, and alignment with objectives, and to label each against the four regulation features and three collective needs. Round two converted that material into structured [[self-report-measures|questionnaires]], and round three fed field observations from the practitioner groups back to the panel for final judgment. The authors chose three rounds on the grounds that Delphi responses tend to stabilize by then.

Field implementation ran between rounds two and three. Five groups of eight preservice teachers each carried out five randomly assigned activities with ChatGPT-4o support, while a researcher observed and recorded group interaction, tool engagement, and pedagogical dynamics. Those observations became evidence for the final round rather than an outcome in themselves, which is why this page classifies the paper as a framework rather than an empirical study.

The 18 final activities span the modeling cycle: accessing contextual information, analyzing representative examples, discussing the problem with open-ended questions, generating alternative strategies, constructing original modeling problems, analyzing simplifying assumptions, developing conceptual explanations, requesting mathematization suggestions, planning procedures, explaining solutions to ChatGPT-4o, critically evaluating strategies, interpreting model outcomes, examining model validity, receiving feedback, conducting critical discussions, summarizing and reflecting, visualising the modeling process, and testing alternative verification conditions.

## Where the classification bites, and where it does not

The tool's practical value is that it forces two decisions at once: what regulation this activity builds, and whose psychological need it serves. An instructor choosing between two modeling tasks can compare them on relatedness rather than on how impressive the output looks. The authors argue this matters because generative AI is usually treated as an information-delivery tool rather than as something that has to be integrated with cognitive, affective, and social processes.

Two limits the authors name carry weight for practice. First, the number of activities that directly target relatedness is small, which they treat as a design gap rather than a finding about the technology. Second, the tool captures the activity as designed, not the group as it behaves. Field observation showed the same activity producing shared negotiation in one group and individual-dominated work in another, which the classification cannot record.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Choose modeling tasks on two axes at once: which regulation feature they exercise and which collective need they feed. Prefer tasks labeled strongly on shared monitoring when groups have not yet learned to regulate together.
- **[[teacher-role|Teacher]] educators.** Use the activity list as a discussion object with preservice teachers, not as a script. Ask them to predict which need an activity will serve, then compare with what happened.
- **Instructional designers.** Treat relatedness as an explicit design variable. Activities that only deliver information or feedback will not build it; activities that require shared decision-making stand a better chance.
- **Researchers.** The classification is a candidate taxonomy, not a validated instrument. Test it across tools other than ChatGPT-4o, and report whether the labels predict group behavior rather than expert judgment.

## Limitations

- No long-term follow-up. The authors state that high-level regulation develops through repeated experience, so a short intervention cannot show whether the activities build lasting skill.
- Sample diversity is limited: 50 participants drawn from one Turkish context, with the preservice teachers enrolled in a course taught by one of the researchers.
- The tool is bound to ChatGPT-4o's affordances and modes of interaction; the authors call for comparative work with Claude, Mistral, and Gemini.
- Participant opinion can shift over time, which the authors say may have limited continuity between rounds and comparability of findings.
- Consensus was defined at 80% of a 10-person panel, so a single activity could pass on eight votes; the threshold is conventional rather than validated.
