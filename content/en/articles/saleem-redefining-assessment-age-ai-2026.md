> **Synthesis:** Saleem and colleagues (2026) interviewed academicians at private universities in Malaysia about what students' AI-assisted assignments do to teaching and assessment. The accounts describe added verification work, a shift from facilitating learning toward monitoring and enforcement, and strain on professional identity as staff try to preserve trust while meeting integrity expectations. Participants responded by moving assessment toward in-class and process-visible formats — oral presentations, live case analysis, simulations, debate, group projects, reflection and problem solving — rather than by banning AI. They asked for institutional support: detection tools, faculty development, clear governance and policies on acceptable use. The paper frames AI's effect as an organizational and professional change, not only a classroom problem, and argues that workload models, assessment design and support for educators' changing roles need to change with it.

## Key Findings
1. **Verification adds invisible labor.** Academicians described extra time spent checking authenticity, consulting students, re-evaluating work and giving repeated feedback, which delayed marking of other submissions.
2. **Roles move toward monitoring.** Participants described themselves less as facilitators of learning and more as supervisors, regulators and gatekeepers responsible for academic honesty.
3. **Professional identity is strained.** Staff who entered teaching to mentor and inspire students reported discomfort at being cast as enforcers of integrity, caught between trust-based relationships and institutional expectations.
4. **Assessment is being redesigned around visible process.** Oral presentations, live cases, simulations, role play, debate, group projects, reflection and in-class problem solving were reported as ways to elicit understanding that a submitted text may not show.
5. **Integrity rules remain hard to apply.** Participants found polished AI text difficult to distinguish from student work, described a thin line between acceptable help and unacceptable use, and doubted existing detection tools.
6. **Institutions, not individual teachers, are asked to carry the response.** The dominant request was for coordinated policy, training, detection resources and forums where staff can learn from one another's cases.
7. **AI is not rejected.** Most participants recognized that AI could support learning if used well; the emphasis was on inclusion and responsible use rather than prohibition.

## What the study measured
The study used a qualitative exploratory design at private higher education institutions in Malaysia. Eighteen academicians took part. They had at least 5 years of teaching experience in higher education, had been involved in assessing undergraduate, master's and postgraduate assignments, and had handled work submitted with AI assistance. Respondents came from six institutions and mainly from business-related teaching areas.

Data were gathered with a structured interview protocol of ten open-ended questions covering teaching responsibilities, workload, assessment change, integrity, identity, coping strategies and support needs. Interviews were conducted online, lasted about 30–45 min, were recorded with the platform's tools and were transcribed. Analysis followed open coding, axial coding and thematic analysis. The authors report 43 initial open codes reduced to 8 axial categories, from which six final themes emerged. Trustworthiness measures included detailed descriptions of context, researcher reflexivity, debriefing of research members and a return to transcripts during interpretation; the principal investigator carried out the coding alone.

## Six themes in the academicians' accounts
The first theme, workload intensification and assessment burden, was reported by all participants. Checking plagiarism, following up with students, re-evaluating revised work and giving repetitive feedback were described as additions to ordinary marking, with knock-on delays to other students' work. The second theme, transformation of the teaching role, captures the reported move from facilitating learning to verification and enforcement. The third, professional identity transformation, goes further: respondents used terms such as "enforcer of academic integrity", "gatekeeper", "quality controller" and "regulator" for themselves, and some described soreness at taking on work they had not expected when entering academia.

The fourth theme records pedagogical adaptation and assessment redesign. Nearly all respondents reported trying alternative formats that demand engagement and immediate demonstration of knowledge, shifting attention from end-of-course products toward learning processes, critical reflection and application. The fifth theme covers academic integrity and AI detection: AI-generated writing was described as more polished and coherent than students' own in-class work, making authorship hard to judge, and respondents questioned both detection software and the boundary between legitimate and unacceptable help. The sixth theme brings together coping strategies and support needs, including more classroom interaction, formative assessment, responsible-use discussions, and requests for [[ai-detection|detection tools]], faculty development, guidelines and [[educational-policy-ai|governance frameworks]]. Participants worried that heavy reliance on AI could reduce the mental effort that develops independent thinking, a concern the paper connects to cognitive offloading.

The discussion treats these themes as one organizational and professional change process. It argues that AI does not replace teachers but changes where their time goes, and that [[authentic-assessment|authentic assessment]] and process-based evidence are a response to that change rather than a rejection of the technology.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Record the additional time spent verifying AI-assisted submissions and discussing integrity with students, so the invisible work becomes visible in workload conversations.
- **Instructors.** Use in-class demonstrations of understanding — oral explanation, live case analysis or a short defense of a submitted argument — when a written product leaves authorship uncertain.
- **Assessment designers.** Rebuild assignments around process evidence such as reflective journals, applied problems and staged drafts, where students must explain how they reached a conclusion.
- **Administrators.** Count assessment verification in workload models and provide clear guidance on acceptable and unacceptable AI use, disclosure and consequences.
- **Faculty developers.** Offer training in responsible AI use and assessment redesign, and create forums where staff can work through difficult authenticity cases together.

## Limitations
- The study was conducted at private higher education institutions in Malaysia and may not generalize to public universities or other contexts, as the authors note.
- Only educators were interviewed; students, administrators and policymakers are absent, so the accounts describe one side of the assessment relationship.
- The design is qualitative and exploratory, aimed at depth rather than statistical generalization, and the sample came from a limited set of institutions and largely business disciplines.
- Coding was carried out solely by the principal investigator; other team members were debriefed, but no independent coding reliability figure is reported.
- AI tools and institutional responses change quickly, so the account of roles and coping strategies captures a particular moment.
