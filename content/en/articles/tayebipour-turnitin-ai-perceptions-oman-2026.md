> **Synthesis:** Tayebipour and colleagues (2026) asked Omani college students and lecturers how they perceive Turnitin and [[generative-ai|ChatGPT]] in relation to [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]]. Questionnaire ratings were generally positive, but the interviews show a sharp difference in what integrity means to the two groups: many students equated it with reducing a similarity score and resubmitting, while lecturers described originality, [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]] and genuine engagement. Both groups valued Turnitin for plagiarism support and doubted its ability to identify AI-generated text; students also wanted access to the AI percentage that they say is withheld from them. Students described ChatGPT as a source of ideas and confidence, and lecturers welcomed that use while warning about dependence. Differences by level and discipline were reported, though the sample was heterogeneous and the AI measure weak. Both groups asked for clearer institutional guidelines, workshops and assessment that demands reasoning.

## Key Findings
1. **Ratings were positive, but lecturers were more cautious.** Both groups saw value in Turnitin and AI tools; lecturers emphasized the risks of dependence and of presenting generated work as one's own.
2. **Students treated integrity procedurally.** Interviewed students often understood it as lowering similarity reports, whereas lecturers described a broader concept including originality and [[active-learning|active learning]].
3. **Turnitin's AI limit was widely recognized.** Both groups found it useful for plagiarism but doubted that it could reliably distinguish human from machine-generated writing, especially when students disputed a flag.
4. **Perceptions varied by level and discipline.** The authors report differences across student groups and associate greater understanding of appropriate AI use with more advanced study.
5. **Students wanted guidance, not prohibition.** They described ChatGPT as a brainstorming and clarification aid and asked for classroom discussion, presentations and handbooks showing correct ways to use it.
6. **Lecturers proposed reasoning-demanding assessment.** Suggestions included workshops, open-ended questions and presentations that let a [[teacher-role|teacher]] check whether the student understands submitted work.
7. **Policy ambiguity was a shared complaint.** Lecturers described gray areas and inconsistent handling of AI cases by different tutors, and both groups asked institutions for explicit rules.

## What the study measured
The mixed-method study used [[self-report-measures|questionnaires]] and focus group interviews at a single Omani college. Participants included 203 students from the General Foundation Program, undergraduate and [[graduate-education|postgraduate]] levels across English Language Studies, Business Management and Information Technology, plus 24 lecturers who responded to a questionnaire distributed among 40 lecturers. Six students and six lecturers took part in interviews. Sampling was non-probability and convenience-based, producing a deliberately heterogeneous group that the authors say limits generalizability.

Student questionnaires covered two constructs: Turnitin as learning support and plagiarism prevention, and AI-generated reports and ethical guidelines. Exploratory factor analysis produced two factors for each construct. The Turnitin construct showed a Cronbach alpha of 0.880, but the AI construct showed 0.410, which the authors attribute to a short, author-developed exploratory scale; they call for better item phrasing and dedicated subscales. The lecturer questionnaire had 11 items and a Cronbach alpha of 0.76. Responses were analyzed with chi-square tests, one-way ANOVA, effect sizes and thematic analysis of interviews with member checking.

## What the ratings show
Average questionnaire ratings were more than 3 out of 5 on the Likert scale, which the authors read as positive perception. A [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|meta-analysis]] of ratings by student level produced an effect size of 1.01 between General Foundation and undergraduate students, described in the abstract as a large effect; the corresponding figure was 0.81 for postgraduate and General Foundation responses and 0.37 for undergraduate and postgraduate responses. The authors caution that these values track the number of responses and the variety of answers in each group, not a simple ranking of understanding.

Lecturer ratings averaged 3.89 out of 5, with low variation across items. Around 25% of lecturers agreed that they would allow students to use AI for assignments and 75% would not, which the paper reads as a trust dilemma. Discipline and level were associated with different ratings: the chi-square analysis reported a value of 18.42 with a p-value of 0.0010 and a Cramer's V of 0.21, described as a moderate association. The authors note that uneven group sizes and unequal variances shaped the analysis, which is why they used Welch's ANOVA.

## What the interviews add
Students' accounts give the ratings meaning. Many tied academic integrity to Turnitin similarity reports and to the chance to paraphrase and resubmit; one student described checking the rate across semesters and changing only what could be paraphrased. The authors interpret this as an operational, procedural understanding that omits honesty and responsibility. Students saw ChatGPT as helping them brainstorm, clarify concepts and work independently, while also acknowledging that copying and pasting from it would not produce independent learners. They were anxious that Turnitin could not detect AI-generated text and that students were not shown the AI percentage needed to improve their work.

Lecturers offered a fuller definition of integrity. One said that awarding a mark for machine-generated work would be unethical; others spoke of originality, critical thinking and engagement. They welcomed AI for generating ideas and supporting writing, but reported students relying on it for reports and formatting. On Turnitin, they described [[ai-detection|plagiarism detection]] as helpful yet contested in AI cases, with students arguing that they had not used the tool. Their proposed responses were workshops, guidelines embedded in teaching, open-ended questions requiring reasoning, and presentations that expose whether a student can defend submitted work. They also reported inconsistent practice among tutors when students were interviewed to prove authorship.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Discuss what counts as acceptable AI assistance in class and in assignment briefs, rather than relying on students to infer the boundary from similarity scores.
- **Instructors.** Use open-ended questions and short defenses of submitted work so that understanding can be checked when AI use is disputed.
- **Assessment designers.** Include contextualized, reasoning-demanding tasks that require students to explain and revise their own ideas rather than paraphrase generated text.
- **Administrators.** Publish explicit AI guidelines and run integrity workshops, since lecturers in the study reported gray areas and inconsistent handling of cases.
- **Researchers.** Test these perceptions against actual Turnitin reports and more homogeneous samples, and improve the reliability of AI-use measures before drawing level comparisons.

## Limitations
- The student sample is heterogeneous across three disciplines and several levels, and the authors state that this may affect generalizability.
- Data are self-reported; participants may have underreported [[ai-misuse-learning-harm|AI misuse]], and no actual Turnitin reports were analyzed.
- The study is specific to one Omani [[higher-ed|higher education]] context, and the interview samples were small.
- The AI construct had low internal consistency, which the authors attribute to a short author-developed scale and flag as requiring further evaluation.
- Comparisons by level were shaped by uneven response counts, and the authors describe the [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] sample as too small for generalizable comparative analysis.
- The paper calls for larger homogeneous samples, other detection tools and multi-institution studies; none of those comparisons is provided here.
