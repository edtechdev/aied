> **Synthesis:** Huang and Ma (2026) interviewed 20 Chinese journalism and communication students and watched them work through a simulated reporting task to ask what [[generative-ai|generative-AI]] dependency actually consists of. Their grounded theory separates three dimensions that vary independently: instrumental dependency (delegating task components), cognitive dependency (accepting generated content as credible without verification) and affective dependency (relying on AI to start work or to feel reassured). What unites them is a single criterion — impaired substitutability, the sense that the task cannot proceed when the tool is unavailable — so a student can delegate heavily while staying critical, or lean on AI for inspiration without handing over the writing. Vague [[educational-policy-ai|institutional policy]], peer culture, tool accessibility and individual [[ai-literacy|digital literacy]] shape which pattern develops. The contribution is a finer-grained object than "over-reliance": three dimensions a course can observe separately, rather than one attitude to measure.

## Key Findings

1. **Dependency has three separable dimensions.** Instrumental dependence operates at the behavioral level, cognitive dependence at the epistemic level, and affective dependence at the [[motivation|motivational]] and emotional level, and the authors report they vary independently across participants.
2. **Impaired substitutability is the unifying criterion.** A dimension counted as dependency only when participants described the task as unable to proceed without the tool, which turns an attitude into a testable condition.
3. **Heavy delegation and critical vigilance co-occur.** Some participants delegated most task components while continuing to check outputs, which is why instrumental and cognitive dependence cannot be read off one another.
4. **Institutional policy shaped what students admitted and did.** Students described strict prohibition, guided use for ideation, and ambiguous or absent policy, and in the ambiguous case could not tell whether AI use was misconduct.
5. **Most students knew of no compulsory AI module.** Institutional barriers named by participants included insufficient staff expertise, outdated materials, missing hardware and software, and slow approval processes against fast-moving tools.
6. **The walkthrough exposed gaps between report and behavior.** Triangulating interview accounts against screen-recorded task traces let the team identify discrepancies between what students said about verification and what they did.

## Where the framework sits

The study treats dependency not as a single scalar but as a profile, and it is careful about what the profile is for: the conceptual map is explicitly non-causal, dashed lines mark boundary conditions rather than subtypes, and the authors repeat that their claims describe a self-selected group of 20 students. For a field that has measured over-reliance with one scale at a time, that is a useful distinction. A response to dependency that only reduces delegation may leave cognitive dependence intact; one that only teaches verification may not touch the affective function that gets students started. The framework also gives [[cognitive-offloading|over-reliance]] research vocabulary for the case where output quality is fine and verification still does not happen.

## Policy ambiguity as an antecedent

The external layer of the map is a policy typology with three states: strict prohibition, guided use, and ambiguous or permissive. The third is the one participants described as hardest to navigate, because a student cannot tell whether a use is misconduct until someone rules on it. Read against the [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]] literature, ambiguity shifts risk onto the individual: the same behavior is fine in one department and a failing grade in another, and the student holds the uncertainty. The participants' account of their own institutions is a reminder that AI policy quality is not a matter of strictness but of whether a rule tells a student what to do on a Tuesday night before a deadline.

## What the walkthrough method adds

The study pairs interviews with a screen-recorded reporting task and a think-aloud protocol, then triangulates the two. That design lets the authors mark which claims are reported perceptions, which are task behavior, and which are their own inference — a distinction most [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] dependency work leaves implicit. It also produced one of the paper's sharper observations: students who said they verified sources often did not do so when the task made verification available, though the task's ban on external search limits what that gap can demonstrate. For [[student-ai-interaction|student–AI interaction]] research, the method is the transferable asset.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Observe the three dimensions separately. Ask what a student delegates, what they verify, and what they cannot start without; the answers diverge, and a single "do you rely on AI" question collapses them.
- **Instructors.** Build at least one assessed task with no tool access so that impaired substitutability becomes visible before an exam or a placement does it for you.
- **Program leaders.** Replace ambiguous permissiveness with a written default and a worked example of acceptable and unacceptable use. Students in the ambiguous condition could not tell whether their work was misconduct, which is a policy failure rather than a student one.
- **Administrators.** Fund staff expertise and tooling before mandating AI modules; participants named missing expertise and hardware, not missing enthusiasm, as the binding constraint.

## Limitations

- The sample was recruited through snowball and network sampling, over-represents [[higher-ed|undergraduates]] (15 of 20) and is entirely Chinese national, so it cannot be generalized to journalism students broadly.
- Interview data rely on [[self-report-measures|self-report]] and are subject to recall and social-desirability effects, and some nuance may not have survived translation from Chinese.
- The walkthrough used researcher-selected articles, prohibited external search, and approximated rather than reproduced authentic writing, so limited fact-checking in the task reflects the design.
- Data were collected in January–February 2025, a window in which at least one tool changed rapidly, so findings describe a particular technological moment.
- Coding was led by one author with a second reviewing rather than coding independently, which is an internal audit rather than inter-rater reliability.
