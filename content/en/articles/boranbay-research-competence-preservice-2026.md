> **Synthesis:** Boranbay and colleagues compared two intact cohorts of pre-service English language teachers at one [[higher-ed|university]] in Kazakhstan. One cohort took an eight-week [[generative-ai|AI-supported]] research-learning intervention and the other received conventional research instruction. Both cohorts improved on a scenario-based performance assessment. After adjusting for baseline, the AI-supported cohort scored higher overall and on methodological and cognitive competence, while its adjusted digital and ethical scores were numerically lower. The authors emphasize that each condition was a single cohort, so these are descriptive contrasts between two groups rather than evidence that the intervention worked. They read the pattern as a case for using AI outputs as material for evaluation and verification, not as answers.

## Key Findings
1. **Both cohorts improved.** Scores rose from pre-test to post-test across all four research-competence dimensions in both the AI-supported and the conventional group.
2. **The AI cohort scored higher overall after adjustment.** The baseline-adjusted overall research-competence score favored the AI-supported cohort.
3. **Methodological and cognitive competence led.** The largest adjusted differences were in the components that required comparing alternatives, evaluating evidence, identifying unsupported claims and justifying methodological decisions.
4. **Digital and ethical scores did not favor AI.** Adjusted digital and ethical scores were numerically lower in the AI-supported cohort, and neither contrast reached statistical significance.
5. **No intervention effect can be claimed.** Each condition was one intact cohort, so instructional condition was completely confounded with cohort membership and the analysis cannot separate AI from other cohort influences.
6. **AI was framed as cognitive contrast.** Students made an initial decision, compared it with AI-generated alternatives, verified information, revised and justified a final choice; the proposed mechanism itself was not measured.

## What was compared

The study involved 133 second-year undergraduates in a [[language-learning|foreign language]] [[teacher-education|teacher education]] program at Zhanibekov South Kazakhstan [[pedagogy|Pedagogical]] University. Two existing cohorts were recruited by convenience because their timetables suited the researcher: 67 students were assigned to the AI-supported condition and 66 to conventional instruction at the researcher's discretion, without randomization. Each cohort was made up of two administrative groups that already attended lectures together, so the four groups formed only two instructional clusters. Both cohorts were taught by the same instructor, who had 16 years of teaching experience, and neither had previously received formal instruction in research methodology or AI.

The eight-week intervention used ChatGPT, reported as ChatGPT 5, through the free interface on students' own accounts and devices; the exact model identifier and any automatic routing were not documented. Each week included one 90-minute session: about 15 minutes of teacher-led introduction, 20 minutes of independent work, 20 minutes of AI-supported exploration, 20 minutes of checking and critical comparison, and 15 minutes of revision and reflection. Instruction and assessment were in English while [[student-ai-interaction|AI interaction]] was in Kazakh, a language arrangement that was described but not tested as an explanation. The recurring sequence was independent attempt, AI-supported exploration, verification, [[critical-thinking|critical evaluation]], revision and student decision, with responsibility for final choices left to students. The control cohort covered comparable research topics over the same period with conventional resources and teacher feedback, but was not given equivalent non-AI scaffolding.

The outcome measure was a purpose-built, scenario-based performance assessment of 12 tasks, three for each of four components: methodological, cognitive, digital and ethical competence. Each task was scored from 0 to 3, giving a 0–9 range per component and a 0–36 total. Form A used a vocabulary-learning scenario at pre-test; Form B used an AI-generated writing-feedback scenario at post-test. Two experts reviewed the forms and rubric, and Form A was piloted with 48 students who were not in the study, though pilot records were not retained. Responses were scored independently by two blinded assessors; single-measure intraclass correlations ranged from .864 to .935 and average-measure values from .927 to .966.

## What the adjusted contrasts show

Both cohorts improved in raw terms. The experimental group's overall mean rose from 16.97 (SD = 1.68) at pre-test to 24.40 (SD = 1.92) at post-test, a raw increase of 7.43 points. The control group rose from 17.68 (SD = 2.04) to 23.44 (SD = 1.91), a raw increase of 5.76 points. At component level, the experimental cohort had higher post-test means for methodological competence (6.14 vs. 5.49) and cognitive competence (5.76 vs. 4.72), while the control cohort had higher means for digital competence (6.65 vs. 6.30) and ethical competence (6.58 vs. 6.19). These raw differences were not read as group effects because the cohorts differed at baseline.

Baseline checks confirmed that gap. Digital competence favored the control cohort (U = 1,565.50, Z = −2.992, p = .003; mean ranks 76.78 and 57.37), and the overall score also differed (Welch-adjusted t(125.76) = −2.20, p = .030, 95% CI [−1.35, −0.07], Cohen's d = −0.38; Levene's test F = 4.443, p = .037). Methodological, cognitive and ethical baselines did not differ significantly. ANCOVA was therefore used as an exploratory, descriptive adjustment rather than a confirmatory test of effectiveness, with the corresponding pre-test score entered as covariate. The authors stress that the reported standard errors, intervals and p-values assume independent student-level errors and do not account for within-cohort dependence.

After adjustment, overall research competence was 1.338 points higher in the AI-supported cohort, with a 95% CI of [0.77, 1.91] and Bonferroni-adjusted p < .001; adjusted means were 24.59 and 23.25, F(1,130) = 21.329, p < .001, partial η² = .141. Baseline score was also associated with post-test performance, F(1,130) = 48.749, p < .001, partial η² = .273, and the model explained 31.6% of variance (R² = .316, adjusted R² = .305). Methodological competence showed an adjusted difference of 0.717 points (95% CI [0.419, 1.016], p < .001; F(1,130) = 22.612, partial η² = .148), and cognitive competence 0.924 points (95% CI [0.669, 1.180], p < .001; F(1,130) = 51.260, partial η² = .283), with adjusted means of 5.703 and 4.779 on the cognitive measure.

Digital and ethical contrasts did not favor the AI cohort. Digital competence showed an adjusted difference of −0.153 points (95% CI [−0.454, 0.148], p = .317, partial η² = .008) and ethical competence −0.247 points (95% CI [−0.532, 0.037], p = .088, partial η² = .022). After Holm correction across the four secondary outcomes, methodological and cognitive competence remained significant at p < .001, while digital (p = .317) and ethical (p = .176) did not. Both confidence intervals included zero, which the authors say establishes neither equivalence nor the absence of an effect.

Within each cohort, all four dimensions rose significantly on Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. In the experimental cohort, 63 of 67 students improved in methodological competence, 62 in cognitive competence and 64 in both digital and ethical competence, with no decreases. In the control cohort, 51 of 66 improved in methodological competence, 37 in cognitive competence, 59 in digital competence and 64 in ethical competence. The authors caution that within-group change is not evidence that one condition was better.

For scale interpretation, the paper notes that the adjusted overall difference of 1.338 points is about 3.7% of the 36-point range, or roughly 0.112 points per task, and that the cognitive difference of 0.924 points is about 10.3% of the nine-point range, or 0.308 points per task. No validated minimum educationally important difference was established, so practical importance remains uncertain. The proposed [[scaffolding]] mechanism was not directly tested, and the intervention bundled AI access with independent attempts, verification, feedback, reflection and revision.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Structure AI-supported research tasks as a cycle: independent attempt, AI exploration, verification, critical evaluation, revision and a justified student decision.
- **Teacher educators.** Do not assume repeated AI use builds ethical judgment; integrate reference verification, disclosure, authorship, privacy and accountability across several research activities rather than one [[ethics]] session.
- **Institutions.** Replicate the design with multiple independent instructional clusters per condition and an active comparison offering equivalent non-AI scaffolding before treating the contrast as an effect.
- **Assessment designers.** Prefer performance tasks with an [[rubrics|analytic rubric]] and independent blinded raters over self-reports of competence, and test the equivalence of parallel forms before using them for pre–post comparison.
- **Researchers.** Add delayed post-tests and transfer tasks in contexts less aligned with the intervention, so short-term gains can be separated from durable competence.

## Limitations

- The study took place at one institution with one cohort per condition, recruited by convenience and assigned without randomization; condition was confounded with cohort, so no intervention effect or causal claim is supported.
- The intervention lasted eight weeks and the post-test followed immediately, leaving retention and transfer untested.
- The assessment was developed for the study; only one form was piloted, pilot records were not retained, forms were not counterbalanced or statistically equated, and the post-test scenario may have been more familiar to the experimental cohort.
- Baseline measures of English proficiency, [[learning-gains|academic achievement]], motivation and prior informal AI use were not collected, and the exact AI model identifier was not documented.
- No formal institutional ethics review or determination of exemption was obtained. The authors acknowledge this and do not claim approval or exemption, although the published ethics statement gives a different account.
