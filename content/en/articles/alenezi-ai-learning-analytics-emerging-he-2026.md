> **Synthesis:** Alenezi reviewed 31 publications on artificial intelligence and [[learning-analytics|learning analytics]] in emerging higher education, drawn from Latin America, Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia and the Middle East. The set comprised 20 empirical studies, six conceptual papers and five policy or gray literature sources, each graded from A to D. Predictive early-warning systems, adaptive platforms, generative AI tools and institutional dashboards were the four application areas. Regional emphases differed, but evaluation almost everywhere privileged technical performance over educational impact, equity and long-term sustainability. From a three-stage synthesis the review proposes the Human-Centered AI and Learning Analytics Implementation Framework, built on Governance, Pedagogy, Equity and Sustainability. It treats AI and analytics as socio-technical systems requiring context-sensitive adaptation, not technology-driven adoption alone.

## Key Findings

1. **Predictive early-warning systems were the most common intervention.** They appeared in approximately 45% of the empirical studies and drew on Grade A and Grade B evidence.
2. **Adaptive and personalized platforms were the next category.** They appeared in approximately 25% of empirical work, with moderate engagement and satisfaction gains but persistent localization problems.
3. **Generative AI evidence was thin.** Much of it rested on self-reported perceptions, with limited empirically validated learning outcomes.
4. **Regional profiles differed.** Latin America showed greater implementation maturity, Sub-Saharan Africa emphasized governance and data justice, South Asia combined technical sophistication with weak pedagogical integration, and the Middle East showed early-stage experimentation.
5. **Technical metrics dominated evaluation.** Only approximately 20% of studies measured educational outcomes directly, while several South Asian models reported very high predictive accuracy.
6. **Sustainability was the recurring weakness.** Most empirical initiatives were pilots or short-term projects, and only a small fraction showed institutionalization beyond the initial phase.
7. **The proposed framework puts four dimensions on equal footing.** Governance, Pedagogy, Equity and Sustainability emerged across regions and evidence types and are described as interdependent rather than sequential.

## How the review was assembled and graded

The study used a critical scoping review design following Joanna Briggs Institute guidance for a Population–Concept–Context framing and reported under PRISMA-ScR. The search covered January 2019 to April 2026 across eight academic databases: ERIC, ACM Digital Library, PubMed, ScienceDirect, Taylor & Francis Online, Wiley Online Library, Frontiers in Education and Google Scholar for supplementary retrieval. Gray literature from UNESCO, OECD, World Bank and Deloitte Insights was searched separately. Scopus and Web of Science were deliberately excluded in favor of discipline-specific databases, a choice the author names as a possible limit on retrieval sensitivity. Eligible sources were in English, Spanish or Portuguese.

The search returned 2,847 records. After removing 924 duplicates, 1,923 records were screened by title and abstract; 142 full texts were assessed and 31 sources were included. The final set contained 20 Type 1 empirical studies, six Type 2 conceptual contributions and five Type 3 policy or gray literature documents. Among the empirical studies, four were graded A, ten B and six C; Grade D covers conceptual and policy evidence. This grading supports interpretive weighting rather than a formal risk-of-bias judgment.

The framework was built in three stages. Inductive thematic analysis of the 20 empirical studies identified recurring enablers and barriers. Deductive alignment connected those themes to UNESCO principles, socio-technical systems theory and African ethical analytics frameworks. Convergent integration then compared findings across all 31 sources, all four regions, all four intervention categories and both analytical stages. Governance, Pedagogy, Equity and Sustainability appeared consistently and became the framework’s dimensions.

## What the evidence showed across tools and regions

Predictive early-warning systems were the largest category, present in approximately 45% of empirical studies (nine Type 1 studies, Grade A–B evidence). Several South Asian studies reported predictive accuracy exceeding 85%–90%, yet only a limited number checked whether predictions translated into better student outcomes or retention. Adaptive and personalized systems appeared in approximately 25% of empirical studies (five Type 1 studies, Grade B evidence), with moderate gains in engagement and learning satisfaction in large STEM courses, though many systems were imported without meaningful localization. Generative AI tools appeared mainly in Grade C evidence based on self-reports. Institutional analytics dashboards showed efficiency gains in selected Grade B case studies but often stayed in departmental silos.

Approximately 20% of studies measured educational outcomes directly. The review found moderate Grade B support for institutional efficiency gains but considerably weaker evidence for educational transformation or learning improvement. Few empirical studies examined equity outcomes; those that did reported algorithmic bias, unequal predictive performance across student groups and the risk of reproducing existing inequalities. Eleven of the twenty empirical studies described pilots or short-term initiatives, while only four documented institutionalization beyond the initial phase. Governance was the most consistently reported implementation requirement across all 31 sources, yet the author notes that most included studies reported no formal governance framework or ethical oversight at the time of implementation.

Regional counts were small: six empirical studies from Latin America, four from Sub-Saharan Africa, three from South Asia, two from the Middle East and five cross-regional studies. The Latin American LALA project illustrated collaborative capacity building but weakened when external funding ended. South African work foregrounded privacy, consent and accountability in analytics. The author cautions that these are emerging trends, not definitive geographic characterizations, because per-region samples ranged from two to six studies.

In the proposed framework, Governance covers data stewardship, ethics oversight, privacy and consent, transparency and stakeholder participation, operationalized through model cards, algorithmic audits and data protection impact assessments. Pedagogy covers human-in-the-loop decisions, analytics literacy, curriculum alignment and co-design with data scientists. Equity calls for bias mitigation at design time and disaggregated outcome monitoring across gender, socio-economic status, disability, first-generation status and digital access. Sustainability covers diversified funding, technical capacity, interoperable infrastructure and organizational learning. The author states that the framework has not yet been validated through expert consensus, Delphi studies, stakeholder evaluation or implementation testing.

## What this means for practice

- **Institutions.** Put governance in place before scaling analytics: consent procedures, data minimization, access controls, algorithmic risk management and documented audit trails.
- **Instructors.** Treat analytics as input to professional judgment. Insist on teacher-in-the-loop interpretation and co-design rather than reading a dashboard as a verdict about students.
- **Administrators.** Evaluate impact beyond accuracy, using mixed methods and results disaggregated by student subgroup, since only approximately 20% of studies measured educational outcomes directly.
- **Institutions.** Plan past the pilot: recurrent budgets, local technical capacity and professional development matter more than model sophistication when initiatives are to be embedded.
- **Policymakers.** Co-create fairness and privacy rules with local stakeholders instead of importing high-income governance templates; the review warns about data colonialism and transplanted innovation.

## Limitations

- Scopus and Web of Science were excluded and searches were limited to English, Spanish and Portuguese, which may have reduced retrieval sensitivity and introduced language bias.
- Heterogeneity in designs, interventions and outcome measures constrained comparability and precluded meta-analysis; per-region samples of two to six studies make regional patterns preliminary.
- No randomized controlled trials were identified, and most evidence was observational or correlational, so effectiveness claims cannot be causal.
- Publication bias may favor positive findings, and gray literature selection may overweight large international organizations while underrepresenting local voices; Southeast Asia and Central Asia were substantially underrepresented.
- The Human-Centered AI and Learning Analytics Implementation Framework is a theoretically informed synthesis, not an empirically validated implementation model.
