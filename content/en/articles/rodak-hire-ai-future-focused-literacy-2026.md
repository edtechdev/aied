> **Synthesis:** Rodak (2026) describes Hire AI, a four-step framework she ran in three sections of a required undergraduate business writing course at Indiana University, 72 students in a single semester, in which students treat a [[generative-ai|GenAI]] tool as a hired assistant. They annotate readings on AI and power, co-write the course's AI use policy, then write a job description, a scope of work and an avatar for a named assistant they consult all term and review like an employee. The design is deliberately low-lift — [[metaphors-and-analogies|metaphor]] plus [[scaffolding|scaffolding]] rather than a new module — and the evidence is a practitioner's: pre/post self-report, reflections, team artifacts and final portfolios rather than a comparison. 68% rated their GenAI skills average or below at the start and 6.7% advanced; by the end 100% reported moderate or greater improvement and 91.8% significant growth, 54% ranked GenAI literacy among their top three career skills behind communication at 71%, and 76.3% taught a GenAI topic on a one-page Tip Sheet aimed at future employers. The most concrete result is the students' own scope-of-work lists, where 100% placed undisclosed copy-paste, completing the assignment for them, and AI-invented sources outside the scope while 81% hired the assistant for practice questions and proofreading.

## Key Findings

- **The framework is four recursive steps, not a module.** Groundwork builds shared vocabulary through social annotation of readings, Discussion converts it into a co-created course policy, Onboard has each student hire and define an assistant, and Coach and Collaborate runs the rest of the term through documented use and performance reviews.
- **Students drew a firm outer boundary and a practical inner one.** Across the scope-of-work lists, 100% excluded undisclosed copy-paste, 100% excluded having the assistant complete assignments, and 100% excluded AI-generated research and sources; 63% excluded unacknowledged use in team projects and 60% excluded use on assignments meant to build core skills.
- **What they did hire it for was rehearsal, not production.** Simulations and scenario testing appeared in 81% of lists, proofreading and grammar in 81%, feedback and revision suggestions in 75%, evaluating rhetorical choices in 75%, and synthesizing the student's own notes in 75%; outlining appeared in 31% and citation formatting in 37%.
- **Self-assessed proficiency moved sharply, from a low base.** Precourse, 68% rated their skills average or below on a five-point scale and 6.7% chose the top rating; postcourse, 100% reported moderate or greater improvement and 91.8% reported significant growth, with no student choosing either of the two lowest options.
- **Students ranked the literacy as a career skill, second only to communication.** 54% placed digital and GenAI literacy in their top three of nine tracked skills, against 71% for communication and 45% for collaboration, and 75.4% strongly agreed plus 24% agreed that the course developed career competencies they will use at work.
- **The portfolio evidence points the same way as the survey.** 52.7% of students featured GenAI prominently in their Learning Statement and 76.3% included at least one GenAI-related topic in their Tip Sheets, with titles such as "How To Use AI Without Losing Your Voice" and "Tips for Responsible AI Use".
- **The assistant framing survives contact with the tool's failures.** Students coached and scolded their assistants by name — one exasperated "Cam, you're lying again! We've discussed this!" produced an unplanned lesson on [[hallucination-risk|hallucination]], [[prompt-engineering|prompt design]] and weighted tokens — and their performance reviews judge output against genre conventions rather than accepting it.
- **The course policy the class wrote is the transferable artifact.** It rests on four principles the students chose: protecting learning over efficiency or convenience, maintaining authorial integrity and the writer–audience relationship, transparency in tool use, and human creativity, [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]] and authentic voice.

## How the study was done

The setting is a required, writing-intensive business writing course that Rodak teaches as a teaching-focused, non-tenure-track faculty member, run in three fully enrolled sections in one semester for 72 students, under IRB Protocol #29843. Hire AI was built into the existing curriculum rather than added beside it, and every stage was graded complete or incomplete with revision available, which is the mechanism that kept the work [[formative-assessment|formative]] while still requiring a submission: a minimum of five Hypothesis annotations on the MLA-CCCC working paper, an in-class policy design exercise, a scope of work with at least ten items on each side, a job ad and avatar, and team revision of a bad-news email with a written performance review of the assistant.

The evidence is mixed and mostly self-reported. Students completed pre- and post-course diagnostic surveys on a five-point scale, wrote reflections and learning statements, and produced individual and team artifacts, and the final capstone asked them to address recruiters in their future industry and explain what they could now do. Rodak also draws on course evaluations and on the artifacts themselves, including four team performance reviews quoted in the paper. There is no comparison section, no control group and no independent measure of writing quality; the argument for the design rests on what students said and produced, read by the instructor who designed and taught it.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors:** give the tool a role before giving it a task. A named assistant with a written scope of work converts "may I use AI" into "is this inside the job", and the students' own lists show they will draw the line tighter than a prohibition would — every list in the study excluded undisclosed copy-paste and AI-invented sources.
- **Faculty developers:** sell it as low-lift. Rodak's explicit claim is that instructors do not need to become technology experts, because the framework runs on disciplinary judgment, a metaphor and a set of writing prompts, which is what makes it adoptable by contingent faculty with full loads.
- **Curriculum designers:** build the sequence into weeks you already have rather than adding a unit. The four steps map onto existing reading, discussion, drafting and revision work, and the persistent chat thread becomes the record that makes the later reflection and performance review possible.
- **Assessment designers:** grade the process artifacts, not the prose alone. The scope of work, the annotated thread and the performance review are where the [[reflection|reflective]] and [[metacognition|metacognitive]] work is visible, and they are also what a student can carry to an employer as evidence.

## Limitations

- The paper reports no formal limitations section, so the constraints have to be read off the design: one instructor, one course, one institution and one semester, with the author teaching, designing and evaluating the intervention she is reporting.
- All outcome measures are self-report or instructor-read artifacts. The post-course item asks how much the course improved the student's skills, which invites a ceiling effect, and 100% reporting improvement is consistent with that wording rather than proof of it.
- The pre/post comparison uses different questions, one asking students to rate their current skill and the other asking how much the course improved it, so the two distributions are not a before-and-after measure of the same construct.
- 72 students in three sections of a required business writing course are a narrow population, the design has no comparison section and no delayed post-test, and the writing itself is never rated by anyone other than the instructor.
- Students could opt out of GenAI use, and few did, so the sample describes volunteers for the approach rather than students who resisted it; the framework has since been taken up by instructors in STEM courses, but the paper reports those adoptions as interest and plans rather than as evidence.
