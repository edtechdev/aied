> **Synthesis:** Yang and colleagues reviewed 57 SSCI journal publications from 2017 to 2025 on artificial intelligence supporting [[computational-thinking|computational thinking]] in K-12 [[stem-education|STEM education]]. Using PRISMA screening, activity-theory coding, and [[network-analysis|epistemic network analysis]], they found that [[primary-education|elementary school]] students were the most studied group and that expert systems and robotics were the most common AI types, usually cast as learning tools. Most studies evaluated effectiveness over periods typically spanning 2–6 months, used [[quantitative-research|quantitative]] methods, and favored exploratory learning in cross-disciplinary settings. Coverage of computational-thinking practices and concepts outweighed coverage of perspectives. The review maps the field and recommends broader participants, richer assessment, and more work on AI as a collaborative partner; it is a synthesis of study counts and coded features, not a pooled estimate of effects.

## Key Findings
1. **Elementary students dominate the sample.** Elementary school students were the most frequently studied group, followed by junior and senior [[secondary-education|high school]] students.
2. **AI is mainly a tool.** Expert systems and robotics were the most common AI types, and AI most often served as a learning tool rather than a partner or facilitator.
3. **Effectiveness questions dominate.** Evaluating how well AI supports computational thinking was the most common research purpose, and most interventions lasted no longer than six months.
4. **Quantitative designs prevail.** Quantitative methods outnumbered mixed and [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] approaches, with [[self-report-measures|questionnaires]] and tests the most frequent assessment methods.
5. **Settings are cross-disciplinary.** Cross-disciplinary learning domains were most common, and mixed participant interaction was the most frequent form of interaction.
6. **Cognitive outcomes lead.** Cognition was the most common research focus, and computational-thinking practices and concepts were studied more often than perspectives.
7. **Coverage gaps remain.** Preschool children, teachers, [[generative-ai|generative AI]] roles, and affective or social dimensions received comparatively little attention.

## How the review was conducted

The authors searched Web of Science and Scopus for English-language SSCI journal articles published on or before 31 December 2025, using combinations of STEM, computational-thinking, AI, and K-12 search terms applied to titles, abstracts, and keywords. Two independent reviewers screened records, with Cohen's kappa coefficient of 0.82. PRISMA screening began with 108 records; 17 editorials, corrections, and early-access items were removed, leaving 91 publications. Title and abstract screening excluded 15 studies unrelated to [[k-12|K-12 education]] and five not formally published by the end of 2025. Full-text review excluded 14 studies that did not address computational-thinking assessment, interdisciplinarity, or AI integration. The remaining 57 studies were coded.

The coding framework drew on [[activity-theory-aied|activity theory]]: subjects were learners and sample sizes; tools were AI types and roles; objects were purposes, periods, and computational-thinking dimensions; rules were methods, statistics, strategies, and assessment; communities were learning domains; division of labor was participant interaction; and outcomes were research foci. Computational thinking was treated in three dimensions — concepts, practices, and perspectives — following the framework adopted from Luo and colleagues. Epistemic network analysis was used to display co-occurrence among elements within computational-thinking concepts, practices, and perspectives across research purposes. The authors report that coding was discussed by two researchers until consensus was reached.

## What the 57 studies contained

Among research subjects, 26 studies focused on elementary school students and 22 on junior and senior high school students; five were mixed, two studied teachers, one studied preschool children, and one fell into other categories. Sample sizes above 80 participants appeared in 29 studies; 41–60 participants in nine; 61–80 in seven; 21–40 in six; fewer than five in three; and 5–20 in two. One study did not state its sample size. Expert systems and knowledge-based systems were the most used AI type (n = 21), usually as learning tools. Robotics appeared in 19 studies: 15 used robots as learning tools, while four explored them as tools, teachers, and learners. Intelligent agents appeared in eight studies, six of which used generative AI; mixed AI types appeared in three, and other [[ai-technologies|AI technologies]] in six.

Evaluating AI effectiveness was the leading purpose (n = 28), followed by investigating perception fields (n = 10), evaluating learner or task characteristics (n = 8), designing AI-enabled systems (n = 4), mixed purposes (n = 5), and other purposes (n = 2). Research periods most often fell at ≤ 6 months (n = 16), followed by ≤ 4 weeks (n = 12), ≤ 8 weeks (n = 10), and ≤ 1 day (n = 6); one study each ran ≤ 12 months and > 12 months, nine did not specify a period, and two fell into other categories. The authors summarize the typical duration as 2–6 months. Quantitative methods were used in 33 studies, [[mixed-methods-research|mixed methods]] in 20, and qualitative methods in four. Questionnaires were the most common assessment (n = 39), followed by tests and examinations (n = 31), evaluation of artifacts (n = 17), and verbal interviews (n = 14). Exploratory learning was the leading strategy (n = 18), followed by game-based or competitive activities (n = 14) and [[project-based-learning|project-based learning]] (n = 13).

Cross-disciplinary domains appeared in 39 studies, technology in 14, and science in four. Mixed interaction types were most common (n = 23), followed by AI-supported [[teacher-role|teacher]]–student interaction (n = 20) and AI-supported peer interaction (n = 10). Computational-thinking practices were coded in 55 studies and concepts in 48, while perspectives appeared in 25. Epistemic network results showed decomposition acting as a hub node in effectiveness studies, with loops central in perception and learner-characteristic studies; the authors state that conceptual and practical dimensions were strongly linked while the perspectival dimension was largely neglected. Cognition was the leading research focus (n = 55), followed by affect (n = 35) and learning behavior (n = 8). China contributed 25 studies and the United States 11, and publication counts reached 13 in 2025.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Check that AI-supported tasks address computational-thinking perspectives such as expressing, connecting, and questioning, not just decomposition and algorithm design.
- **Instructors.** Use the activity-theory elements as a design checklist: who acts, which AI tool is used, what the task object is, which rules and strategies apply, and how participants interact.
- **Curriculum designers.** Give attention to mixed teacher–student–machine interaction, since blended interactions were common in the reviewed studies but role division is seldom analyzed.
- **Researchers.** Favor mixed-method assessment and process data where possible; the review notes that questionnaires and tests dominate while video, self-report, and log data are rare.
- **Policymakers.** Support longer-term and more diverse studies, including preschool learners and teachers, before drawing broad conclusions about generative AI as a collaborative partner.

## Limitations
- **Narrow publication base.** Only SSCI journal publications in English were included, which may have excluded relevant work in other journals or languages.
- **Database coverage.** The paper describes searching both Web of Science and Scopus, but its limitations section states that the review was based solely on SSCI journals from Web of Science; this inconsistency is not resolved in the text.
- **No pooled effect estimate.** The synthesis combines coded study features and network displays rather than [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|meta-analytic]] effect sizes.
- **Coding framework boundaries.** The authors note that tool and division-of-labor dimensions could be expanded to cover learning tasks and generative-AI teacher–student–machine interaction.
- **Sparse long-term evidence.** Studies exceeding one year were rare, limiting conclusions about sustained computational-thinking development.
- **Interpretive ENA limits.** The network results describe co-occurrence in coded studies; the authors also note that significance of computational-thinking elements varies by task type and research objective.
