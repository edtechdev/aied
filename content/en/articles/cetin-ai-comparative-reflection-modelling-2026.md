> **Synthesis:** Çetin and Bilgili ask what happens when a learner's own work is set beside a polished [[generative-ai|generative AI]] output rather than replaced by it. Twelve pre-service primary mathematics teachers each built a model of water use in a school garden, then compared it with the same ChatGPT-4o model and wrote about the comparison. Most treated the AI output as an object of comparison — identifying limitations in both models, noticing alternative representations and describing how they would revise their own — and their accounts contained indications of critical, creative and [[metacognition|metacognitive]] reasoning. But several referred to the output as the "answer key", some criticisms repeated weaknesses the AI had listed about itself, and none noticed a computational inconsistency deliberately retained in the model. The study is an exploratory case study with no comparison condition and no pre–post measure, so it describes patterns of reasoning rather than gains.

## Key Findings

- **Comparison, not substitution.** Most participants used the AI model as something to compare against rather than as a ready-made answer: they identified limitations in both models, recognized alternative variables and representations, and said how they would revise their own.
- **The framing invited authority.** Several called the output the "answer key", "answer sheet" or "solution sheet"; the printed handout was headed "Activity Solution", which the authors suggest may have reinforced the reading of the AI model as definitive.
- **Critiques were partly borrowed.** Some criticisms of the AI model coincided with weaknesses the AI had itself listed in its output — questioning how coefficients were determined, its treatment of plant types, its omission of soil type. Because participants read that [[self-assessment]], the authors cannot tell independent judgment from repetition.
- **Nothing verified the arithmetic.** No participant's comparison or reflection referred to a discrepancy between the AI's formula and its summary table, or to its poor fit to one day's data — an inconsistency the researchers had identified and deliberately left in place.
- **Reasoning was visible, not measured.** Accounts contained indications of critical, creative and metacognitive reasoning, but with no comparison group or pre–post measure the study cannot attribute any change in thinking to the activity.
- **One output, one model.** Every participant compared against the same AI model and no human-produced model, so the study cannot separate what belongs to comparison itself from what belongs to the AI as the comparison partner.

## The activity and its analysis

The design was a single-case exploratory study in a Primary School Mathematics Teaching program (preparing teachers for Grades 5–8). Participants modeled irrigation water for a school garden under varying conditions, then compared their model with a ChatGPT-4o model using two guiding questions, and finally completed a [[reflection]] and [[feedback]] form. The researchers analyzed the modeling products, the written comparisons and the reflection forms with inductive content analysis. They were explicit about what was not done: no comparison group, no pre–post measure, and no judgment of the relative quality of the participants' models and the AI's — the units of analysis were what participants wrote about the two models, not the models themselves.

## What the comparison produced

The comparisons did real work. Participants evaluated the AI solution on the variables it included and omitted rather than as simply right or wrong — one noted that their own model accounted for soil fertility while the AI's did not, and another objected that the AI reduced plant types to a count, losing information their own table kept. They also assessed their own models against what they saw, noticing approaches absent from their work such as mathematical expressions and quantitative relationships between variables. The authors read this as comparison creating occasions where reasoning becomes visible and discussable in [[teacher-education|teacher education]].

## Where the authority leaked in

Two features qualify that reading. First, the perceived status of the AI solution: labels such as "answer key" and "answer sheet" recurred, and the handout's own heading, "Activity Solution", plausibly encouraged treating a fallible model as the reference. Second, the origin of the criticisms: because the AI's output listed its own weaknesses, several participant critiques matched those items, and some participants referred to that table explicitly. The study therefore cannot establish whether these critiques were independent. The deliberately retained computational inconsistency — visible in the mismatch between the AI's formula and its summary table — went unnoticed by every participant, which the authors take as evidence that a polished output can be admired without being verified.

## What this means for practice

- **Teachers.** Treat the AI output as one draft among several, and say so — the study's own handout heading, "Activity Solution", may have pushed readers toward authority.
- **Teachers.** Have students compare two or three models, including their own and a peer's, rather than one human model against a single AI output, so critique is not anchored to a reference answer.
- **[[teacher-education|Teacher educators]].** Ask students to state the basis of each criticism: whether it came from their own analysis or from a weakness the AI listed about itself.
- **Researchers.** Treat verification as a separate measured step: the comparison provoked reasoning, but no participant checked the AI's arithmetic, so a comparison task is not a verification task.
- **Designers.** Retain or seed a checkable flaw when trialling a comparison activity, so that the quality of scrutiny, not just the fluency of the critique, can be observed.

## Limitations

- A single cohort of 12 pre-service teachers in one program, with no comparison group, so nothing can be attributed to the activity.
- No pre–post measure: the findings describe reasoning displayed during the task, not improvements in critical, creative or metacognitive skill or in modeling competence.
- Every participant compared against the same AI model and no human-produced model, so the AI-specific contribution cannot be separated from comparison in general.
- The researchers supplied the AI model and the handout, including its "Activity Solution" heading, which may have shaped how authoritative participants found it.
- Several participant critiques cannot be distinguished from the weaknesses the AI had already listed about itself.
