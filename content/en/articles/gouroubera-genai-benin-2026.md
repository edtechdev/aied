> **Synthesis:** University students in Benin reach for [[generative-ai|generative AI]] because they are overloaded, not because they are convinced the tool is useful. In a survey of 633 students at eight universities, use was near-universal — most opened the tool for academic work at least twice a week — and academic workload was the strongest driver, while age, gender and level of study made no difference. Heavier use went with better self-rated academic performance, but also with more procrastination, a [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulation]] cost, and more [[self-report-measures|self-reported]] memory decline; most students could not say whether AI text counted as plagiarism. The study documents an ambivalent experience at one moment in time; it cannot show that the tool caused either the gain or the costs.

## Key Findings

1. **Nearly every student uses generative AI, and often.** 92.3% used it for academic work at least twice a week; 30.9% used it four times a week and 10.1% more than five times. Adoption is no longer the thing an institution has to solve.
2. **Workload, not enthusiasm for the tool, is the strongest driver.** Academic workload was the largest predictor of use, well ahead of time pressure, sensitivity to quality and sensitivity to rewards. Students reach for the tool because they are overloaded, not because they have been persuaded it is useful.
3. **Demographics do not predict use.** Age, gender and education level were all non-significant, contrary to prior research. When the pressure is general and the tool is free at the point of use, adoption stops sorting by who the student is.
4. **Use travels with perceived gain and perceived cost at the same time.** Heavier use was associated with better self-rated academic performance, but also with more procrastination and more self-perceived memory decline, and procrastination was the largest of the three associations.
5. **Workload reaches outcomes through use.** The indirect paths from academic workload through GenAI use were significant for performance, memory decline and procrastination alike, which is the study's account of how pressure turns into both benefit and cost.
6. **Integrity awareness lags far behind adoption.** 81% were unaware or unsure whether GenAI content could count as plagiarism, or how to cite it; only 19% were aware. The rule arrived after the practice.

## Why workload, not usefulness, is the strongest driver

The theoretical setup promises a [[motivation|motivational]] explanation. Expectancy-value theory supplies utility value, attainment value and perceived cost; [[technology-acceptance-model|UTAUT]] supplies performance expectancy, effort expectancy and facilitating conditions. Both predict that workload and deadlines push students toward the tool.

The estimates rank the constructs differently. Academic Workload produced the strongest path (β = 0.384, p < 0.001), against Time Pressure (β = 0.129), Sensitivity to Quality (β = 0.129) and Sensitivity to Rewards (β = 0.112). The four drivers together explain R² = 0.446 of the variance in use. The effect sizes disagree — Sensitivity to Rewards has the largest effect size with the smallest coefficient — and the predictors share enough variance that their order should not be read finely. What survives is the gap between workload and everything else.

The authors read use as a coping response to limited institutional support and constrained educational infrastructure. The setting fits: support services and connectivity are uneven, while a phone-based tool needs no institutional provisioning. That also explains the null demographics — when pressure is general and the tool is free at the point of use, adoption stops sorting by age, gender or degree.

The implication cuts against acceptance research's reflex. If use followed perceived usefulness or effort expectancy, the lever would be awareness or interface design; here 92.3% already use the tool, and the condition producing use is the assignment load.

What the design cannot show is whether workload is cause or accounting: students who already delay may report heavier workload, and the construct is four items about feeling overloaded, not an audited count.

## A benefit and two costs, measured by the same instrument

The three outcomes are not measured to the same standard. Academic Performance is a single self-reported item on a six-point scale from 1 (significantly worsened) to 6 (significantly improved), a judgment of change with no grade or artifact behind it. Procrastination is four items adapted from Choi and Moran, and Self-Perceived Memory Decline is three. The performance path (β = 0.429) therefore rests on one ordinal self-rating while both costs rest on multi-item scales.

That asymmetry shapes the headline. Heavier use was associated with better perceived performance (β = 0.429), more procrastination (β = 0.525) and more perceived memory decline (β = 0.415), all at p < 0.001. The triad is not a trade-off among commensurable outcomes: a heavy user who feels their work improved answers both the use items and the performance item from the same self-concept, so the association can come from consistency, felt fluency, or real improvement. The paper concedes that the design cannot establish that GenAI use caused these outcomes, but the abstract still frames a dual role — a stronger claim than one self-rating can carry.

Self-perceived memory decline has a related limit. It is a belief about memory, measured by items about forgetting and feeling as though one is losing one's memory. That is a real cost, but not a retention test, and the [[cognitive-offloading|cognitive offloading]] literature's claims about durable learning rest on delayed unaided performance. Reading the association as evidence that GenAI erodes memory overstates three Likert items.

Direction is unresolved too. Procrastination is the largest path, which admits a reverse reading: students who delay use GenAI to compress work at the end. That is one of at least two stories consistent with the covariance.

## What saturation does to the equity story

The demographic result is easy to overread. Age, gender and education were non-significant where 92.3% used the tool at least twice a week, which the authors attribute to smartphones and mobile internet and read as adoption transcending traditional divides. Their own qualification is better: basic access may be equalizing, while [[digital-divide|second-level divides]] — data affordability, network reliability, digital literacy — decide whether access converts into benefit, and this study measured none.

That qualifies how this knowledge base usually tells the [[global-south|Global South]] story, which is mostly about constraint. It is bounded hard: 85.2% public universities, 62.4% agronomic and environmental sciences, 70.3% bachelor's, non-probability recruitment. The study describes eight institutions, not Beninese higher education.

The integrity number is more actionable. 81% of students were unaware or unsure that GenAI output could count as plagiarism, or how to cite it. Adoption outran the rule roughly four to one. Where use is near-universal and the norm is not understood, [[academic-integrity|integrity]] is a [[ai-literacy|literacy]] problem before it is a policing problem: detection would penalize a rule most students cannot state.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors:** Redesign the demand, not the attitude. If workload drives use, staggering deadlines and staging large assignments changes the condition that produces last-minute AI use. A workshop for students who already use the tool weekly has little room to move.
- **Instructors:** Grade process artifacts, not only products. Because the performance outcome is a self-rating, add one task the student cannot self-assess: an unaided exercise, a short oral defense, or a graded revision log.
- **Instructors:** Teach the citation rule as a rule. Give students the concrete form — what must be disclosed, how AI assistance is cited in this course, what counts as unacceptable — and test it on examples. The 81% is a norm that was never made operational for them.
- **Administrators:** Treat GenAI as coping infrastructure in low-resource systems. Prohibition without workload relief relocates use rather than removing it, and access quality — data cost, connectivity, device capability — needs measuring separately from access itself.
- **Researchers:** Read perceived improvement as experience, not achievement. Label the performance result as self-reported perceived change, and pair it with an outcome measure from a different source.

## Limitations

- **Cross-sectional [[quantitative-research|survey]], single wave, no control group.** Every path, including the mediation paths, is a covariance at one time point; the authors state the design supports association, not causation.
- **Convenience sampling, one country, eight institutions.** 633 of 645 responses were retained and no response rate is reported. 85.2% public, 62.4% agronomic, 70.3% bachelor's, 6.6% doctoral: the null for education level rests mostly on a bachelor's-versus-master's contrast.
- **Every construct is self-report.** Academic Performance is one perceived-change rating, Self-Perceived Memory Decline is perceptual, and no common-method test is reported.
- **Measurement is marginal at two points.** Three of eight GenAI items were dropped for loadings between 0.593 and 0.696, and the GenAI average variance extracted is 0.521 against a 0.50 threshold. The HTMT between Academic Workload and Time Pressure is 0.848 against a 0.85 ceiling, so discriminant validity is weakest between the two strongest drivers.
- **Two mediation decisions do not match their intervals.** Sensitivity to Rewards → GenAI use → Academic Performance is 0.048 with a 95% CI of [−0.001; 0.094] and p = 0.044, and the procrastination path is 0.059 with [−0.001; 0.117] and p = 0.045; both are marked Accepted although the intervals include zero.
- **Explanatory power is modest and the power claim is circular.** R² runs from 0.172 to 0.446, leaving most outcome variance unexplained, and the post hoc calculation used the observed f² = 0.79 to return power of approximately 1.00 — which the authors acknowledge is descriptive rather than confirmation of adequacy.
