> **Synthesis:** Ortega and colleagues reviewed empirical studies of artificial intelligence in secondary and [[higher-ed|higher education]], focusing on learning, motivation, academic emotions, and risks. A search of four databases for work published between 2016 and 2026 yielded six eligible studies, which were synthesized qualitatively rather than pooled statistically. The review reports favorable associations between pedagogically integrated AI and learning, engagement, and emotional regulation, alongside risks of [[cognitive-offloading|overreliance]], reduced autonomy, and technology-related [[anxiety-and-stress|anxiety]]. It treats academic emotions broadly, including [[self-efficacy]], cognitive load, uncertainty, anxiety, and procrastination. The authors conclude that AI's educational value depends on how it is designed and supervised, not on its mere presence. They also stress that the evidence is mostly observational, drawn predominantly from universities, and too heterogeneous to establish durable effects in secondary classrooms.

## Key Findings
1. **The review narrowed six studies from a broad search.** Four databases produced an initial set of records, but strict criteria on AI use, motivation or emotion outcomes, and student population left a small [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] corpus.
2. **Learning outcomes were generally positive.** The included studies reported better mathematics performance with more constructive interaction and stronger prediction of negative emotions from self-efficacy and related variables.
3. **Motivation and engagement appeared alongside caveats.** Positive attitudes toward [[generative-ai|ChatGPT]] were linked to usefulness and ease of use, while low [[motivation|intrinsic motivation]] and procrastination were linked to dependence.
4. **Emotional outcomes were mixed.** Personalized and socially supportive AI was associated with regulation and [[well-being]], but other work linked AI use to anxiety, performance concerns, or emotional disengagement.
5. **Risks were part of the synthesis.** Overreliance, loss of autonomy, dependence, and threats to critical thinking were treated as outcomes in their own right.
6. **The evidence base is skewed.** Most samples were university students, and the designs were largely cross-sectional and descriptive.
7. **The authors call for stronger designs.** [[longitudinal-research|Longitudinal]] and experimental work with control groups is needed before sustainable effects can be claimed.

## What the review searched and included

The search was run on February 3, 2026 across Web of Science, Scopus, Dialnet, and PubMed, covering 2016 to 2026 and English or Spanish articles. The initial yield was 28 records from Web of Science, 11 from Scopus, 11 from Dialnet, and 10 from PubMed, for 60 records before deduplication. Three duplicates were removed, leaving 57; eight records were excluded for period or language, leaving 49. Screening of titles, abstracts, and keywords excluded 21, leaving 28 for full-text review. At that stage, 22 publications were excluded: two without full text, 15 systematic reviews, and five that failed other criteria. Six empirical studies entered the qualitative synthesis.

The review used a PICO framework but explicitly allowed studies without comparison groups because of heterogeneous designs. Data extraction recorded year, country, sample, design, variables, and findings, then grouped results into learning and performance, motivation and engagement, emotional variables, and risks. No [[quantitative-research|quantitative]] synthesis was attempted. Methodological quality was assessed with the 2018 [[mixed-methods-research|Mixed Methods]] Appraisal Tool: two studies received 100% (High) and four received 80% (Medium). Screening and appraisal were led by one reviewer, with a random 20% of records and appraisal sheets independently cross-checked by an external researcher until consensus was reached.

## What the six studies reported

The studies covered high school and college samples in China, Spain, and several Middle Eastern countries. The reported designs were mostly cross-sectional quantitative analyses, with some mixed-methods work. Ma and colleagues used [[machine-learning|machine learning]] to predict negative academic emotions in 1,708 high school students, identifying self-efficacy and emotional control as important predictors; Random Forest gave the best predictive performance. Abdaljaleel and colleagues surveyed 2,240 college students across seven countries and validated seven dimensions of a technology-acceptance instrument for ChatGPT. Galindo-Domínguez and colleagues studied 467 college students and reported that low intrinsic motivation and high procrastination predicted dependence on ChatGPT.

Zong and Yang used structural equation modeling with 816 college students and reported positive associations of AI-enhanced [[social-emotional-learning|social-emotional learning]] with engagement and emotional regulation. Lin and Chen examined AI-integrated applications and reported improvements in motivation and emotional regulation along with reduced academic uncertainty. Liu and colleagues studied [[secondary-education|middle school]] students working with AI [[learning-by-teaching|teachable agents]] in mathematics and found that the students who improved most were those with the most constructive interaction patterns. Across these studies, the review emphasizes that how students interact with AI, rather than simple access to it, is associated with outcomes.

The discussion expands beyond the six included studies to related reviews about [[human-ai-collaboration|hybrid intelligence]], [[affective-computing|affective computing]], [[automated-assessment|automated assessment]], and [[teacher-role|teacher]] acceptance. It notes that ChatGPT can increase engagement while requiring supervision, that using AI as a substitute can undermine deliberate practice and autonomy, and that adolescents may be more vulnerable than university students to emotional and identity-related harms. The authors frame secondary and higher education together as transitional stages where scaffolded adolescent use contrasts with more autonomous university use.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Establish clear protocols for supervised AI use in writing tasks instead of assuming that access alone improves learning.
- **Instructors.** Teach students to check the veracity of AI-generated content, since the review identifies [[critical-thinking|critical evaluation]] as a recurring concern.
- **Curriculum designers.** Design AI-supported activities around active student contribution rather than substitution for deliberate practice.
- **Administrators.** Treat anxiety, dependence, and autonomy as outcomes to monitor alongside performance when introducing AI tools.
- **Researchers.** Prioritize longitudinal and experimental studies in secondary schools, where the current evidence base is thinnest.

## Limitations
- Only six empirical studies met the inclusion criteria, limiting any overall conclusion.
- Heterogeneous designs, from predictive modeling to mixed-methods studies of teachable agents, prevented quantitative pooling.
- Screening and quality appraisal were conducted primarily by one reviewer; external cross-checking covered only a subset.
- Higher-education samples predominate, so generalization to secondary students is limited.
- The search used narrow keyword combinations requiring all three conceptual blocks to appear together, which the authors note produced a small final set.
- Most included studies were cross-sectional, so predictive language in the [[primary-education|primary]] sources does not establish causal or developmental order.
