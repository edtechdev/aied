> **Synthesis:** Mora-Secaira and colleagues evaluated the quality of a learning management system at one Ecuadorian university by combining survey data from students, faculty members, and administrative–technical staff with semi-structured interviews and an integrated interpretation of the two. A four-dimension model covering technical, pedagogical, usability, and administrative aspects showed an acceptable overall structural fit, and the platform was judged strongest as a system for academic management and stable operation. Faculty members rated it more critically than the other two groups, and interviews identified weak pedagogical interaction, limited formative assessment, missing accessibility provisions, and absent integrity tools. Across all three groups, the implementation of plagiarism and [[ai-detection|AI-content detection]] emerged as a stated requirement, and the authors discuss artificial intelligence only as a future integration possibility, not as an intervention the study tested.

## Key Findings

1. **A four-dimension framework represented perceived platform quality acceptably.** The technical, pedagogical, usability, and administrative dimensions fit the data at a reasonable overall level, though some incremental fit indices fell below stricter thresholds.
2. **Faculty members were the most critical raters.** Students and administrative–technical staff gave more favorable evaluations across the dimensions that were measured.
3. **Administrative management was the clearest strength.** Participants across the three groups recognized the system as efficient for academic management and operationally stable.
4. **Pedagogy was the weakest area.** Interviews pointed to little interaction, few collaborative tools, and limited formative assessment and structured feedback.
5. **Integrity and inclusion were shared priorities.** Participants called for plagiarism and AI-generated-content detection and for accessibility and universal design principles.
6. **The two phases told the same story at different depths.** Quantitative ratings were moderate to positive, while interviews exposed structural limitations that the ratings alone did not reveal.
7. **Including technical staff changed the diagnosis.** Adding the perspective of the people who maintain and secure the platform broadened the evaluation beyond end-user satisfaction.

## What the study measured

The quantitative phase included 230 users: 140 students, 80 faculty members, and 10 administrative–technical staff members at the Universidad Técnica Estatal de Quevedo. The qualitative phase involved 20 students, 12 faculty members, and 8 administrative–technical staff members. The standardized instrument had already been validated through two Delphi rounds with 12 experts, reaching consensus levels exceeding 80%, and was organized into four dimensions, thirteen criteria, and thirty indicators. The authors applied descriptive statistics, confirmatory factor analysis, multigroup measurement invariance testing, and robust group comparisons, then integrated the phases through methodological triangulation.

The confirmatory model specified four correlated latent factors and thirty observed variables. Its configural solution gave a χ² value of 1,177.297 with 720 degrees of freedom and a χ²/df ratio of 1.635, with RMSEA = 0.054 (90% CI = 0.048–0.059), CFI = 0.838, TLI = 0.804, and GFI = 0.756. The authors regard the parsimony-adjusted and approximation-error indices as acceptable given the model's complexity, while noting that the incremental indices were below stricter thresholds and that some indicators showed low or negative loadings. Reliability was strong: the global Cronbach's alpha was 0.950, composite reliability ranged from 0.84 to 0.91, and average variance extracted ranged from 0.51 to 0.55.

A Harman single-factor test on the thirty items found that the first factor accounted for 39.657% of the variance, below the 50% threshold used to flag common method bias. In the qualitative phase, two researchers coded 20% of the transcripts independently and reached Cohen's κ = 0.84. Across 629 quotations, students contributed 165, faculty members 280, and administrative–technical staff 184.

## Where the groups diverged

Faculty members recorded the lowest mean scores in most dimensions. On the technical dimension they scored 2.38, against 3.49 for students and 3.51 for administrative–technical staff; on the pedagogical dimension the corresponding means were 3.01, 3.50, and 3.53. For usability, students rated the platform highest at 3.55, followed by administrative–technical staff at 3.30 and faculty at 2.82. In the administrative dimension, administrative–technical staff scored highest at 3.85, students 3.43, and faculty 3.09.

Welch's ANOVA found significant differences among the groups on all four dimensions (p < 0.001 in each case). The largest effect was in the administrative dimension (η² = 0.494), followed by the pedagogical dimension (η² = 0.259), with moderate effects for usability (η² = 0.144) and technical quality (η² = 0.084). Games–Howell comparisons showed that students and faculty differed on all four dimensions (p < 0.001), while comparisons involving administrative–technical staff were less consistent. A bootstrap procedure based on 1,000 resamples produced minimal bias (|Bias| < 0.012). Measurement invariance between students and faculty was supported at the configural level but only weakly at the metric and scalar levels, so the authors caution against strict comparisons of latent means.

The interviews explained the ratings. The pedagogical dimension drew the largest share of quotations among faculty (55.7%), while students emphasized the technical dimension (40.0%). Participants described a system that worked as a content repository and academic management tool but offered little [[academic-integrity|integrity]] support, few rubrics or discussion spaces, and no systematic accessibility standards. Plagiarism and AI-generated-content detection were described as almost unanimous demands among faculty and administrative–technical staff and widely supported by students.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Treat the platform's strong administrative functions as a base, but press for the rubrics, discussion forums, and structured feedback mechanisms that faculty in the study said were missing.
- **Administrators.** Include the staff who configure, secure, and support the platform in quality reviews; their ratings differed from those of end users and their interviews surfaced distinct infrastructure concerns.
- **Instructional designers.** Prioritize accessibility and universal design in course templates, since the interviews identified their absence as both a user-experience problem and a compliance risk.
- **Administrators.** Plan integrity capacity explicitly, including plagiarism and AI-detection tooling, rather than assuming a management-oriented platform already covers assessment integrity.
- **Researchers.** Pair rating surveys with interviews when evaluating a platform; the qualitative phase revealed interaction, inclusion, and integrity limitations that moderate quantitative scores concealed.

## Limitations

- The study was conducted at a single institution and focused on engineering programs, so transfer to other universities and disciplines is asserted rather than demonstrated.
- The administrative–technical group numbered only 10 in the quantitative phase, limiting measurement invariance testing and inferential comparisons for that group.
- Sampling was purposive and participation voluntary in the qualitative phase, leaving open the possibility of self-selection bias.
- The evidence is overwhelmingly perception-based and self-reported; the authors identify usage logs, activity metrics, and learning analytics as indicators that could complement it.
- The cross-sectional design cannot show how quality perceptions change over time, and metric and scalar invariance were not fully supported.
- Several indicators showed low or negative loadings, pointing to a need for further refinement of the thirty-indicator instrument.
