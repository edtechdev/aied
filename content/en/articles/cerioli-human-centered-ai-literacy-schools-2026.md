> **Synthesis:** Governments are splitting into two camps on [[generative-ai|generative AI]] in schools — scaling tools or restricting them — and the iRAISE report argues both camps leave the same work undone. Drawing on a structured review, policy documents from France, Canada, Egypt and Qatar, and consultations with officials and NGOs, it reframes [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] as the newest layer of [[information-and-media-literacy|digital and media literacy]] rather than a separate subject. Its central empirical claim is an implementation gap: 171 of 194 countries reference media and information literacy in national frameworks, but only 84 (43%) put elements beyond technical digital skills into curricula, and few systems measure whether students acquire them.

*This page summarizes a multi-stakeholder policy report, not a peer-reviewed study. The report presents no new primary data: its quantitative claims are drawn from published research and official statistics, and its country analysis rests on document review and interviews with officials, NGOs and experts rather than with children, families or teachers.*

## Deploy or restrict, and what both camps skip

The report opens on a squeeze. The 2025 PISA results recorded the lowest average performance ever observed across OECD countries in science, reading, and mathematics, extending a reading decline that began before generative AI reached classrooms. Systems have responded by moving in opposite directions: Sweden, Finland, Denmark and France restricted phones during school hours for non-instructional use, Sweden returned to printed textbooks, and in 2026 Norway, New York City and Los Angeles restricted students' direct use of generative AI, while Estonia and India expanded AI tools or curricula.

The authors' argument is that this framing poses the wrong question. Whether a system deploys or restricts, it still has to prepare children to learn, socialize and take part in civic life in environments that digital technology shapes. They borrow Gert Biesta's three purposes of schooling — qualification, socialization and subjectification — and argue that as information becomes freely available outside school, the second and third become more important, not less.

## What the report actually measured

The methods are worth stating plainly, because the report's authority rests on breadth rather than on new evidence. It combines a structured review of global evidence (OECD, UNESCO, UNICEF, recent meta-analyses), analysis of policy documents, official statistics and independent evaluations for four countries chosen to contrast governance models, and interviews and consultations with government officials, NGOs and experts. In Egypt that meant the Ministry of Communications and Information Technology's Digital Community Development team; in Canada, leaders from Public Safety Canada, Actua and MediaSmarts; in France, digital-education officials; and in Qatar, a written consultation with the Ministry of Education and Higher Education.

The four systems differ by orders of magnitude. France has about 11.6 million students and 851,500 teachers at a system-wide ratio near 14:1. Canada has about 5.5 million students in public elementary and secondary schools with 422,000 educators at roughly 13:1. Egypt has about 28.5 million pre-university students and 1.0–1.1 million teachers, with a system-wide ratio near 25:1 and 31:1 in primary schools. Qatar has about 419,000 students in 649 public and private schools and kindergartens, with 17,700 teachers in government schools and a system-wide ratio near 10:1.

## The gap that matters is between frameworks and classrooms

The report's sharpest contribution is a distinction the [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] literature tends to blur: the difference between a competence framework and a curriculum. Frameworks such as DigComp and France's CRCN describe what students should be able to do; [[curriculum-design|curricula]] turn them into sequenced learning experiences with time attached. UNESCO's 2025 mapping found that 171 of 194 Member States referenced media and information literacy or related competences in national frameworks, but only 84 (43%) included elements beyond technical digital skills in school curricula. An earlier UNESCO count put the baseline lower still: only about half of countries worldwide have defined the digital skills they expect learners to acquire.

Measurement is thinner than either. Few education systems track whether students actually acquire these competences, and the report treats the forthcoming PISA 2029 assessment of Media and AI Literacy as the first comparable instrument at scale. Where evaluation exists, effects are narrower than advocates claim: two recent US evaluations found that programs improved students' knowledge, but changes in behavior were mainly observed among older students.

## Four governance models, one shared conclusion

The country comparison yields a typology rather than a ranking. France pairs a sovereign framework — the CRCN competence framework, the Pix certification and a data-protection gateway — with territorial inequalities and a teacher-training bottleneck. Canada combines NGO programs with broad reported reach but unmeasured depth, funded through time-limited grants and coordinated in fragments. Egypt scaled infrastructure rapidly but faces equity gaps, teachers' anxiety about technical failures and parental distrust of digital learning. Qatar has closed the access divide but now confronts wellbeing concerns and a shortage of Arabic-language AI content.

The conclusion across all four is the report's most portable finding: infrastructure and content alone did not guarantee effective outcomes. Teacher confidence, parental mediation and community trust mattered as much as policy design. That is a claim about implementation, and it cuts against [[educational-policy-ai|procurement-led strategies]] that treat devices and platforms as the intervention.

## Key Findings

1. **Human relationships remain an irreplaceable condition for learning.** The report grounds this in studies of caring teachers, teacher enjoyment and students' sense of meaning, and concludes that AI should support human pedagogy rather than replace it.
2. **Schooling has a collective civic function that AI-era policy must protect.** Socialization happens in the hidden curriculum, and fragmented personalized pathways risk weakening the common ground that democratic dialogue requires.
3. **Effective programs rest on three pillars — resources, content design and pedagogy — and are unevenly implemented.** Teacher training, infrastructure, partnerships and dedicated timetable time are the resources most often missing.
4. **Frameworks are abundant while curricula, instruction and measurement lag.** Only 84 of 194 countries put media and information literacy beyond technical skills into curricula, and few systems measure acquisition.
5. **NGOs and universities act as human infrastructure.** They translate national strategies into local practice and reach marginalized youth, but project-based funding and weak evaluation capacity constrain them.
6. **Inter-ministerial coordination is a precondition, with the education ministry as coordinator.** No single ministry can govern a technology that touches health, child protection, justice and culture.
7. **Human factors decide outcomes in every governance model studied.** France, Canada, Egypt and Qatar differ in structure but converge on teacher confidence, parental mediation and community trust.

## What this means for practice

- **Policymakers: legislate the timetable, not just the framework.** The report's recurring finding is that curricula, training and materials have limited impact unless schools reserve time, and that systems must decide what that time replaces.
- **Teacher educators: put [[teacher-ai-competency|training]] inside paid working time.** Training should begin in initial teacher education, continue through careers, and differentiate by subject and role rather than offering one generic AI course.
- **School leaders and funders: treat teacher confidence as infrastructure, and fund it over years.** In all four case studies, anxiety and confidence shaped whether tools were used at all, and political cycles are shorter than the time training and evaluation need to show effects.
- **Curriculum designers: build AI literacy on media and information literacy rather than beside it.** The report reads AI literacy as the newest component of digital literacy, which is also the integration PISA 2029 will assess.
- **Regulators: require evidence of efficacy and human oversight.** The report asks regulators to check that tools offered to teachers protect student data and are backed by independent evidence, and never to automate high-stakes decisions such as grading or placement.

## Limitations

- Most of the quantitative evidence the report reviews is correlational, drawn from cross-sectional studies and meta-analyses of associations; reverse causation and confounders such as socioeconomic status and home environment are rarely fully controlled.
- Children, adolescents and parents were not consulted directly, so their perspectives enter only through officials, NGOs, experts and the survey literature.
- The four case studies cover different regions and governance models but include no low-income country and no Asian or South American system, so transfer to very different resource settings is untested.
- The country indicators are approximate and, by the authors' own note, not fully harmonized across countries.
- The report proposes a Universal Learning & AI Literacy Toolkit but does not evaluate one; its recommendations are design proposals rather than tested interventions.
- As a multi-stakeholder advocacy report, its framing reflects the priorities of its coalition, and its claims about AI's learning effects depend on the studies it cites rather than on evidence it generated.
