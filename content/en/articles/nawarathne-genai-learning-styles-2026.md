> **Synthesis:** Nawarathne, Haddela and Kodagoda (2026) review how [[generative-ai|generative AI]] can be integrated with four established [[learning-theories|learning style models]] — VARK, Kolb, Felder-Silverman, and Honey & Mumford — to support [[student-engagement|learner engagement]] in large online classes. Following PRISMA 2020, they searched Scopus, IEEE Xplore and Google Scholar for studies published between 2015 and 2026 and included 41 that met their eligibility criteria. The review maps generative AI capabilities onto each model's preferences, synthesizes existing implementations, and proposes pathways for research and practice. Its central argument is that the technology's value lies not in matching a fixed style but in [[multimodal|multimodal]] flexibility: offering the same content in several formats at once lets students select what works for their context, avoiding the rigidity of earlier adaptive systems. The authors also caution against treating styles as fixed traits, noting the longstanding criticism that learning style theories lack robust empirical evidence.

*PRISMA 2020 [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|systematic review]] of 41 studies published between 2015 and 2026, spanning four learning style models and large online classes.*

## Key Findings

- **Multimodal flexibility, not style matching.** The review argues that generative AI's strength is offering the same content in multiple formats at once, so students select what works rather than being forced into a single mode.
- **VARK and Felder-Silverman dominate.** Most included implementations use those two models; Kolb appears mainly through [[experiential-learning|experiential learning]], and Honey & Mumford rarely.
- **The contribution is a mapping.** The review aligns each dimension of the four models with generative AI tools and stated learning benefits, building on implementations that often stopped at static matching.
- **Personalization extends beyond style.** The authors argue that generative AI also addresses engagement, emotional state, isolation, feedback, knowledge level and student-teacher interaction.
- **The neuromyth critique is acknowledged.** The review notes that learning styles lack robust empirical evidence and cautions against over-reliance on styles as fixed traits.

## Four models, one flexible technology

The review compares four [[theories-and-frameworks|learning style frameworks]]. VARK organizes sensory preferences — visual, auditory, read/write and kinesthetic — and is criticized for relying on [[self-report-measures|self-reports]]. Felder-Silverman sorts [[learners]] along active/reflective, sensing/intuitive, visual/verbal and sequential/global dimensions, and assumes preferences lead directly to behaviors. Kolb treats learning as an experience cycle of concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization and active experimentation, but oversimplifies the process and lacks social context. Honey & Mumford derives activist, reflector, theorist and pragmatist preferences from Kolb, yet shows low validity and reliability. Against this, the authors position generative AI as a way to offer the same content in several formats at once, so that [[personalized-learning|personalization]] need not mean locking a learner into one mode.

## What the 41 studies actually built

The search identified 110 records; after removing 18 inaccessible items and 5 duplicates, 87 remained, and 16 more were excluded for language, leaving 71 reports. Of these, 30 were excluded — 13 for no use of [[intelligent-tutoring|virtual assistants]] and 17 for no learning style model integration — and 41 studies were included. The included work clusters around Felder-Silverman and VARK, with Kolb appearing mainly through experiential learning and Honey & Mumford rarely used. Earlier systems typically matched learners to predefined content with clustering, classification or fuzzy logic; generative AI, the authors argue, allows real-time adaptation, multimodal output and conversational interaction that overcome that rigidity. The review maps each model's dimensions to tools — for example, text-to-speech narration for auditory preferences, concept maps for global processors, and journaling prompts for reflectors.

## Beyond style: engagement, emotion and feedback

The authors argue that style-based personalization addresses only part of the challenge. In large online classes, generative AI can also monitor sentiment and adapt tone, simplify content complexity and provide reassuring [[feedback]] when it detects frustration; simulate [[group-work|group work]], peer mirroring and discussion prompts to reduce isolation; deliver immediate, personalized feedback aligned with a student's preferred mode of processing; and adjust task difficulty through diagnostic quizzes or [[knowledge-graph|knowledge graphs]] so that students are categorized by level as well as by preferred mode. For teachers, learning style data can flag disengaged students and suggest grouping learners with complementary styles. The review also notes that learning style theories face criticism for oversimplification, and that [[ethics|ethical considerations]] and sensitive data require attention as these systems are built.

## What this means for practice

- **[[teacher-role|Instructors]].** Offer the same material in more than one format — text, audio, diagram, interactive steps — and let students choose, rather than assigning a single style-based path.
- **Instructional designers.** Use [[learning-theories|learning style models]] as a first layer for variety, then add engagement, emotional state, feedback and [[prior-knowledge|prior knowledge]] to the personalization design.
- **Educational technology developers.** Build real-time adaptation and multimodal output rather than static matching, and log which formats learners actually select.
- **Researchers.** Treat style adaptation as a hypothesis to test, not a settled design rule, given the criticism that learning styles lack robust empirical evidence.

## Limitations

- The review covers 41 studies found in Scopus, IEEE Xplore and Google Scholar between 2015 and 2026, so relevant work outside those databases or window is missed.
- Learning style models themselves lack robust empirical evidence, and the review cautions against treating them as fixed traits.
- The mappings are assembled from existing implementations and conceptual work, not from measured learning gains.
