> **Synthesis:** Jiang and Abdullah (2026) systematically reviewed 27 peer-reviewed studies of AI in university governance, searched in Web of Science and Scopus for publications from 2010 to 2025 and synthesized through descriptive mapping and thematic analysis following PRISMA. AI use clusters in strategic, administrative and risk-related governance rather than peripheral automation, and in 23 of the 27 studies it sits inside an integrated [[governance|decision-support system]] rather than standing alone. Operational efficiency and predictive accuracy are the outcomes most often reported; transparency, accountability, trust, equity and workload redistribution are comparatively underexamined. The review identifies three mechanisms — anticipatory modeling, data-driven coordination and accountability-oriented sense-making — that reshape the informational conditions of decisions without replacing human agency. It treats organizational learning, professional learning communities and university competitiveness as conceptual extensions requiring empirical study, not as demonstrated effects.

## Key Findings
1. **AI adoption concentrates in steering functions.** Governance uses cluster in strategic, administrative and risk, compliance and accountability domains, positioning AI as an instrument of institutional coordination rather than an operational automation tool.
2. **AI works mostly as infrastructure inside decision support.** In 23 of the 27 studies, AI functionality is embedded in decision-support systems, and predictive analytics is the dominant analytical engine; generative and language-based applications appear more selectively in text-intensive governance work.
3. **Efficiency and prediction are the measured wins.** Approximately two-thirds of the studies report operational efficiency, and roughly half to two-thirds emphasize predictive accuracy, with governance quality often inferred from model validation statistics rather than downstream policy effects.
4. **Normative and structural outcomes are thin.** About one-third discuss transparency, accountability or legitimacy, direct empirical assessment of equity or trust appears in only a small minority, and approximately one-quarter describe workload redistribution rather than workload elimination.
5. **Three mechanisms explain the governance change.** Anticipatory modeling makes decisions forward-looking, infrastructural coordination standardizes indicators across units, and accountability-oriented sense-making improves traceability and documentation.
6. **The learning and competitiveness claims remain inferential.** Organizational learning is rarely an explicit object of analysis, governance and pedagogy are treated as separate domains, and no direct causal evidence links AI-enabled governance to rankings, reputation or teaching quality.

## Scope, selection and quality of the review
The review followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and combined descriptive mapping with thematic synthesis. Eligible studies were peer reviewed, focused on higher education, involved AI technologies and addressed institutional-level governance functions such as strategic planning, resource allocation, quality assurance and institutional analytics; studies focused only on teaching, on non-AI technology or outside higher education were excluded. The search covered 2010 to 2025 in Web of Science and Scopus because the topic crosses education, management, computer science and policy.

The database search identified 1,365 records: 1,003 from Web of Science and 362 from Scopus. After 145 duplicates were removed, 1,220 records were screened by title and abstract and 1,067 were excluded. Of 153 reports sought for full-text retrieval, 152 were obtained and assessed; 125 were excluded after full-text evaluation, leaving 27 studies in the final synthesis. Although the search window began in 2010, no pre-2019 study met all inclusion criteria.

Quality appraisal used the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool for empirical studies and the Joanna Briggs Institute tool for conceptual and text-and-opinion papers, with no numerical scores calculated. Of the 27 studies, 12 were rated high quality, 14 moderate and one low. Screening, extraction and thematic coding all used a single-coder procedure, with iterative checking and conservative inclusion but no dual independent screening or inter-rater statistic. The protocol was not prospectively registered, though materials were retrospectively archived in OSF.

## Where AI lands and what gets measured
Six governance domains emerge from the coding: strategic governance (20 studies), administrative governance (15), risk, compliance and accountability (15), resource and financial governance (14), enrollment and student governance (9), and academic governance (7). AI types are distributed unevenly: AI-integrated decision support systems appear in 23 studies, predictive analytics in 19, hybrid architectures in 11, generative AI in 7, natural language processing in 5, institutional-level learning analytics in 4 and unsupervised learning in 2. Predictive analytics aligns strongly with strategic, administrative, risk and resource governance, while generative and language-based systems appear where documentation, reporting and interpretation dominate.

Outcome clusters are similarly imbalanced. Operational efficiency is the most consistently measured result, reported in approximately two-thirds of studies through processing time, throughput and workflow measures. Epistemic outcomes appear in roughly half to two-thirds, framed as forecasting precision, risk detection and alignment between projected and realized outcomes, usually through model validation statistics rather than longitudinal observation. Normative outcomes receive less attention: approximately one-third discuss transparency, accountability or institutional legitimacy, often through stakeholder perceptions rather than measurable governance indicators, and direct empirical assessment of equity or trust is rare. Structural outcomes appear in about one-quarter of studies, where routine data processing is delegated to algorithms while humans retain interpretive, ethical and strategic responsibility.

The three mechanisms are anticipatory modeling, infrastructural coordination and accountability-oriented sense-making. The review argues that these reshape feedback, information flows and decision routines, and that their value depends on mature data infrastructure, leadership commitment, AI literacy among governance actors and cross-unit collaboration. It also flags a governance–pedagogy interface: when governance privileges efficiency and measurable indicators, it may narrow pedagogical purposes to what can be optimized.

## What this means for practice
- **Administrators.** Treat AI as part of governance infrastructure and invest in data governance, institutional analytics capacity and leadership capability so that model outputs can be interpreted rather than merely deployed.
- **Administrators.** Establish frameworks covering transparency, accountability, data stewardship, audit and ethical oversight, keeping educational judgment and professional agency above efficiency or prediction alone.
- **Instructors.** Question governance metrics that define teaching quality only by what is easily measured, and press for evaluation that includes equity, reflection and educational value.
- **Policymakers.** Move beyond compliance-oriented guidance toward institutionalized governance that clarifies accountability, data stewardship and monitoring of AI-supported decisions.
- **Researchers.** Use longitudinal and multi-institutional designs, examine how governance decisions shape pedagogy, and test the proposed links to organizational learning and competitiveness instead of assuming them.

## Limitations
- Screening, data extraction and thematic coding were performed by a single coder with no full dual independent screening and no formal inter-rater reliability statistic.
- The protocol was not prospectively registered; it was only retrospectively archived, limiting transparency about post hoc methodological decisions.
- The search was limited to Web of Science and Scopus and to English-language peer-reviewed journal publications, with the exclusion of sources such as ERIC noted as a possible selection bias.
- The underlying evidence is concentrated in cross-sectional and single-institution case studies evaluated through technical performance indicators, limiting generalization and causal inference.
- Normative outcomes, equity, trust and long-term institutional transformation receive far less empirical attention than efficiency and prediction.
- Links to professional learning communities and university competitiveness are explicitly framed as conceptual extensions; the review reports no direct causal evidence for them.
