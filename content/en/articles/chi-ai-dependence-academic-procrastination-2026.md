> **Synthesis:** Medical undergraduates are heavy users of [[generative-ai|generative AI]], and the field has tended to assume that dependence and academic procrastination rise together. Chi, Zhang and Jia tested that assumption at the level of the individual student rather than the variable. In a cross-sectional survey of 530 undergraduates at two Chinese medical [[higher-ed|universities]], they ran a [[latent-profile-analysis|latent profile analysis]] on four AI-dependence dimensions and three procrastination dimensions, then compared the resulting groups on background characteristics and on positive acceptance and striving. Three profiles fit best. A quarter of the sample (25.8%) combined high dependence with high procrastination and reported the lowest adaptive resources; a third (33.0%) showed elevated functional and cognitive reliance on AI while procrastinating no more than the low-dependence group. Heavier and more familiar use belonged to the functional-reliance group, not the co-occurring one, which is the paper's practical point: the pattern of reliance, not its amount, carries risk.

## Key Findings

- **Three profiles fit best, not a continuum.** The retained solution put 41.1% in low dependence–low procrastination, 25.8% in high dependence–high procrastination, and 33.0% in functional and cognitive reliance with low procrastination.
- **A third used AI heavily without procrastinating.** Class 3's emotional dependence (3.10) and loss of control (2.88) sat near Class 1's (2.70 and 2.72), while its functional reliance (3.44) approached Class 2's (3.63).
- **The co-occurring group carried the resource deficit.** Class 2 averaged 30.50 on positive acceptance against 43.30 for Class 1 and 40.98 for Class 3; striving showed the same ordering (38.58 against 57.43 and 55.18).
- **Familiarity separated the two heavy-use groups.** Low rather than high AI familiarity predicted Class 2 membership over Class 3, and Class 3 held the largest share of high-familiarity students at 42.9%.
- **First-choice major tracked the functional profile.** Non-first-choice enrollment predicted Class 1 over Class 3 and Class 2 over Class 3; 77.7% of Class 3 was first-choice.
- **Frequency alone did not sort risk.** Among Class 2 students, 20.4% used AI seldom, the highest such share of any profile, while 54.0% used it always, so the frequency-risk relationship was not monotonic.

## Three subgroups, not a severity ladder

A variable-centered analysis returns one dependence-procrastination correlation for everyone. Latent profile analysis instead sorts students by the shape of their responses across seven indicators. The retained three-class solution had entropy of 0.947, below the two-class 0.984 and above the four- and five-class 0.889 and 0.884. The four-class model was set aside: its extra class (13.8% of the sample) was a downward shift of the low-dependence group, not a distinct pattern. Dropping the desire-to-change indicator reproduced three profiles with 99.2% agreement (526 of 530). Two cautions belong with the labels: "low" and "high" are relative, since every item mean fell between 2.70 and 3.77 on a 1–5 scale, and the profiles describe one survey, not a trajectory.

## What functional reliance looks like

The informative profile sits in the middle of the risk ordering. Class 3 students delegated task execution and information processing to AI at rates close to the high-dependence group — functional dependence 3.44 against 3.63, cognitive dependence 3.40 against 3.52 — while emotional attachment (3.10) and loss of control (2.88) stayed near the low-dependence group's 2.70 and 2.72. Their three procrastination means (3.08, 2.84, 3.16) were indistinguishable from Class 1's (3.07, 2.76, 3.11). The authors read this as instrumental versus substitutive [[cognitive-offloading|offloading]]: AI as a tool inside a working study routine rather than an escape from effortful engagement. A single dependence total cannot make that distinction, so the profile shape rather than the level is the interpretable quantity in [[medical-education|medical education]].

## Resources tracked avoidance, not reliance

Positive acceptance and striving separated the profiles along the procrastination dimension, not the dependence dimension. Class 2 averaged 30.50 on positive acceptance and 38.58 on striving, against 43.30 and 57.43 for Class 1 and 40.98 and 55.18 for Class 3, with every pairwise comparison at p < 0.001. The Class 1–Class 3 gap was conventional in size (d = 0.94 and 0.83); the Class 2 contrasts reached d = 4.26 to 7.75, inflated by unusually small within-class dispersion, which the authors flag. The ordering is the finding: heavy reliance with intact self-acceptance resembled low dependence far more than the co-occurring profile. Lower self-acceptance and striving traveled with avoidance, not with delegation, as a [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulated learning]] account would predict.

## Reading the correlates

Three background variables distinguished the profiles; sex, residence, year, income, only-child status and age showed no association. The correlates that mattered point away from a simple disaffection story. Class 3 membership was associated with first-choice major enrollment (OR = 0.471 for non-first-choice against Class 1) and with greater subjective familiarity with AI (OR = 0.447 for low against high). Frequency behaved oddly: "often" rather than "always" use predicted lower odds of both elevated-reliance classes, yet Class 2 held the largest share of seldom users at 20.4%. Knowing what a tool can and cannot do appears to matter more than using it constantly, which puts [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] rather than usage caps at the center and points at [[differential-effects-across-learner-groups|differences across learner groups]].

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Screen on pattern, not volume: 33.0% used AI heavily with procrastination means indistinguishable from low users, so a blanket restriction would catch the wrong students.
- **Administrators.** Pair dependence screening with a procrastination measure and a one-item familiarity check; familiarity separated the two heavy-use profiles (OR = 0.369) where dependence scores did not.
- **Instructors.** Target the co-occurring group first: Class 2 combined high dependence, high procrastination and the lowest positive acceptance (30.50) and striving (38.58).
- **Researchers.** Report dependence by dimension. The four AIDep-22 subscales behaved differently, and a summed score would fold Class 3's functional reliance into a risk index it does not fit.

## Limitations

- Cross-sectional single survey (March to May 2026): every association is co-occurrence, with no ordering among dependence, procrastination and adaptive resources.
- Cluster sampling of intact classes at two medical universities, with class and institution identifiers not retained, so design effects and cluster-robust standard errors could not be computed.
- Subscale reliability was modest: α ran 0.390–0.661 for AI dependence and 0.392–0.602 for procrastination, against 0.864 and 0.817 for the full scales.
- All measures were [[self-report-measures|self-reported]], with no behavioral record of AI use or task delay.
- Positive acceptance and striving were internally consistent to a degree (α = 0.959–0.981) the authors suggest reflects item redundancy, and separated Class 2 too sharply to serve as a predictor.
