> **Synthesis:** Estaphan and Zulfiqar (2026) interviewed the three educators who taught a second-year health and medicine course through a switch from a traditional section-based rubric to an AI-aware analytic rubric. Thematic analysis produced five themes: perceived changes in student learning and [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]], refinement of assessment expectations, expansion of [[pedagogy|teaching strategies]], implementation challenges around authenticity, and institutional readiness. Educators reported that making ethical and reflective AI use assessable increased their confidence in grading, prompted more explicit teaching of [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] and encouraged students to justify and critique generated output. They also described heavier marking, uncertainty about concealed AI use and little formal preparation. The authors present an interpretive, mechanism-based account of educator experience, not evidence of improved student outcomes.

## Key Findings
1. **The rubric changed what educators assessed.** The revised analytic rubric added explicit criteria for process, participation and [[ethics|ethical AI]] use, while the old rubric was section-based and focused on interpreting a research paper.
2. **Educators perceived deeper [[student-engagement|student engagement]].** They described a move from passive reliance on generated answers toward justification, critique and verification, with more of the student's reasoning visible.
3. **Grading felt more defensible.** Clearer criteria for AI use and originality were associated with greater confidence and a stronger sense of consistency in marking.
4. **Teaching expanded beyond the assignment.** Educators introduced explicit discussion of AI, accuracy checking, formative checkpoints and more deliberate modeling of critical thinking.
5. **Authenticity remained unresolved.** Reflective components made some misuse visible, but educators said concealed AI use could not be detected with certainty, including AI-[[reflection|written reflections]].
6. **Implementation depended on institutional support.** Educators called for [[educational-development|professional development]], clearer [[higher-ed|university]] policies and shared resources; they reported little direct training.

## How the study was conducted
The setting was a second-year Health and Medicine Course in the three-year [[undergraduate-education|Bachelor]] of Health Science at the Australian National University. The six-credit course uses [[case-based-learning|case-based learning]] to teach disease mechanisms, diagnostic investigations and clinical reasoning, with cohort sizes typically ranging from 60 to 80 students. Its capstone assignment, Active Reading of a Research Paper, paired each student with a research paper and an academic mentor, used structured questions and culminated in an independently submitted 2-page critical summary.

The study used semi-structured interviews with three educators who had taught with both rubrics. They were the complete teaching team for the course, so the perspectives covered every educator who implemented the change. Each took part in a single video-conference interview lasting approximately 20 minutes; interviews were recorded with consent and transcribed. Analysis followed a six-phase thematic analysis framework: transcripts were read in full, codes were generated inductively, candidate themes were refined iteratively, and final themes were then deductively mapped against the Twelve Tips for Designing AI-Resilient Assessments framework. To reduce social desirability bias, interviews and analysis were conducted by a researcher who did not know the participants and had no role in designing the rubric.

The rubric comparison is concrete. The old version comprised Significance (20%), Methodology (40%) and Methods (40%), with performance levels from Excellent to Unsatisfactory. The AI-aware version added Understanding of the Paper, Clarity of Methods Explanation, Depth of Critique, Integration of External Sources, Writing Mechanics, Evidence of Process and Engagement, In-class Contributions and Ethical Use of AI, assessed across performance levels from Excellent to Poor. Process, participation and ethical AI use were previously absent.

## What the educators reported
Five themes structure the findings. Educators perceived a shift in student behavior from passive use of generated answers toward critique and justification, with students arriving with a perspective rather than saying they did not understand the material. They described the revised [[rubrics|rubric]] as giving clearer, more defensible criteria for grading, especially around [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]], and felt better able to distinguish superficial AI use from authentic student reasoning. They also expanded their teaching into ethical use, accuracy checking and explicit discussion of AI's capabilities and limits, extending these changes into formative checkpoints and feedback on reflective writing.

The costs were practical. Reflective submissions increased marking workload, student familiarity with AI varied, and staff needed time and resources to apply the rubric consistently. Concerns about [[authentic-assessment|assessment authenticity]] persisted: one educator noted that the AI-reflection section could itself be written with AI. Educators also described limited preparation, with training largely informal and university documents only vaguely recalled.

The authors interpret the rubric as a pedagogical intervention operating through three mutually reinforcing pathways: student learning behavior, educators' assessment practices and teaching strategies. Implementation challenges and institutional readiness moderate these pathways. They explicitly call the model interpretive and reject direct causal claims about learning outcomes. The study also refines the Twelve Tips framework, treating it as a flexible design heuristic rather than a prescriptive checklist: [[process-oriented-assessment|process-based assessment]] and [[metacognition|metacognitive]] engagement were readily operationalized, while workload and variable AI literacy called for stronger institutional [[scaffolding]].

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Make AI use an explicit, assessable criterion with transparent expectations, so students know which forms of engagement are acceptable and must justify their choices.
- **Instructors.** Add process evidence such as drafts, reflection and engagement with feedback to the final written product, and give feedback on the reasoning it shows.
- **Assessment designers.** Budget for heavier marking when reflective components are introduced, and calibrate criteria across the teaching team before implementation.
- **Faculty developers.** Provide direct training in ethical AI use and shared resources instead of relying on the rubric's author to brief colleagues informally.
- **Researchers.** Collect direct student data, such as reflective writing samples or assessment outcomes, and use prospective designs to test whether the reported changes appear outside educator perceptions.

## Limitations
- Three educators from a single course at one institution participated; although they were the complete teaching team, transferability to other disciplines and institutions is limited.
- The study relied exclusively on educator [[self-report-measures|self-report]]. No student outcome data were collected, so claims about student learning and engagement reflect perceptions rather than independently verified change.
- Social desirability bias was possible because the rubric was developed by an author and participants knew at least one author, though a researcher without a stake conducted interviews and analysis.
- There was no control group, pre-intervention baseline or [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal]] follow-up, and retrospective comparison across rubric versions introduces recall bias; causal inference is not warranted.
- Concealed AI use remained undetectable with certainty, including in the reflective section itself, so assessment authenticity was only partly addressed.
