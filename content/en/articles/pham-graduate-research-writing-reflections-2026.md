> **Synthesis:** Pham and Nguyen interviewed nine Vietnamese MA students taking a Research Writing course to learn how graduate writers critically reflect on AI tools, perceive over-reliance and academic-integrity risks, and imagine AI's place in future instruction. Thematic analysis of the interviews yielded six themes. Participants described selective use of ChatGPT, Grammarly, QuillBot and other tools for brainstorming, vocabulary, outlining and source discovery, together with habits of checking AI output against dictionaries, Google Scholar and other chatbots. They valued instructor corrections and peer discussion as the prompts that made AI's limits visible, and envisioned a hybrid model in which AI supplies quick foundational information while teachers provide depth and contextual judgment. Because the nine volunteers came from one course, were interviewed by their own instructor and supplied only self-report accounts, the findings are exploratory.

## Key Findings
1. **The sample was nine volunteers.** Nine of the 18 students enrolled in the course agreed to semi-structured interviews at the end of the semester.
2. **Students described strategic, selective use.** They reported asking AI for vocabulary and outlines, then adapting suggestions to their own ideas rather than copying full drafts.
3. **Verification was a recurring habit.** Participants described checking AI output against dictionaries, Google Scholar, textbooks, official materials and other chatbots.
4. **Over-reliance was a stated concern.** Students linked heavy dependence to weaker independent thinking, fragmented or mechanical prose and risks to academic integrity.
5. **Instructor feedback acted as an epistemic corrective.** Explicit criticism of incomplete or contextually inappropriate AI output was described as prompting later skepticism and verification.
6. **Peer discussion filtered AI output.** In group work, students reported filtering main ideas themselves before consulting AI and using it to prompt debate rather than to supply answers.
7. **The preferred model was hybrid.** Participants consistently placed AI at the stage of fast information access and retained teachers for specificity, credibility and interpretation.

## Course and interview design

The study took place at a university in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, with a student population of approximately 40,000. Its Graduate Program in English Language Studies was established in 2022 and had enrolled 61 students. The Research Writing course was a core requirement; 18 graduate students were enrolled in the semester of data collection, with ages ranging from 24 to 32, five males and 13 females, and English proficiency at C1–C2 on the CEFR scale. Because the researcher also taught the course, recruitment used convenience sampling from the single class, precluding between-group comparisons.

Instruction combined class discussion, case studies, problem-solving tasks, student presentations, peer and teacher feedback, and weekly scaffolded assignments covering cause-effect, descriptive and argumentative essays, paraphrasing, summarizing, literature reviews, data commentary and abstracts, with APA-style citation throughout. ChatGPT, Grammarly and QuillBot were introduced as supplementary aids that students were told to cross-check against scholarly sources. The course ended with a literature-based paper or small-scale empirical project. In lessons, the instructor selected four to five student projects for real-time whole-class comments on structure, clarity, coherence, referencing and source engagement; Turnitin was used occasionally.

Nine students volunteered for individual interviews lasting 22–36 minutes. Audio was transcribed with a web tool, and the teacher and a colleague separately reviewed recordings and transcripts for consistency. The analysis followed Braun and Clarke's six-phase procedure with inductive and deductive coding, producing six themes across the three research questions. Participants were assigned codes such as Student-1 through Student-9. By the end of the course, all nine reported using multiple AI tools, and the authors present the resulting themes as context-based insights rather than measured effects.

## What the interviews reported

Under the first research question, students described using AI to surface vocabulary and structure while keeping control of the final text. One participant asked ChatGPT for words and chose only those that fit; another checked suggested vocabulary in Cambridge or Oxford dictionaries. Some drafted independently first and then compared their work with an AI version, while others used Google Translate to clarify a suggestion before rewriting it in their own phrasing. These accounts were read through self-regulated learning theory as deliberate monitoring of a resource rather than outsourcing of writing.

Verification recurred across the second theme. Participants checked ChatGPT responses in Google Scholar, dictionaries, textbooks, official materials and other chatbots, and some cross-checked three chatbots (ChatGPT, Gemini and Copilot) before speaking in class. One student limited AI use to one or two phrases from a conclusion; another described an essay written by ChatGPT as fragmented and illogical. Students spoke of plagiarism, mechanical reproduction and loss of authenticity, and said they rewrote ideas in their own words to preserve [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]].

For the third research question, students attributed their critical orientation to teacher comments and group discussion rather than to AI itself. They described a division of labor: AI offers a fast "basic view", while teachers confirm information, supply depth and explain what is contextually appropriate. Some said AI-supported preparation reduced discussion anxiety and made them more willing to speak. The authors read the six themes together as a sequential account in which instructor-guided critical pedagogy activates self-regulatory strategies that consolidate into [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]], while cautioning that interview data cannot establish that sequence causally.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Point out specific instances where AI output is incomplete, off-target or unsupported, and ask students to revise in response rather than simply replacing the text.
- **Instructors.** Require students to document where an AI suggestion came from and how it was checked, verified or rejected before it entered a draft.
- **Institutions.** Embed AI-literacy components in writing-intensive graduate courses, linked to source verification, integrity policy and genre conventions rather than offered as a standalone add-on.
- **Researchers.** Combine interviews with analysis of actual writing artifacts, think-aloud protocols or observation to test whether reported verification practices are enacted.

## Limitations
- The sample of nine self-selected volunteers from one course at one university is too small and context-specific for generalization.
- Convenience sampling excludes students who declined to participate, whose views may have differed systematically.
- The researcher's dual role as instructor and interviewer creates a structural risk of social-desirability bias; interviews were scheduled after grades were submitted, but residual bias cannot be ruled out.
- The design captures one course in one semester, so it cannot show whether critical orientations persist across a full graduate program.
- Evidence is entirely self-reported interview data with no triangulating artifacts, observations or independent coding; formal member checking, intercoder reliability and a dedicated reflexive journal were not used.
