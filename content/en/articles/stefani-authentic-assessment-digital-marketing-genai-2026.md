> **Synthesis:** Stefani and Vamvatsikou traced three cycles of an [[authentic-assessment]] redesign in an undergraduate digital marketing course, analyzing three assignment briefs and 65 student submissions from 2024, 2025, and 2026. The first brief demanded a real mini-campaign but yielded no defensible performance evidence because students lacked audiences, time, and budgets. The second replaced execution with strategic simulation and predictive indicators, which made planning visible but produced unanchored targets and opaque AI-assisted production. The third added external evidence, benchmark expectations, a weighted [[rubrics|rubric]], and mandatory AI-use disclosure. All 17 readable 2026 artifacts acknowledged AI, but only three gave comprehensive accounts, and verification, risk awareness, and human accountability remained largely invisible. The authors propose that authenticity can rest on evidence-based professional judgment rather than literal execution, and that [[ai-use-disclosure]] must be distinguished from [[ethics|Responsible AI]] competence.

## Key Findings
1. **Execution authenticity failed structurally.** Students produced websites and [[learning-analytics|analytics]] setups, but none of the first-cycle submissions established a defensible chain from exposure to a justified optimization decision.
2. **Simulation made strategic reasoning visible.** Removing implementation shifted work toward designing a product and defending predicted indicators, at the cost of claims that required supporting evidence.
3. **Mandated themes shaped student output.** Sustainability moved from an optional attribute to a dominant theme and then broadened to social, financial, and health resilience.
4. **[[scaffolding|Scaffolds]] became answers.** A suggested persona template and worked indicator examples reappeared in submissions, sometimes without adaptation.
5. **Disclosure became universal but uneven.** Every readable 2026 submission acknowledged AI use, while only a few identified tools, purposes, and affected artifacts in detail.
6. **Evidence lagged behind benchmark language.** Market-gap sources became common, but source-linked benchmark chains remained rare.
7. **Visible AI use is not responsible use.** The rubric made AI participation discussable without assessing verification, retained human authority, or proportionate risk analysis.

## What the design cycles compared

The study followed [[design-based-research|design-based research]] logic: each annual brief was treated as a design conjecture, student submissions as evidence of how it worked, and the next brief as a revision informed by observed problems. The course was a third-year undergraduate class at the University of Patras. Submissions were optional individual projects adding up to three bonus points in 2024 and up to four in 2025 and 2026. Annual cohorts numbered 110, 120, and 115 students; analyzed submissions numbered 19, 29, and 17, for participation rates of 17.3%, 24.2%, and 14.8%.

The researchers coded briefs with an eleven-dimensional indicator framework and artifacts with nineteen codes, including product type, analytics authenticity, AI-use indicators, scaffold anchoring, market-gap evidence, innovation substantiation, benchmark traceability, declaration granularity, and AI cognitive involvement. A second researcher independently coded 30 submissions, ten per cycle, yielding 570 paired decisions. Overall agreement was 72.3%, and agreement was high for AI-use acknowledgement (100%, κ = 1.00), declaration granularity (weighted κ = 0.97), and AI embeddedness (weighted κ = 0.96). Agreement was low for deliverable integrity (30%, weighted κ = −0.02) and channel rationale (43%, weighted κ = 0.09), so findings depending on those codes are presented as indicative.

Across the briefs, execution fidelity fell from a real site with analytics to simulation, cognitive demand rose from Apply/Analyze to Create/Evaluate, EntreComp competencies increased from one to six, and sustainability integration moved from absent to core and evidenced. Weighted rubric criteria went from none to eight, external source requirements from none to at least two, and AI-use declaration from not required to required.

## What the submissions showed

In 2024, 14 of 19 submissions supplied a website or preview link and 13 of 19 mentioned an analytics tool, but none provided defensible real performance evidence. Students coped in five mutually exclusive ways: three reported non-success candidly, one substituted hypothetical results, three asserted positive performance without adequate evidence, four reported setup without analysis, and eight omitted evaluation or described only future measurement. Sustainability appeared in six submissions and was central in two.

In 2025, 28 of 29 fully codable submissions offered a student-designed fictional digital product, and sustainability was central or significant in 26 of 29; three green clusters accounted for 79.3% of ideas. Numerical indicators appeared in 28 of 29 submissions, but targets were commonly called realistic without historical data or external [[benchmark|benchmarks]]. No submission contained a structured AI-use declaration, and AI was an explicit product feature in six of 29. The two worked indicator examples supplied in the brief recurred in student work, an instance of scaffold anchoring that the authors treat as indicative because inter-coder agreement on that code was low.

In 2026, all 17 readable submissions acknowledged AI use, three gave comprehensive tool–purpose–artifact accounts, 13 cited at least two identifiable external sources, and only one provided source-linked benchmarks across its indicator set with an explicit derivation. Numerical keyword data appeared in 15 of 17, while source transparency varied sharply. AI was embedded in eight of 17 value propositions, and environmental markers appeared in six project names while two names included AI. The eight-criterion weighted rubric allocated points across product idea and market-gap evidence (0.5), persona (0.4), brand identity (0.4), social-media post and copy (0.4), [[storytelling-in-education|storytelling]] video (0.7), SEO strategy and mock-up (0.8), indicators and conclusions (0.6), and coherence and presentation (0.2), totaling 4.0 points.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** When real audiences and budgets are unavailable, use an explicit strategic simulation rather than a token website with near-zero analytics.
- **Instructors.** Grade justification chains that link indicators to mechanisms, benchmarks, and defensible evidence instead of rewarding polished artifacts alone.
- **Assessment designers.** Embed AI documentation in the assessed artifact and score declaration granularity separately from AI cognitive involvement.
- **Assessment designers.** Scaffold how to derive and verify a target rather than supplying completed example targets that students may reproduce.
- **Administrators.** Treat verification evidence, retained human authority, and proportionate risk analysis as separate learning outcomes if Responsible AI is a stated goal.

## Limitations
- The study concerns one course at one institution, and the 65 artifacts are bounded corpora rather than a probability sample.
- Participation was voluntary and self-selected, so the submissions likely over-represent engaged students.
- The course instructor designed the briefs, administered the incentive, and coded the artifacts; a second coder checked only 30 submissions.
- Several higher-inference codes had low inter-coder agreement, especially deliverable integrity and channel rationale.
- Cohort differences prevent attributing changes across years to the redesign alone.
- The study analyzes artifacts, not [[learning-gains|learning gains]], and source-level fact-checking of cited market statistics remains incomplete.
