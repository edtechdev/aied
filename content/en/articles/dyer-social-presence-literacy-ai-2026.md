> **Synthesis:** Dyer, Larson and Aroz ask what is lost when [[generative-ai|generative AI]] is folded into asynchronous [[online-teaching-and-learning|online teaching]] for efficiency's sake. Their conceptual, practice-informed synthesis identifies three relational risks that compound one another: task automation displacing meaningful engagement, diminished [[empathy|empathy]] and emotional attunement, and a broader drift from human-centered teaching toward speed and convenience. Against these they propose [[community-of-inquiry|Social Presence Literacy]] — an educator's capacity to recognize, intentionally foster and evaluate the relational dimensions of teaching in AI-mediated environments — and a five-step decision-making framework that turns it into evaluate, identify, adjust, test, refine. The paper is explicit that the extension is conceptual and has not been empirically validated.

## Key Findings

- **Automation can hollow out presence.** AI-generated discussion responses, feedback and content may look complete while addressing no specific student idea and inviting no further dialogue. The authors' concern is not efficiency but the shift from relational to transactional exchange, and they cite evidence linking instructor immediacy and meaningful interaction to engagement and satisfaction.
- **Emotional attunement is the first casualty.** When AI-mediated practices substitute for human interaction, opportunities to read and respond to a student's emotional and contextual state disappear — a loss the authors treat as distinct from lost participation counts.
- **The three risks reinforce each other.** Task automation narrows relational contact, which weakens the instructor's ability to perceive student need, which makes further automation more likely. The authors present this as a cycle of disconnection rather than three separate problems.
- **Social presence has five measurable dimensions.** Drawing on Whiteside's Social Presence Model, they work with affective association, community cohesion, instructor involvement, interaction intensity, and knowledge and experience — the criteria against which an AI-supported practice can be judged.
- **The framework is a loop, not a ban.** The five steps — evaluate current practice, identify relational gaps, make adjustments, test outcomes, refine — are familiar instructional-design moves; the contribution is applying an established social-presence framework as the criteria for deciding when AI should automate, augment, or leave the human in place.
- **The claim is deliberately modest.** The authors state that the extension remains conceptual, untested for practical applicability, and that its effects on perceived instructor presence, relational engagement and student experience require study across varied contexts.

## The three risks, and why they compound

The synthesis draws on Social Presence Theory, the Community of Inquiry framework, the Social Presence Model, social development theory and human-centered pedagogy. Its first risk is substitution: an instructor uses AI to acknowledge contributions without engaging them, and interaction becomes performative. The second is empathy: teaching is inherently relational, and a tool cannot read the circumstances behind a student's message. The third is orientation: as routine tasks get faster, decisions begin to follow convenience rather than pedagogical intention, and AI-generated output gets treated as a finished product instead of a starting point. The authors' point is that these are connected — each makes the next more likely — which is why they frame the remedy as a literacy rather than a rule.

## What social presence literacy asks of a teacher

Social Presence Literacy is defined as the capacity to recognize, intentionally foster and evaluate the relational dimensions of teaching within AI-mediated environments. The five-step framework operationalizes it: evaluate existing practice, identify where relational contact is thinning, adjust the division of labor between tool and human, test whether the change restored it, and refine. The five dimensions of the Social Presence Model supply the criteria at each step, so the question is never simply "is this faster?" but "does this preserve affective association, community cohesion, instructor involvement, interaction intensity and shared knowledge?" The authors position instructors as designers of relational experience rather than adopters or rejecters of the technology.

## What this means for practice

- **Audit AI use against relationship, not speed.** For each automated task, ask which of the five social-presence dimensions it serves or erodes.
- **Keep the human in the loop for meaning.** Reserve direct human involvement for responses that require judgment about a specific student's ideas, circumstances or emotional state.
- **Treat generated output as a starting point.** Feedback or discussion replies drafted by AI should be adapted to the student before they are sent, not issued as finished products.
- **Watch for the cycle.** A drop in relational contact reduces the information needed to notice the next drop; the framework's test-and-refine step exists to interrupt that.
- **Match the tool to the dimension.** Automation is defensible where it supports rather than replaces presence — handling routine logistics so instructor time goes to dialogue.

## Limitations

- The paper is a conceptual, practice-informed synthesis: it reports no empirical data, no implementation and no measured effect on presence, engagement or student experience.
- The five-step framework is explicitly unvalidated, and its practical applicability across instructional contexts is identified as future work.
- The analysis centers on asynchronous online higher education, so its transfer to synchronous, hybrid or school settings is asserted rather than shown.
- The relational challenges are derived from prior literature and the authors' practice experience rather than from a systematic review with stated inclusion criteria.
