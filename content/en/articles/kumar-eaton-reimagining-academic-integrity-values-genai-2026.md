> **Synthesis:** Kumar and Eaton (2026) argue that [[generative-ai|generative AI]] calls for a values-level reimagining of [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]] rather than a technical or disciplinary fix. Holding the International Center for Academic Integrity's six fundamental values steady — honesty, trust, fairness, respect, responsibility, and courage — they show that each value can be either preserved or contravened by the same tool, depending on how it is used and what conditions surround it. Disclosure of AI use, known authorship, [[equity-in-ai-education|equitable]] access paired with training, and clearly communicated expectations are the conditions under which the technology aligns with integrity; concealment, uneven access, absent guidance, and fear-driven avoidance are the conditions under which it undermines integrity. The wider claim is that hybrid human–AI authorship introduces complexities that detection-first, punitive models cannot resolve, and that postplagiarism names this transitional era without dissolving traditional plagiarism or its sanctions. The article closes with coordinated recommendations for [[educational-policy-ai|institutional policy]], faculty [[educational-development|professional development]], [[student-support-and-success|student support]], and further research.

*Conceptual position paper: the authors report no study and no data of their own, working from a literature analysis combined with analytic deduction, and figures attributed to other work are presented as those studies' claims rather than as findings of this article.*

## Key Findings

- **Generative AI is not inherently good or bad for integrity:** each of the six fundamental values can be preserved or contravened by the same tool, so the decisive question is not whether students use it but under what conditions they use it.
- **Disclosure is the honesty condition:** transparent disclosure of which tools were used, and how, keeps learning visible, whereas concealment obscures it, and partial disclosure is treated as a violation in its own right.
- **Trust spans four dimensions:** it runs between students and instructors, among peers, toward the credential-granting institution, and toward the intellectual content itself, and restrictions survive when they carry a clear [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] rationale.
- **Fairness requires access plus training, not access alone:** uniform availability of tools, computing infrastructure, and support is necessary but insufficient, because students also need structured help to use generative AI ethically and effectively.
- **Responsibility is distributed rather than individual:** students, faculty, administrators, platform providers, instructional designers, and technical staff each carry distinct obligations, and the authors frame integrity as collective action rather than blame assigned to one group.
- **Courage operates at every level:** students must sometimes refuse permitted use, faculty must make contested judgments about when tools help or hinder learning, and administrators must enforce policy consistently while resourcing that work.
- **Postplagiarism reframes the response:** hybrid human–AI authorship makes plagiarism frameworks insufficient without abolishing misconduct, shifting emphasis from detection and punishment toward ethical engagement and pedagogically grounded policy.

## Postplagiarism after the information age

Kumar and Eaton open from the observation that generative AI has arguably displaced the information age. These systems create novel content — text, images, music, and other outputs — by analyzing vast datasets and identifying patterns, and although the extent of their permeation of [[higher-ed|higher education]] is unknown, usage is likely greater than reported and continues to rise across face-to-face, blended, and online delivery. The consequence for the authors is not a new category of misconduct so much as a stress test for the values underneath it.

The organizing idea is postplagiarism, an era in which human [[creativity]] and technological advancement converge, rendering traditional notions of plagiarism insufficient. Postplagiarism does not declare plagiarism obsolete or misconduct unsanctionable; it recognizes that increasingly collaborative [[human-ai-collaboration|human–AI interactions]] introduce new authorship complexities alongside established understandings of plagiarism, and that this pushes practice beyond exclusively detection-focused strategies toward ethical engagement. The authors situate this against the International Center for Academic Integrity, founded in 1992, which articulated five fundamental values in 1999 — fairness, honesty, respect, responsibility, and trust — added courage in the 2014 second edition, and published updated descriptions in 2021. They acknowledge critiques of the framework along the way, including calls to add compassion or dignity and scholarship arguing that a moralistic values framing perpetuates colonial norms and marginalizes Indigenous understandings of teaching and learning with integrity; their analysis keeps the six-value set while accepting its limits.

Two pieces of framing point the same way. The first is the argument that a crime-and-punishment approach is antithetical to pedagogy that prioritizes student learning over catching cheaters, a position the authors support with recent [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|systematic reviews]] emphasizing a culture of integrity that balances the benefits of generative AI with ethical standards. The second is criminologist Donald Cressey's fraud triangle — incentive, opportunity, and rationalization — later extended into a fraud diamond by adding capability, which the authors read as fortuitously aligned with what these tools enable students to do. Both frames locate the problem in structure rather than in individual bad actors, which is why the authors treat categorical bans as a failing proposition.

## Six values, two directions

The paper's central device is a two-column analysis: for each value, the authors set out how generative AI may preserve it and how it may contravene it. Read across the six values, one pattern recurs — the same use can be integrity-preserving or integrity-violating depending on transparency, access, guidance, and ownership. That pattern is why the article refuses the question of whether the tools are good or bad.

Honesty turns on disclosure. Learners can use generative AI to understand ideas, connect concepts, and communicate more clearly, but the same tools serve deception when the intent is to fake learning. One practical response the authors cite is a set of pictographs developed by Peters (2023) for disclosing AI involvement: NAI for no artificial intelligence, AIG for AI-generated content, and AIA for AI-aided collaborative work. Yet honesty is not only a binary declaration. The authors illustrate the point with a hypothetical student who used generative AI for most of a paper but claimed only a small share; that partial truth violates honesty. At the same time, they caution that using percentages or scores to decide whether plagiarism occurred has been problematized for over two decades, and that deeply iterative human–AI drafting can make a precise accounting unrealistic. Their preferred move is conversational: ask students which tools they used, how they used them, and what they learned. This is the territory [[ai-use-disclosure|AI use disclosure]] occupies, and it shows why disclosure is a pedagogical practice rather than a compliance checkbox.

Trust is the second value, and the authors give it four dimensions. It runs between students and instructors, among students, toward the institution that grants the credential, and toward the intellectual content and pedagogical process itself. Undisclosed use erodes the first; suspected peer advantage corrodes the second; credentials obtained without requisite knowledge weaken the third; and delegating cognitive work to a machine makes the fourth superficial. [[hallucination-risk|Hallucinations]] — inaccurate information, fabricated citations, and contextually inappropriate content — compound the problem, since unverified output compromises both the submission and the student's learning. Restrictions need not damage trust, the authors argue, provided they come with clear rationales and pedagogical justification; students who understand the learning objectives behind a technology policy tend to respect its boundaries.

Fairness is described as the value learners recognize most intuitively, and it depends on consistent criteria, consistently applied policies, and clear procedures for reporting violations. The access dimension is where generative AI raises the sharpest questions: fairness requires that all students have the tools, infrastructure, connectivity, accommodations, and training they need, and the authors note that access alone is insufficient without the ability to use it well. They point to ETH Zurich's 2024 guidelines, grounded in responsibility, transparency, and fairness, as one operational example, and they concede that whether truly equitable outcomes are achievable remains an open question given structural and enforcement constraints. Respect is analyzed across self, others, and knowledge — self-respect compelling honest practice, respect for others requiring credit where it is due, and respect for knowledge requiring verification of generated sources and candor about copyrighted training data. Responsibility is explicitly distributed across students, faculty, administrators, and additional stakeholders such as platform providers and technical staff, and courage is its counterpart: faculty must judge when tools enable or diminish learning, students must resist prohibited use and sometimes decline permitted use, and administrators must enforce policy fairly while funding the conditions that make ethical action possible.

## Conditions, culture, and shared responsibility

Across the analysis, the authors treat prevention, detection, and pedagogy as a pipeline in which generative AI disrupts several checkpoints at once. Student honor codes, clear policies, low-stakes [[assessment|assessment design]], and in-class proctoring remain relevant preventive measures, while detection through text-matching software or manual review carries ongoing concerns about accuracy, cost, and [[ethics]]. Their argument is not that these measures are worthless but that none of them can carry integrity alone, and that capacity building and culture building are the more foundational strategies. Institutional rewards and consequences shape whether members value integrity, and education for students plus professional development for educators serves both preventive and pedagogical functions.

That logic leads to the article's central normative claim: responsibility is shared, and responses must be pedagogically grounded rather than punitive. This is the orientation [[critical-pedagogy|critical pedagogy]] names — education that treats students as learners rather than suspects — and it runs through the authors' reading of the integrity literature: environments that prioritize learning over catching cheaters produce better integrity outcomes than surveillance and suspicion. The implication for [[governance|governance]] is that policy should articulate when and how generative AI may be used, explain the reasoning, and engage students in dialogue about responsible integration. The article closes with three coordinated demands: comprehensive institutional policy that recognizes disciplinary variation, substantial investment in faculty professional learning including release time and collaborative communities, and student-facing resources such as writing workshops and [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] programs.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** State when and how generative AI may be used, explain the pedagogical rationale, and treat disclosure as a conversation about which tools were used, how they were used, and what was learned — not as a percentage audit.
- **Administrators.** Build policy that is consistent, clearly communicated, and equitably resourced, pairing access to tools and infrastructure with training rather than access alone.
- **Instructional designers.** Design tasks and assessments that reduce the incentive and opportunity for misconduct — frequent low-stakes work, clear criteria, [[authentic-assessment|authentic tasks]] — and document expectations at the course level.
- **Students.** Disclose AI use and its extent, verify generated sources and citations, and exercise judgment about when not to use the tool at all.
- **Policymakers.** Frame integrity policy around the six values and their pedagogical rationale rather than detection and punishment, and resource professional development accordingly.

## Limitations

- The analysis rests entirely on the International Center for Academic Integrity's six-value framework, which the authors themselves describe as American-focused, Euro-centric, and not subjected to rigorous empirical testing, so the discussion may be incomplete and non-exhaustive.
- The authors deliberately bound their scope: privacy, copyright and intellectual property, generative AI's environmental impact, and gender bias are named as omitted dimensions of the broader ethical debate rather than analyzed.
- No empirical data were collected. The conditions under which values are preserved or contravened are argued from literature and analytic deduction, not observed, so the framework awaits empirical testing across diverse educational contexts.
- Definitions of academic integrity vary among and within countries, and the authors proceed from one framework while acknowledging that understandings of integrity are culturally and contextually bound.
