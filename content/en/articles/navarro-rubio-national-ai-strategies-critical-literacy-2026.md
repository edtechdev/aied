> **Synthesis:** Navarro-Rubio and Rumayor (2026) apply an eleven-indicator framework to the [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] and education provisions of forty-three national AI strategies and find a consistent asymmetry: strategies address procedural matters such as transparency and awareness far more than the cultivation of distinctively human capacities. Reflexivity reaches 25.2% of its possible maximum and the cultivation dimension 9.6%, a hierarchy that holds in every coding condition. [[creativity|Creativity]] scores 1 or below in 95% of strategies, and the indicator closest to critical literacy — whether learners may question and override AI — lags well behind transparency. Absolute scores are not the point: the same strategies coded from full documents average 13.4 of 33 against 4.83 from structured profiles, and the authors present their findings as relative. What the paper establishes is a vocabulary problem in [[educational-policy-ai|AI education policy]], not a league table of governments.

## Key Findings

1. **Procedural content crowds out cultivation.** Reflexivity reaches 25.2% of its maximum across the corpus and the cultivation dimension 9.6%, a gap the authors call proceduralization.
2. **Critical override authority is thin.** The indicator closest to critical AI literacy, whether learners may question and override AI, lags well behind transparency and awareness provisions.
3. **Creativity is near-absent.** Creativity scores 0.32 out of 3 on average, 29 of 43 strategies (67%) score exactly zero, and 41 of 43 (95%) score 1 or below; coding full documents leaves 36 of 41 at or below 1 (88%).
4. **Scores are low in absolute terms but condition-dependent.** Mean total is 4.83 of 33 (14.6%), median 4.25, and no strategy reaches half the scale under profile coding; raw-document coding gives a mean of 13.4.
5. **Educational reputation does not predict policy content.** Finland (1.75), Germany (2.75), Singapore (2.00) and the United Kingdom (1.00) all score below 3, while Greece scores highest at 12.5.
6. **No regional, income or cohort differences were detected.** The authors caution that statistical power is low, so this is an absence of evidence rather than evidence of uniformity.
7. **Model coding agreed closely with human coding.** Four LLMs coded independently at Krippendorff's α = 0.808, checked against researcher coding, a second coder and raw-document coding.

## What the framework measures, and what it does not

The Human–AI Integrated Education Framework scores each strategy on eleven indicators grouped into alignment, reflexivity and cultivation, for a maximum of 33. Coding ran in two stages: models drafted a Functional Profile of each strategy's education provisions, every quotation was audited against its source, and four models then coded the profiles independently. Because the profiles throttle what can be scored, the absolute numbers say little about how much humanistic content a document contains; the authors say so plainly and read the results comparatively across dimensions and indicators. A low score therefore means that a country's AI strategy does not engage these dimensions, not that its education policy as a whole does not — several countries handle education in separate digital-education or curriculum documents outside the corpus.

## Why the vocabulary gap matters for critical AI literacy

The paper's argument is that "human-centred" functions as a legitimation device that reassures without committing anyone to anything. A ministry can mandate wellbeing services; it cannot as easily mandate the conditions under which creativity develops, so capacities that translate into administrative provisions get written and capacities that require pedagogical action do not. That produces a policy genre in which the evaluable parts are the procedural ones, and in which critical AI literacy is asserted rather than specified. The remedies the authors propose are modest and institutional: policy vocabularies that make cultivation evaluable, and instruments that let [[governance|governance]] bodies check whether a strategy commits to anything a school could act on.

## What this means for practice

- **Policymakers and their advisers.** Write provisions a school can act on — what students may question, override or refuse, and what teachers are expected to do — rather than restating transparency and awareness commitments.
- **Administrators.** Read your national strategy for what it obliges you to teach, not what it praises. Where cultivation is unaddressed, institutional policy has to supply the vocabulary.
- **Researchers.** Use the framework as a diagnostic and report coding condition alongside scores; a single number presented as absolute would misrepresent what the instrument can support.
- **Faculty developers.** Treat "critical AI literacy" in policy text as a prompt to define locally what questioning and override look like in a course, since the documents rarely do.

## Limitations

- The corpus is one strategy per country and cross-sectional, so within-country change cannot be tracked.
- Selection criteria exclude major actors: France, the United States, China, Canada and Australia are omitted by the priority-sector rule, and Brazil, Hungary and most of Francophone Africa by accessibility and language criteria.
- Documents differ in status, purpose and length (roughly 1,300 to 75,000 words), and some countries handle education questions in separate strategies outside the corpus.
- The framework rests on Western humanistic and Kantian foundations, so it measures distance from one articulated conception of humanistic education rather than humanistic engagement as such.
- The two-stage design embeds a circularity: profiles were model-drafted and the template mirrors the framework, so inter-model agreement measures consistency given the profiles.
- No regional, income or publication-cohort differences were detected, but the authors state that power is low.
