> **Synthesis:** Farooq, Khan and Raza (2026) ask how teachers divide educational work with [[generative-ai|generative AI]] when they remain accountable for the result. In Punjab, Pakistan, they interviewed 13 teachers from government, private, NGO and vocational institutions, four administrators and one student, and coded each teacher's position on twelve educational tasks. Teachers delegated production and kept the standards: nine accepted AI drafts or substantial assistance with lesson planning, none accepted AI-drafted learning objectives, and eleven kept encouragement and emotional support for themselves. The pattern the authors call standard-anchored delegation holds that a teacher hands over a component when a standard outside the model, such as a textbook or rubric, defines a correct output and the teacher can apply that standard to the result. Having a standard was necessary but not sufficient: government-school teachers refused AI marking of quizzes that had answer keys because they expected students to look for evidence of the teacher's effort. Checking depended on subject knowledge and on time that institutions allocated unevenly, and participants read [[ai-use-disclosure|disclosure of AI use]] as a statement about whether the teacher had reviewed the work.

*Qualitatively driven [[mixed-methods-research|mixed-methods]] study; the task codes describe what participants reported doing or said they would do, not observed practice, and student perspectives come from one participant.*

## Key Findings

- **Delegation followed checkable standards.** Nine of 13 teachers accepted AI drafts or substantial assistance with lesson planning, and nine did the same for quiz and test generation.
- **Objectives and encouragement stayed with teachers.** No teacher accepted an AI-drafted learning objective, and eleven of thirteen excluded AI from encouragement and emotional support.
- **AI marking was conditional on a rubric.** Teachers who accepted AI marking required that the model be given a rubric or marking criteria.
- **A standard was not sufficient.** Government-school teachers refused AI marking of quizzes that had answer keys, expecting students to read it as a shortcut.
- **Checking required subject expertise and time.** Teachers compared outputs with the textbook, and private [[k-12|schools]] gave more institutional support for this than government schools.
- **Disclosure read as a report of review.** Teachers and the student participant treated disclosure of AI assistance as information about whether the teacher had reviewed the work.

## Where teachers drew the line

Preparation was the most delegated work in the sample. Nine teachers accepted drafts or substantial assistance with [[learning-design|lesson planning]], and nine accepted the same for quizzes and tests; one dictated plans to a [[conversational-ai|chatbot]] while commuting between schools. Learning objectives drew a different response from the same teachers: ten limited AI to formatting, suggesting ideas or checking wording, one excluded it, and none accepted a draft. Of the nine who delegated lesson planning, eight kept AI to formatting or suggestions on the same plan's objectives. The authors read this as the order [[curriculum-design|curriculum design]] prescribes, with outcomes set first and activities planned to serve them. [[assessment|Grading]] divided teachers more than any preparatory task: six accepted drafting or substantial assistance, three restricted AI to ideas or checking, and three excluded it, with four of five private-school teachers accepting against one of six government-school teachers. The study concerns [[human-ai-collaboration|teacher–AI collaboration]] among teachers who adopt general-purpose chatbots without a curation layer.

## Standards, syllabuses and subject knowledge

The standards teachers applied took three forms: codified standards such as a textbook, [[rubrics|rubric]] or answer key; knowledge of particular [[learners]]; and the learning objectives the teacher set. Codified standards were easiest to state, which is why preparation drew most of the accepted codes. Teachers who accepted AI marking tied it to a rubric given to the model, and one private-school teacher held to that threshold under pressure. Checking also required subject knowledge: a teacher who generated a twelve-question paper found three or four questions matched the textbook and described cross-checking against it as what turned AI from a risk into an asset. Another teacher restricted ChatGPT use after an AI-assisted answer contradicted the prescribed textbook, and a third warned that the model could produce explanations far beyond the depth the syllabus required. The capacity to make such comparisons is what the authors call [[evaluative-judgment|evaluative judgment]], and it depends on expertise that a polished output can disguise.

## Checking as invisible work

If delegation depends on checking, then the time and expertise for checking determine what a teacher can safely delegate. Teachers' accounts of available time differed by workplace: one government-school teacher described classes of 50 to 70 pupils and non-teaching assignments, including polio campaigns, elections and examination invigilation, that left a month of missed lessons. Private-school teachers described organized support, including a monthly staff award for incorporating AI into lesson planning, mentoring arrangements and built-in planning software. The coding record rated institutional support strong for four of five private-school teachers and none of the six government-school teachers, and rated the absence of [[educational-policy-ai|formal policy]] strong for five of the six government-school teachers. Where a coordinator reviewed work, part of the check moved off the individual teacher; elsewhere it stayed with the teacher or became a prohibition. The study situates these teachers in the [[global-south|Global South]], where many meet generative AI through general-purpose chatbots on their own phones.

## Disclosure as a report of review

Teachers disagreed about disclosing AI-assisted [[feedback]], and each position rested on a prediction about what students would infer about [[trust|trust]] and effort. One took the most open position because students already recognized AI-generated text; another predicted that students would discount AI involvement; a third accepted disclosure for assessment aligned with learning outcomes but resisted it for welcome notes and personal remarks. The student participant judged feedback by whether the teacher's own judgment was present in it, accepting AI-generated wording that carried the teacher's reading and assessment criteria and rejecting feedback pasted without reading. The authors conclude that a bare AI label hides the teacher's review, while a statement of what the teacher checked and changed shows it. Disclosure therefore works best as a report of review rather than a bare label.

## What this means for practice

- **[[teacher-role|Instructors]].** Treat the standard as part of the task: fix the lesson's objectives or the rubric before generating, and compare each output against the textbook or criteria rather than accepting fluent prose.
- **Administrators.** Treat adoption as [[change-management|organizational change]]: match oversight to the kind of standard, keep learner-dependent messages with the teacher who knows the student, and count checking time in workload.
- **Educational technology developers.** Ask for the board, grade, textbook and chapter before generating, and show each output beside the passage it draws on so the comparison becomes a visual check.
- **Policymakers.** Write rules for teachers' own AI use that require disclosure to describe the teacher's review, since the provincial department had issued no guidance at the time of the study.

## Limitations

- Participants reported their practices and reasoned live about scenarios; the study did not observe classroom practice or log generation alongside edits.
- Student perspectives came from one participant and no parents were interviewed.
- The sample is 13 teachers, four administrators and one student in Punjab, Pakistan, so the findings describe this context and may not transfer where curricula leave teachers more discretion.
- Government-school interviews covered fewer task categories, so unclear codes concentrate among those teachers.
