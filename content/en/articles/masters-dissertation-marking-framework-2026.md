> **Synthesis:** Bikanga Ada (2026) introduces the Master’s Dissertation Marking Framework (MDMF), a six-component model for fairer and more defensible master’s dissertation [[assessment]] in UK computing science. Six [[design-based-research|design-based research]] phases built it between 2022 and 2025: a literature review and 2022 staff surveys, two 2023 surveys, three follow-up interviews, and a late-2025 mixed-methods survey. The MDMF moved from procedural consistency and [[bias-mitigation]] toward a sociotechnical view treating ethical boundaries, professional judgment, institutional responsibility, and marker well-being as structural conditions of fair marking. Staff rejected AI for evaluative grading and rated it ineffective against long-standing fairness problems, while reporting that AI-mediated fluency compresses grades and masks weak work. The final version keeps six interdependent components, covering ethical boundaries and AI policy, fairness and equity, pre-marking tasks and calibration, marker allocation, marking processes and well-being, and technology as both enabler and disruptor. Version 5 defines what AI must not do rather than how it should be integrated.

## Key Findings

- Six [[design-based-research|design-based research]] phases produced the MDMF: a literature review (v1), a survey of 31 staff (v2), seven interviews (v3), two 2023 surveys (n = 19; n = 8), three 2025 interviews (v4), and a mixed-methods survey (v5).
- In 2023, 16 of 19 respondents (84%) reported never using ChatGPT for dissertation marking, and 5 of 8 in the post-marking survey reported no use, with 3 not answering.
- Late-2025 concerns were very high on 1 to 7 scales: AI’s limited originality evaluation (M = 6.45, SD = 0.69), while support for AI replacing marking was very low (M = 1.27, SD = 0.65).
- Subscales rated AI effectiveness low for bias (M = 2.08) and workload (M = 2.20) issues, against higher concerns (M = 5.60); a Friedman test confirmed the rank ordering, χ2(3) = 11.73, p = 0.008 (n = 11).
- Markers described AI-mediated grade compression and fluency able to “dress up an otherwise poor project,” prompting moderation disputes when readers lacked insight into the project process.
- MDMF v5 keeps the six components introduced in v4 but re-weights them, making ethical boundaries and policy clarity the anchor defining which judgments stay human.

## How the framework was built

The study used Design-Based Research, following the Generic Model for Design Research (McKenney and Reeves, 2012) through cycles of analysis and exploration, design and construction, and evaluation and reflection. Empirical work ran at one UK institution with staff who supervise, second-mark, and moderate MSc dissertations in Computing Science. Methods were a literature review, surveys, semi-structured interviews analyzed thematically, and a final mixed-methods survey analyzed descriptively with composite subscales. The purpose was design-relevant insight, not statistical generalization, with cautious non-parametric tests. Phases 1 to 3 addressed fairness challenges (RQ1), Phases 4 to 6 examined how these evolved with generative AI (RQ2), and all phases fed framework refinement (RQ3).

## How the framework changed across versions

MDMF v1, drawn from the literature, had three components: pre-marking tasks, assigning marking by marker characteristics, and the marking process. The 2022 survey added fairness and equity issues and technology as components (v2), and the 2022 interviews added marking culture and language bias (v3). The 2023 surveys were a diagnostic checkpoint rather than a redesign. Version 4, from the early-2025 interviews, added ethical boundaries and policy clarity in the age of AI and elaborated the framework to six components, including pre-marking calibration, marker allocation, marking processes and culture, and technology as both enabler and disruptor. Version 5 adds no components; it re-weights them using the late-2025 survey.

## What markers reported after generative AI

The final survey captured 14 qualitative and 13 quantitative responses. Structural problems dominated severity ratings: time spent on marking (M = 3.38, SD = 2.29), high marking load (M = 3.31, SD = 2.46), and lack of subject knowledge as a reader (M = 3.23, SD = 2.01), while AI was rated poorly against not knowing student contribution (M = 1.31, SD = 0.75) and lack of anonymity (M = 1.46, SD = 1.39). Bias-effectiveness items were low (supervisor bias M = 2.00, SD = 1.58; reader bias M = 1.83, SD = 1.53). Spearman correlations linked bias effectiveness to workload effectiveness (ρ = 0.683, p = 0.010) and to overall AI support (ρ = 0.914, p < 0.001). Some markers allowed only narrow, non-evaluative uses such as summarizing text.

## How the paper frames fairness and institutional responsibility

The framework treats dissertation marking as a sociotechnical system in which fairness is contextual and negotiated rather than purely procedural. Double marking, anonymity, and moderation alone cannot resolve supervisor familiarity, project complexity, language differences, or interpretation drift; generative AI reshapes rather than removes subjectivity. Version 5 treats fairness risks as emergent properties of AI-mediated writing: grade compression, fluency masking weak reasoning, and obscured authorship as a systemic [[equity-in-ai-education|equity]] problem rather than individual misconduct. Ethical [[academic-integrity|integrity]] and policy clarity become boundary-setting: institutions define non-delegable judgments, staff keep professional ownership, students declare AI use, and technology stays a peripheral support with traceability. The paper offers framework development, not an effectiveness trial.

## What this means for practice

- Pair rubrics with pre-marking calibration and discussion-based moderation; consistency depends on shared interpretation, not documents alone.
- Write explicit AI-use policies and declarations separating acceptable support (grammar checking) from unacceptable generation of arguments or paragraphs.
- Train markers to weigh critical engagement over surface fluency, and include AI-fluent but conceptually weak exemplars.
- Reduce administrative friction: single-point submission, clearer project metadata, and integrated rubrics mattered more than AI tooling.
- Treat marker fatigue and workload as fairness conditions, because [[evaluative-judgment]] degrades under overload.

## Limitations

- Empirical work came from one UK institution, mainly Computing Science, limiting transferability to other disciplines and settings.
- Samples were small and purposive, especially three Phase 5 interviews and 13 to 14 Phase 6 respondents, so findings are not statistically generalizable.
- The MDMF was iteratively refined but never fully implemented and evaluated as a complete intervention, so no effectiveness evidence is offered.
- Student perspectives on fairness, authorship, and AI use were not collected, and the identified challenges may shift as generative AI evolves.
