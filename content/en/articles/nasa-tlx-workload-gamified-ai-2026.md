> **Synthesis:** Speranza (2026) compares perceived workload across three conditions in a [[higher-ed|university]] experiment — traditional individual study, conventional gamification, and gamification with conditioned progression supported by [[generative-ai|artificial intelligence]] functions — using the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX). Welch ANOVAs found no statistically significant differences between the groups on any of the six workload dimensions: mental demand was high across all conditions while physical demand remained low, as expected for a study-based digital task. An exploratory pairwise-weighting analysis revealed two significant differences (mental demand versus perceived performance, and temporal demand versus perceived performance). The author concludes that neither [[game-based-learning|gamification]] nor AI-supported progression simply increases or reduces workload; rather, cognitive [[sustainability]] depends on how the learning path is designed, paced, explained, and supervised.

## Key Findings
1. No statistically significant differences in perceived workload (NASA-TLX) were found across traditional, gamified, and AI-supported learning conditions on any of the six dimensions.
2. Mental demand was high in all conditions; physical demand remained low — consistent with a study-based digital task.
3. Exploratory NASA-TLX pairwise weighting revealed two significant differences involving mental demand versus perceived performance and temporal demand versus perceived performance.
4. Results do not support a simple claim that gamification or AI-supported progression either raises or lowers workload — cognitive sustainability hinges on design, pacing, explanation, and supervision.
5. The study reads its findings alongside NASA-TLX [[research-methods-aied|research]] in professional, industrial, and work-related technology training.

## Discussion
The paper contributes a measured, null-result perspective to the often-overenthusiastic literature on [[game-based-learning|gamified]] and AI-supported learning in [[higher-ed|higher education]]. Its value lies in complicating the assumption that adding [[student-engagement|engagement]] mechanics or AI-driven progression automatically improves the learning experience — here, workload was not reduced by either condition, and mental demand stayed uniformly high. For the knowledge base, it connects [[student-experience|student experience]], [[motivation]], and [[learning-design|instructional design]] literatures, and complements findings on how the *quality* of technology-mediated environments — not their mere presence — shapes outcomes. It also illustrates the use of standardized [[learning-analytics|measurement instruments]] (NASA-TLX) to assess the cognitive ergonomics of AI-supported learning.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Evaluate a gamified or AI-supported unit for perceived workload before scaling it — run a short NASA-TLX check with the class rather than judging it on engagement or outcomes alone.
- **Instructors.** Expect mental demand to stay high in all conditions and offset it with clear pacing, orientation time, and worked examples instead of adding more game rules.
- **Designers.** Soften time pressure in constrained pathways: the AI-supported conditioned progression made temporal demand more salient (H = 9.995, p = .007), so explain progression rules up front and avoid hard deadlines inside the loop.
- **Instructors.** Supervise and explain the AI-supported path — the study's moderate reading is that a structured AI path is tolerable when it is coherent and supervised, not that AI lowers load by itself.

## Limitations
- Single-site university sample of 142 students split across three conditions (traditional n = 41; conventional gamification n = 49; AI-supported conditioned progression n = 52), with no claim to generalization beyond this context.
- NASA-TLX is a self-report instrument: it captures perceived workload, not objective cognitive load.
- The analysis isolates the NASA-TLX data from a broader project, so learning outcomes, motivational data, and platform traces are not examined here.
- All six primary comparisons were non-significant, so the interpretation rests on descriptive separations and two exploratory pairwise salience results that were not corrected for multiple comparisons.
