> **Synthesis:** Ahmed and Shehata argue that the integrity-and-[[ai-detection|detection]] response to [[generative-ai|generative AI]] answers the wrong question. What a submitted essay no longer establishes is that the student can do the thinking, and the risk they name is developmental: repeated delegation of retrieval, elaboration, inference and error correction may erode the capacity those activities build, or prevent a novice from ever building it. Their proposed framework, AI-integrated programmatic assessment (AIPA), keeps the [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal]], feedback-first, triangulated commitments of [[process-oriented-assessment|programmatic assessment]] and of [[assessment|assessment]] practice more broadly, and adds a formal treatment of AI use, built on three evidence streams — unaided cognition, AI-mediated performance, and [[agency|orchestration quality]] — sampled through matched task pairs and read as developmental signals rather than compliance flags.

## Key Findings

- **The inference from product to competence has broken.** When a fluent essay can be produced with little independent effort, assessment can no longer treat the submitted artifact as evidence of what the student can do.
- **The named risk is developmental, not just procedural.** Cognitive erosion means the gradual degradation of independent generative cognition — retrieval, elaboration, inference, error-based revision — when those activities are repeatedly delegated.
- **Erosion and non-development look identical in a snapshot.** Both produce a learner who performs better with AI than without it; only a documented earlier level of independent capability distinguishes decline from never having been built.
- **AIPA adds three evidence streams.** Unaided Cognition Evidence, AI-Mediated Performance Evidence and Orchestration Quality Evidence are interpreted together, so no single domain decides a high-stakes judgment.
- **Matched task pairs make the comparison fair.** Two tasks targeting the same construct at the same difficulty, one completed without AI and one with it, with a short structured [[oral-assessment|viva]] attached to each.
- **Orchestration is treated as a meta-competence.** The framework defines it as selecting, governing and integrating AI contributions while retaining epistemic responsibility, and expects a developmental arc from uncritical reliance to accountable use.
- **Credit moves from polish to reasoning.** Because judgments accrue to demonstrated reasoning rather than output quality, students have a reason to show their thinking rather than minimize it.

## The architecture in practice

The model is layered. An expanded evidence base feeds a judgment system of [[feedback|feedback cycles]], triangulation, assessor moderation, discrepancy interpretation and competence committee review, with bounded AI-assisted [[learning-analytics|analytics]] supporting but not deciding. A meta-competence strand runs across the layers, tracking five orchestration capacities from task–tool judgment to epistemic responsibility, and judgments feed back to learners as targeted sampling rather than as a final score.

The developmental arc runs from independence through hybrid performance to accountable orchestration, with the emphasis early in a program on building retrieval, explanation, problem formulation and reasoned justification, and a later shift toward selective, governed AI use. Worked examples in the paper pair a clinical case analysis written in 90 supervised minutes on a locked-down device with a parallel case written with access to an institutional AI tool, each followed by a structured viva; an engineering analogue pairs two design-scoping briefs of equivalent complexity.

The framework is explicit that orchestration must be taught before it is assessed, and that discrepancies between streams are read as developmental signals. A learner whose AI-mediated work far outperforms unaided work is not a cheat to be caught; that pattern says something about where practice is missing.

## What the framework changes for institutions

The paper's [[governance|governance]] section keeps human judgment central as a design principle rather than a residual safeguard, and treats data governance, [[privacy|privacy]] and [[equity-in-ai-education|equity]] as conditions of the model rather than afterthoughts. It also names faculty workload and institutional culture as feasibility constraints, which is where most assessment reforms fail: three evidence streams, matched task pairs and vivas multiply assessor time.

The research agenda follows from the framework's own logic. If erosion is a claim about change, it needs longitudinal designs with a documented baseline; if orchestration is a meta-competence, it needs instruments that can detect growth. Neither exists yet, and the paper presents AIPA as a testable proposal rather than an established effect.

For a program already running programmatic assessment, the practical claim is that generative AI does not require a new assessment philosophy. It requires one more evidence stream, a matched-task design, and a judgment rule that treats mismatch between streams as information.

## What this means for practice

- **Assessment designers:** stop buying detection. Add an unaided task and an AI-available task on the same construct, at the same difficulty, and read the difference rather than either score alone.
- **Instructors:** attach a short oral defense to AI-permitted work. The viva is what separates orchestration quality from output quality and it is the cheapest version of the third evidence stream.
- **Program directors:** teach orchestration before assessing it. A framework that grades AI-mediated judgment without a [[curriculum-design|curriculum]] that built it penalizes students for a capability nobody taught.
- **Administrators:** budget assessor time honestly. Three evidence streams and matched tasks are a staffing decision, not a software purchase.
- **Researchers:** treat cognitive erosion as a hypothesis requiring a documented baseline; the cross-sectional evidence the paper reviews cannot separate decline from non-development.

## Limitations

- This is a conceptual analysis: no data are collected, and every claim about erosion or orchestration growth is a proposal awaiting empirical test.
- The erosion construct requires a documented earlier level of independent capability under comparable conditions, which no study in the reviewed literature supplies.
- Three evidence streams and matched task pairs multiply assessment workload, and the paper names faculty capacity as a feasibility constraint without estimating the cost.
- The worked cases are illustrative designs rather than evaluated implementations, so the model's reliability, fairness and defensibility are untested.
- The worked cases and much of the assessment literature it builds on come from medical and health-professions education, where programmatic assessment is established; transfer to undergraduate general education is asserted rather than argued.
