> **Synthesis:** Rothwell (2026) reviews nine empirical studies on using AI to rehearse interpersonal skill and turns the evidence into a classroom exercise. The central distinction is rehearsal versus transfer: a student who handles a chatbot exchange well has demonstrated practice, not competence, because the system may supply a cooperative response, suggest language or structure the turn-taking. Across the reviewed studies, the strongest direct evidence on undergraduate team conflict shows confidence gains and conflict strategies during simulated chatbot exchanges, with later team behavior unobserved; adjacent clinical studies report mixed added benefit from AI practice. The proposed exercise sequences baseline performance, AI rehearsal with behavior-based feedback, unaided near transfer, delayed retention and optional observation in real teamwork.

*This page summarizes a focused narrative review and a proposed classroom exercise. It reports no new data: the article reviews nine studies and offers a design, so every study figure quoted here belongs to another source and is attributed to it.*

## Key Findings

- **Direct evidence on undergraduate team conflict comes from one study.** Aggrawal and Magana (2024) taught conflict management to 56 students in a systems development course, had them rehearse a conflict with a chatbot, and analyzed 46 usable submissions; students reported greater confidence and showed conflict strategies in the simulated exchanges, but later team conversations were outside the assessment.
- **The adjacent evidence sits at three different levels.** Learner reaction or perceived competence, assisted performance with AI available, and unaided performance with a human partner after practice are not interchangeable, and the review warns against collapsing them into a single claim that AI improves interpersonal skill.
- **Controlled clinical studies show mixed added benefit.** Among 51 physicians, virtual-patient practice produced greater immediate improvement in rated standardized-patient encounters (Haut et al., 2026); in two randomized medical studies both groups improved, and the added benefit of supplementary AI practice remained uncertain or imprecise (Jay-Jímenez et al., 2026; X. Wang et al., 2026).
- **Rehearsal quality decides what practice teaches.** Scenarios need plausible ambiguity and private information so the learner must discover what happened, and feedback must name the specific turn where the move went wrong.
- **Transfer must be assessed without AI.** The exercise removes the tool for the near-transfer and delayed stages, so improvement cannot be attributed to prompts or suggestions available at the moment of performance.
- **Peer rehearsal is the right comparator.** The proposed study holds objectives, scenario difficulty, time, attempts and rubric constant across AI and peer conditions and prespecifies the delayed unaided score as the primary outcome.

## What the nine studies actually measured

Rothwell began with web searches on November 18, 2026, using terms such as "conversational agent," "virtual patient" and "automatic feedback," then ran a supplementary site-restricted search on ERIC and the ACM Digital Library on November 20, 2026. The review used targeted selection and did not conduct independent screening or a formal risk-of-bias assessment.

Only one study addressed undergraduate team conflict directly. Aggrawal and Magana (2024) combined instruction, rehearsal and reflection in one course group of 56 enrolled students, analyzing 46 usable submissions; later team conversations were outside the assessment. Adjacent studies covered other populations and outcomes. Medical students reported greater perceived communication competence immediately after one GPT-4o role-play (Müller et al., 2026; 162 medical students), while an English-language learning study found favorable self-reported outcomes for an avatar condition but no significant difference in speaking performance among 99 EFL learners (C. Wang et al., 2024). Undergraduate salary negotiation outcomes improved inside a simulator before and after a lecture, with no comparison group (Geier & Zarzosa, 2025). In online peer support, messages composed with real-time AI suggestions scored higher on an automated empathy measure in a randomized study of 300 peer supporters; later unaided messages were outside the assessment (Sharma et al., 2023).

Studies of unaided performance with a human partner came closest to the educational claim. Participants who received fuller feedback from an automated interview coach improved on human-rated mock interviews over a week (Hoque et al., 2013; 90 undergraduates). In clinical communication, Haut et al. (2026) found greater immediate improvement in rated standardized-patient encounters among 51 physicians assigned to virtual-patient practice, but did not compare simulation with human rehearsal. Jay-Jímenez et al. (2026) found that both medical student groups improved after conventional instruction, with uncertain added benefit from supplementary AI practice and complete pre–post data for 51 of 80 randomized students, 29 of them incomplete. X. Wang et al. (2026) found improvement in both groups over an academic year among 50 analyzed medical students, with an imprecise estimate of the added benefit of simulator access.

## Rehearsal is not transfer

The article's load-bearing argument is that AI-assisted performance is evidence of rehearsal, while later unaided interaction is the more demanding evidence of transfer. A conversational system can reduce the scarcity of meaningful practice by allowing repeated attempts and by changing the private information, emotional tone or obstacle presented by the simulated partner. Repetition alone is not deliberate practice: the learner needs a defined behavioral target, feedback tied to observable turns in the exchange, and another opportunity to try the behavior. Conversely, a learner may appear skillful because the system supplies a cooperative response, suggests language or structures the exchange, so performance inside an AI-supported conversation is not equivalent to interpersonal competence. The stronger test is whether the learner can recognize a changing social situation, elicit another person's perspective, revise an initial interpretation and negotiate a workable next step when the tool is absent.

Scenario and [[feedback]] design follow from that distinction. A simulated teammate who apologizes and accepts the first proposal gives the student little reason to investigate what happened; a teammate offering a plausible alternative account creates the reason to ask a follow-up question and reconsider. Generic praise gives the learner no basis for a better second attempt, whereas behavior-based feedback identifies the precise moment when the learner moved too quickly, failed to check an interpretation or proposed a solution before hearing the account. Instructors should also avoid building an unrealistically compliant partner, because practicing a simplified interaction does not resemble teamwork.

## The proposed classroom exercise

The exercise is organized as a sequence rather than a single chatbot activity, with 35 minutes allowed for the initial session and 12 minutes for a follow-up one to two weeks later. Baseline performance comes first: in triads, students rotate starter, teammate and observer through three three-minute rounds, allowing two minutes for dialogue and one minute to record scores, with each starter receiving a different version of the baseline card. Rehearsal and retry follows for 12 minutes: one minute to review baseline feedback, four minutes for a first exchange, two minutes to check the feedback against the actual dialogue, four minutes for a retry and one minute to note what changed. Students who rehearse with a peer instead use the same fictional scenarios and feedback criteria. A sample prompt asks the chatbot to play a fictional teammate who missed two deadlines, to reveal private information when asked, and then to switch to coach, quote one line the student wrote, comment on one of three behaviors and suggest one thing to try before a retry with a new private detail.

Immediate near transfer is assessed over 13 minutes with uneven-workload cards and different partners, with no AI or scripts; delayed retention is checked one to two weeks later over 12 minutes with a parallel missed-deadline card and the same rubric. Observers score three behaviors — inviting the teammate's account, checking understanding and working toward a next step — separately on a 0-to-2 rubric, recording a phrase or action supporting each score. If the partner unintentionally withholds a cue or a round is cut short, the affected behavior is marked "not observed" and the round repeated where feasible. Accent, eye contact and adherence to a script sit outside the rubric.

## Directions for research

A first test could compare AI rehearsal with peer rehearsal. Students or intact sections would be randomly assigned as circumstances allow, accounting for clustering; both conditions would share the same learning objectives, scenario difficulty, time, number of attempts and feedback rubric. The primary outcome would be prespecified as the summed rubric score on a delayed, unaided conversation about a new problem, with the three item scores and the baseline, immediate near transfer and delayed retention measures reported separately. Raters would be trained on sample conversations and two raters would independently score a subset blind to condition, reporting agreement, missing assessments by condition and the between-group difference with a confidence interval. Researchers would describe the AI model and version, system instructions, prompts, feedback rules and protocol departures. When consented observation of actual team meetings is feasible, an opportunity would be identified before rating a behavior, so that ordinary uneventful teamwork is not misclassified as nontransfer, and meetings with no relevant opportunity would be recorded separately from missed opportunities.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Give students repeated AI rehearsal, then test them without it: sequence baseline, rehearsal, unaided near transfer and delayed retention, and treat improvement during chatbot practice as practice evidence, not proof of learning.
- **Instructors.** Write scenarios with plausible ambiguity and private details the learner must elicit, avoid an always-compliant partner, and make feedback point to the exact turn where the conversation went wrong.
- **Faculty developers.** Score inviting the account, checking understanding and working toward a next step separately on a 0-to-2 rubric, and mark a behavior "not observed" when no opportunity arose rather than scoring it absent.
- **Researchers.** Compare AI rehearsal against an equivalent amount of peer rehearsal, prespecify the delayed unaided outcome, and report the AI model, prompts and protocol departures so replication is possible.

## Limitations

- The article is a focused narrative review, not a systematic review: targeted selection, no independent screening or risk-of-bias assessment and an incomplete original search record mean coverage of the evidence base remains uncertain.
- The nine studies differ in population, interpersonal task, AI role, comparator and outcome; the author states they should not be collapsed into a single claim.
- Direct evidence for undergraduate team conflict rests on one course study whose later team behavior was unobserved; the remaining studies are adjacent in setting and several rely on self-reported confidence or assisted performance.
- Controlled clinical studies report mixed added benefit, and uncertainty or imprecision surrounds the estimated contribution of AI practice.
- The classroom exercise and research design are proposals; neither has been piloted or evaluated in the article.
