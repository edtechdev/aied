> **Synthesis:** Lara Nieto-Márquez, Madrigal-Cerezo, Ramos-Marcos, Rueda-Díaz, García Martín and López-Muñoz set out to measure something existing instruments do not: students' own account of the [[critical-thinking|critical-thinking]] processes they activate when they work with AI. They built the 14-item Critical Thinking with AI Scale for Students (CTAI-S) from published critical-thinking questionnaires, had it reviewed by a three-member panel, and administered it to 271 university students at one private Spanish university; one item was dropped after psychometric screening. Parallel analysis favored a single factor, total-score reliability was high, and ordinal confirmatory models fitted best when three theoretically proposed domains were allowed to correlate, though robust fit indices were less favorable and the domains overlapped heavily. Students agreed with the statements above the scale midpoint overall, with Reflective Judgment and Self-Regulation highest and Problem Analysis lowest; nearly all had used AI for academic work, ChatGPT dominated, and verification was spontaneously mentioned by about one respondent in ten. The authors state plainly that the scale measures perceived rather than demonstrated thinking and that dimensional or diagnostic use awaits independent validation.

## Key Findings

- **A short global scale survived screening.** An initial 15-item pool became 14 items after one item was removed on converging evidence: it had the weakest exploratory loading, the lowest communality, the lowest corrected item–total correlation, and the weakest loadings in both confirmatory specifications.
- **Parallel analysis pointed to one factor.** The first observed eigenvalue far exceeded its simulated counterpart while the second did not, and the one-factor solution explained just under two-fifths of the variance; the researchers still examined a theory-guided three-factor solution.
- **The total score is reliable; the dimensions are provisional.** Total-score internal consistency was high, and two of the three proposed dimensions reached adequate values, but the third fell well below conventional thresholds and rests on only three items.
- **Three domains overlap heavily.** Latent correlations among the proposed factors were very high, average variance extracted fell below the squared correlations, and the discriminant-validity criterion was not met for any factor.
- **Fit improved with three correlated domains, but robust indices lagged.** Scaled indices were more favorable for the three-domain and second-order models than for the one-factor model, while the robust indices remained below conventional cutoffs for both.
- **Students endorsed the statements.** Overall agreement sat above the five-point scale midpoint, with Reflective Judgment and Self-Regulation scored highest and Problem Analysis and Solution Evaluation lowest; total scores were similar across the four knowledge areas.
- **AI use was nearly universal, verification talk was rare.** Almost all respondents reported prior academic AI use, ChatGPT was named by more than nine in ten, and information search accounted for nearly three quarters of coded uses while explicit contrast or checking accounted for about one in ten.

## Building and screening the instrument

The CTAI-S began as a targeted narrative review of published [[critical-thinking|critical-thinking]] instruments, which supplied an initial 15-item pool organized into five conceptual blocks: interpretation and analysis of information, information assessment and review, systemic thinking, inference and decision-making, and problem solving and assignments. A three-member multidisciplinary panel from the research team reviewed each draft item against its source instrument, refined wording, and removed redundant statements; no quantitative content-validity index was calculated. The questionnaire was administered in Spanish on a five-point Likert scale, with all items scored in the direct direction. Before rating, students were instructed to answer in relation to their use of AI tools in academic activities. The English version in the appendix is a reporting translation and has not been validated.

Item screening drove two decisions the authors flag openly. One item combining creative alternatives with careful analysis was excluded because it performed worst on every indicator, and the exclusion produced the largest gain in internal consistency. A second item was retained despite a comparatively weak loading because of its theoretical relevance to the third domain, and the authors mark that retention as provisional. Two further items carry conceptual caveats: one expresses a preference for familiar methods over new syntheses, which may not indicate stronger reasoning, and another combines holding beliefs under insufficient evidence with identifying biases.

## Structure: one general factor, three overlapping domains

The sample was a convenience sample of 271 university students at a single private university (mean age 20.62; ages 17–48), enrolled across Health, Education, Communication, and Technology programs. Fourteen participants left items unanswered, so descriptive results by knowledge area and the confirmatory analyses used 257 complete cases. Exploratory analysis on the 14 items found excellent sampling adequacy (KMO 0.927) and a significant sphericity test. Parallel analysis retained one factor: the first observed eigenvalue clearly exceeded its simulated value, while the second did not. The one-factor solution accounted for 39.4% of the variance. A theory-guided three-factor solution with oblique rotation accounted for 44.9% but showed cross-loadings and substantial inter-factor correlations, so the authors treat it as provisional.

[[assessment-validity|Confirmatory]] analysis used ordinal weighted least squares on the 257 complete cases, with the five response categories treated as ordered indicators and no post hoc residual covariances added. The one-factor model produced acceptable scaled indices but weaker robust ones. The correlated-three-factor and second-order models fit identically, which the authors note is expected because a three-factor higher-order structure is just-identified; identical fit therefore cannot independently confirm a hierarchical organization. Both three-domain specifications improved on the one-factor model according to an adjusted difference test, yet latent correlations between domains were high, average variance extracted stayed below the squared correlations, and the [[self-report-measures|scale's]] discriminant validity remained limited. Internal consistency was high for the total score and adequate for the first two domains, but the third domain showed low reliability.

## Scores, tools, and reported uses

Descriptively, item means ranged from 3.44 to 4.19, all above the midpoint. The highest-scoring proposed dimension was Reflective Judgment and Self-Regulation, followed by Critical Reasoning and Decision-Making, with Problem Analysis and Solution Evaluation lowest. Total scores were similar across the four knowledge areas, ranging from 3.91 in Education to 4.07 in Communication; the authors stress these subgroup values are descriptive only and were not tested inferentially. The construct touches [[metacognition|metacognitive]] monitoring and [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulation]], which the authors connect to revising judgments when new evidence appears.

Of the 257 respondents answering the contextual questions, 96.5% reported previous academic use of [[generative-ai|generative AI]] tools. ChatGPT was by far the most frequently mentioned tool, followed by Copilot and visual or image-generation tools. Coding of the open-ended responses produced seven main categories and 16 subcodes. The most frequent uses were information search and cognitive support, followed by inspiration and planning, presentation design, summarizing, writing support, and explicit contrast or verification, which appeared in about one response in ten. Because a single researcher performed the coding and inter-coder reliability was not assessed, and because the question offered information search as an example, the authors caution that these frequencies describe what students spontaneously articulated rather than what they actually do. They also note that widespread AI use should not be equated with critical engagement, and that perceived criticality may not translate into articulated [[hallucination-risk|verification]] practices.

## What this means for practice

- **Teachers.** Treat the total score as the defensible number and the three groupings as hypotheses: the instrument supports a global reading of self-perceived critical thinking, while the third grouping is not reliable enough to report separately.
- **Assessment designers.** Use the scale for research and program-level description rather than individual diagnosis; the authors state that identifying competence gaps or evaluating interventions requires further validation.
- **Instructors.** Note the gap between widespread AI use and rare spontaneous mention of verification: activities requiring source checking, comparison of alternatives, justification of decisions, and evaluation of AI output are the obvious design response.
- **Researchers.** Administer performance-based tasks alongside the CTAI-S, since the instrument measures perception and cannot show whether students actually verify, compare, or revise.
- **Faculty developers.** Treat critical thinking with AI as related to general critical thinking rather than distinct from it; several items assess dispositions that also operate outside AI-supported contexts.

## Limitations

- The CTAI-S is a self-report measure, so it assesses perceived rather than demonstrated critical-thinking performance; performance-based tasks, [[rubrics|rubrics]], or independently evaluated products are needed to test the relationship.
- The convenience sample came from a single private university and was dominated by students who already used AI, restricting generalizability and comparisons with non-users.
- Item selection and the confirmatory analyses ran on the same development dataset without independent cross-validation, and the exploratory analysis used pairwise missing-data handling while the confirmatory analysis used 257 complete cases.
- Scaled and robust fit indices were not concordant, and robust indices did not support claims of excellent fit for either the one-factor or the three-domain specifications.
- The three proposed dimensions showed high latent correlations, inadequate discriminant-validity evidence, cross-loadings, and low internal consistency for the third domain; the retained and removed items include formulations the authors say need conceptual re-examination.
- The open-ended findings rest on a single coder without inter-coder reliability, and the example given in the question may have inflated the frequency of information search relative to other uses, including verification.
