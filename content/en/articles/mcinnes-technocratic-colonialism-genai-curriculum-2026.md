> **Synthesis:** McInnes (2026) argues that the adoption of [[generative-ai|generative artificial intelligence]] in higher education curriculum design is not a neutral efficiency gain but a form of technocratic colonialism. Working from critical realism and [[critical-pedagogy|critical pedagogy]], and reviewing scholarly and gray literature published since late 2022, he asks who benefits from these tools and whose interests are marginalized. His answer traces a chain from data surveillance and [[bias-mitigation|algorithmic bias]] through digital colonialism, labor exploitation and environmental cost to the ideological capture of the university by neoliberal and techno-futurist logics. Generative AI, he contends, normalises surveillance, automates epistemic exclusion, de-skills academic labor and presents Western knowledge as universal while rendering Indigenous and local epistemologies illegible. The paper is an argument, not a study: it reports no data of its own and rests on cited secondary claims. Its remedy is critical education — participatory [[governance|governance]], critical literacies and refusal of the framing that adoption is inevitable.

*Critical position paper built from a narrative [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|literature review]]: it reports no primary data, so every figure and finding below is an attributed claim about other studies rather than a result of this one.*

## Key Findings

- **Adoption is framed as technocratic colonialism.** Tools presented as neutral are shaped by the values and ideologies of the technocrats who design them, and their rollout reproduces the sociotechnical inattention associated with earlier educational technologies.
- **The harms cascade from data extraction to global inequality.** Data surveillance and algorithmic bias feed digital colonialism, in which Global South contributors supply data and labor without benefit while Western-owned platforms control the knowledge infrastructure universities depend on.
- **The human-in-the-loop safeguard is largely illusory.** Users [[trust-calibration|overtrust]] inconsistent systems, conform to their outputs and can reinforce the very biases that oversight is meant to catch, leaving learners especially vulnerable while their critical skills are still forming.
- **Systemic costs are labor, environmental and corporate.** He links adoption to exploitative content-labeling work, the water and carbon footprint of training large models, and a black-box monopoly in which a few firms control foundation-model infrastructure and data access.
- **The ideological driver is neoliberal rationalisation joined to techno-futurism.** Together they treat efficiency and inevitability as duties, narrow the space for critical refusal and subordinate education to speculative futures and market logics.
- **The remedy is critical education rather than better safeguards.** Reflexive, collective and structurally aware praxis — critical literacies, participatory governance centered on marginalized voices, and protected space for human judgment, care and [[creativity]] — is his alternative to unchecked integration.

## From tools to technocratic colonialism

McInnes opens from an expanded definition of curriculum — the conscious and unconscious experiences that are intended, taught, assessed and learned — so that both the visible and hidden curriculum become sites of inquiry into whose knowledge counts. Curriculum, on this account, is a sociopolitical artifact shaped by historical, cultural, ideological and epistemological forces rather than a neutral sequence of teaching. That framing carries two inheritances he keeps in view: neoliberalism's transformation of [[higher-ed|higher education]] from a public good into a market-driven business, and colonial curricula that devalue or render invisible Indigenous Knowledges. In Australia he notes that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledges are often treated as supplementary rather than structurally embedded, and that publicly available [[generative-ai|generative AI]] frameworks focus on technical competence, ethical use and [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]] without any reference to decolonial principles or epistemic justice.

He situates his own position explicitly, writing as a cis-hetero white male educator on unceded Kaurna Yerta and naming the privileges that shape what he can see. The stance is critical realist: he distinguishes the empirical, the actual and the real, and argues that most research on [[ai-education|AI in education]] stays at the empirical surface — tools, functionalities, observable impacts — while the underlying drivers, mechanisms and structures go uninterrogated. That is the move that turns the title into an argument. [[critical-pedagogy|Critical pedagogy]] and educational technology critiques supply the lenses, and technological solutionism, he contends, has leached from the wider edtech literature into generative AI, where tool-agnostic ethical guidelines function less as safeguards than as legitimising discourses that permit unchecked expansion under the guise of responsible use. The [[curriculum-design|curriculum design]] question therefore becomes one of power before it becomes one of efficiency.

## Surveillance, bias and the human-in-the-loop paradox

McInnes builds the case from technical concerns outward. Data surveillance comes first: models trained on datasets scraped at scale, often without consent, with copyright disputes and mass-harvesting [[legal-issues-and-risks|lawsuits]] exposing the extraction. Universities historically committed to academic integrity then integrate tools that commodify knowledge and turn student and educator data into corporate assets, a shift he reads through surveillance capitalism and writes about in terms of [[privacy|privacy]], intellectual property and behavioral extraction. Algorithmic bias follows, because training data reflects the historical dominance of Western, Anglicised and male perspectives; outputs reproduce and intensify stereotypes, and proprietary black boxes make that bias difficult to audit or rectify.

The [[human-in-the-loop-ai|human-in-the-loop]] ideal does not escape this. He cites evidence that people overtrust unreliable systems, inadvertently reinforcing rather than correcting machine bias — users he describes, following Krügel and colleagues, as "zombies in the loop" — and notes that learners are especially vulnerable because they are still developing critical skills. The result is anticipatory obedience, a tendency to conform to AI output under misplaced trust, which erodes the shared agency on which curriculum work depends. [[automation-bias|Automation bias]] is the mechanism he sees operating whenever a fluent output arrives without a cue that verification is required.

## Digital colonialism, labor and the black box

The technical concerns widen into institutional and global patterns. He describes digital colonialism: communities in the [[global-south|Global South]] receive little or no benefit from the data and labor that train models, while Western-owned platforms control the infrastructure through which curricula, research agendas and institutional strategies are mediated. A privilege of the early "clean" internet compounds this, because models trained before the flood of synthetic content enjoyed a first-mover advantage, and recursive training on AI-generated data risks model collapse that omits marginalized perspectives. A [[digital-divide|gen-AI divide]] then stratifies access, with premium subscriptions favoring the economically advantaged and free systems more likely to encode hegemonic bias.

At the level of production he documents exploitative labor: low-paid workers labeling and filtering harmful content, exposed to deeply disturbing material with minimal psychological support, alongside accusations of child labor and of delayed or canceled payments. The [[sustainability|environmental]] costs follow, including the water and carbon footprint of training large models such as GPT-3, costs that fall disproportionately on communities least responsible for them. Finally he names a black-box monopoly in which a small number of firms — Google, Microsoft and Amazon collectively control over two-thirds of the foundation-model market — dominate infrastructure, exclusive data deals and the terms on which universities can access these tools. Concentration at that scale, he argues, constrains which datasets and models institutions can reach and shapes [[llm|large language model]] development around Global North priorities.

## Neoliberalism, TESCREAL and epistemic injustice

The deepest layer is ideological. Under neoliberal pressure — productivity, workload, competition — universities automate core teaching and curriculum work, repositioning educators as facilitators of AI-generated content and reducing education to a transaction rather than an intellectually generative process. He connects this to the TESCREAL bundle of techno-futurist thought, which frames generative AI as inevitable, beneficial and morally imperative, embedding elite techno-utopian assumptions while obscuring immediate [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] and social harms. The colonial logic runs alongside: generative AI embeds Western epistemologies as the norm and renders other ways of knowing illegible, amplifying testimonial injustice and contributing to hermeneutical injustice. Knowledge absent from these systems risks becoming functionally non-existent as the tools come to define what is recognized as real.

His conclusion is that universities are active participants rather than passive recipients: generative AI reshapes what counts as knowledge and who gets to produce it, and technical fixes or policy safeguards cannot address consequences that require a sustained commitment to critical education. The alternative he proposes is reflexive, collective and structurally aware praxis — critical literacies embedded in [[educational-development|academic development]], participatory [[governance|governance]] centered on marginalized voices, and reclaiming human judgment, care and creativity as qualities worth protecting rather than inefficiencies to optimize. [[educational-policy-ai|Policy]], on this account, has to change the neoliberal imperatives shaping university governance rather than merely write principles for tool use. The stakes are [[equity-in-ai-education|equity]] and [[agency|intellectual agency]] at once, and the choice he poses is not whether to adopt generative AI but whether to do so in ways that align with the values higher education claims to uphold.

## What this means for practice

- **Curriculum designers.** Treat generative AI adoption as a question of power before a question of efficiency: ask whose knowledge the tool amplifies and which epistemologies it renders invisible before it shapes a course.
- **Instructors.** Keep authorship with the learner and the educator — preserve tasks that build judgment, and make verification a designed step rather than an assumed disposition when students use these tools.
- **Faculty developers.** Build critical literacies into academic development, covering how models are trained, why they fail, and how data extraction and labor practices connect to classroom use.
- **Administrators.** Write governance that centers marginalized voices and Indigenous data sovereignty instead of adopting tool-agnostic ethical principles that legitimize expansion.
- **Policymakers.** Require structural accountability — transparency, auditability and equity review — rather than relying on voluntary [[guardrails]] that carry no enforcement.

## Limitations

- This is a narrative literature review rather than a systematic one: no PRISMA flow, no dual independent coding and no formal quality appraisal, and the author notes that search-engine algorithms may have introduced selection bias into the results.
- It reports no primary data, so its claims about surveillance, algorithmic bias, labor exploitation and epistemic exclusion rest on cited secondary sources and gray literature rather than on measurement.
- The critique addresses post-2022 generative AI in higher education as a single category; differences between models, disciplines, task types and national systems are not separated out.
- The analysis is explicitly situated in Australia and in the author's stated positionality, so its examples — Indigenous Knowledges, university frameworks, colonial legacies — may not transfer directly to other contexts.
- The remedies, from participatory governance to critical literacies and refusal of inevitability, are programmatic and are neither piloted nor evaluated in the paper.
