> **Synthesis:** Richter and colleagues (2026) ask why one technology spread so quickly through [[higher-ed|higher education]] systems that otherwise share little. Their answer is practice. In episodic interviews with 29 students at universities in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Germany, students describe a strikingly similar instrumental stance toward [[generative-ai|generative AI]]: the tool should make their studies more efficient without changing what an essay or a presentation means. ChatGPT was named by every participant, and use clustered on text work — drafting, summarizing, structuring, revising, translating. Students presented themselves as being in charge of the technology, and they framed studying as the efficient completion of assigned tasks whose outputs are graded. That, the authors argue, is why generative AI is easy to adopt and hard to govern from above: it lets students keep familiar academic practices intact, while making their work amenable to rote computing and mechanical processing.

*Comparative [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] interview study: 29 students at three universities (10 from Indonesia, 9 from Malaysia, 10 from Germany), interviewed in national or local languages between September 2024 and April 2025.*

## Key Findings

- **A shared instrumental stance.** Across all three countries the students treated generative AI as a tool that should not alter the nature or purpose of their academic work; what varied was which tasks they handed over to it.
- **Wide tool range, narrow purposes.** The 29 students named 27 different applications, yet ChatGPT was mentioned by every participant and use concentrated on text-related tasks such as drafting, summarizing, structuring, revision, and translation.
- **Three roles, one hierarchy.** Students cast AI as an assistant that scaffolds their own thinking, as relief from routine labor, and occasionally as a conversation partner — and in every framing human judgment stayed primary.
- **Control is the recurring claim.** Detailed prompting, critical reading of output, and cross-checking against other sources were how students said they stayed in charge of the technology.
- **Studying means finishing assignments.** Students consistently described higher education as the systematic completion of assigned tasks whose artifacts are graded, which made saving time the most reported reason for using AI.
- **Continuity rather than disruption.** The authors argue the technology spread because it fits established academic practices and the epistemic regimes behind them, not because it transformed those practices.

## A practice lens on adoption

Much critical literature treats generative AI as an educational technology: something institutions can design, regulate, and control from outside. Richter and colleagues want a critique from within, and they ground it in a practice-theoretical position drawn from Schatzki. On that account, appropriation is the active and ongoing incorporation of a technology into an existing social practice, and it reconfigures the practice rather than merely adding a tool to it ([[theories-and-frameworks]]). Practices are organized by practical rules, understandings, and aspirations; in higher education those organize into *epistemic regimes* — collectively shared ideas about what counts as legitimate knowledge, how it is produced, and how authority is established or contested ([[situated-learning]]).

Students, in this view, are gradually enculturated into the epistemic practices of a disciplinary and institutional community ([[sociocultural-learning]]). Uptake, then, cannot be reduced to individual attitudes or a diffusion campaign, but has to be explained by how well a tool fits the existing objects, bodies, and meanings that make up a practice. It also shifts attention to [[agency|epistemic agency]] as situated rather than individually possessed, and to which ways of knowing a technology favors or marginalizes ([[learning-theories]]).

## Three systems, one instrumental stance

The sample was recruited through public calls at three universities: 10 students in Indonesia, 9 in Malaysia, and 10 in Germany, 21 of them women and 8 men, all at least in their third semester and active users of generative AI. Interviews were conducted in Indonesia in September 2024, in Germany in December 2024 and January 2025, and in Malaysia in April 2025, then transcribed, anonymized, and translated into English.

ChatGPT was mentioned by every participant. Quillbot was named by six students, Grammarly and Gemini by five each, and in total the 29 students named 27 different applications, from generic [[conversational-ai|chatbots]] to writing aids, research applications, and specialized tools. Students named between one and five applications: students in Malaysia used an average of 3.4, students in Indonesia named 2.7 actively used applications, and students in Germany mentioned 1.6. Use clustered on text-related activity — analysis, drafting, summarizing, structuring, and revision — with roughly a third also reporting literature searches, concept clarification, and concept development. Half of the German students named translations as a use case, against one participant from Indonesia and none from Malaysia.

Three framings recur. AI as an *assistant*: a [[scaffolding|scaffold]] for clarifying concepts, generating outlines, or producing draft material the student then revises, explicitly positioned as support, not replacement — "I believe it is important to use AI tools as assistants rather than as shortcuts" (M1). AI as *practical relief*: a labor-saving device that reduces cognitive, temporal, and emotional burden, described by one student as "just working for me" (G1). And AI as *partner*: "a conversation partner or study partner" (I7), "brainstorming partner" (M1, M2), addressed in warmly relational terms (G7) yet still identified as "not a human being, but a computer" (G10). Even then the partnership stays instrumental: the function shifts with the task, and human judgment remains primary ([[student-ai-interaction]]).

## The control claim and its premise

Almost every interview contains a claim to be in charge of the epistemic process and of the tool's outputs — "But the final product still has to come from me" (M2). Three strategies carry that claim. First, specificity in [[prompt-engineering|prompting]]: vague prompts brought inaccurate results, and one student called prompting a skill that "should be taught just like research methodology" (M3). Second, critical assessment of output: students edited, refined, or combined suggestions rather than adopting them verbatim, and said they avoided results they did not understand. Third, verification: systematic cross-checking against lecture materials, textbooks, databases, journals, or alternative tools, sharpened by fabricated output — one student checked every reference against Google Scholar (M4) ([[hallucination-risk]]).

The authors' caution is that the claim to control rests on a premise: the delegated operations must be of a kind whose adequacy can be mechanically derived from what is already known. Where that premise fails — a task involving religious sensitivity (M5), or repeated prompting that produces images but not the intended ones (G5) — the claim becomes harder to sustain. This, they argue, is what makes instrumental use affordable: it lets students present themselves as responsible users while leaving large parts of their work devoid of personal stance, and therefore available to rote computing and mechanical processing ([[metacognition]], [[critical-thinking]]). Their argument is not that students are duped; it is that the technology lets a familiar regime continue undisturbed, which is why the challenge narrows to rules and skills rather than to what academic knowledge work is ([[academic-integrity]], [[trust]]).

## Studying as assignment completion

Zooming out from the tool, the interviews describe studying as the systematic completion of assigned tasks — weekly readings, exercises, portfolios, presentations, term papers, larger projects — which orient activity, determine available resources, and set the criteria for success. Success is read off tangible artifacts — accepted, positively graded, able to withstand questioning — and several students resolved uncertainty through an instructor's [[feedback]] rather than by asking directly: "If I can explain them correctly and my lecturer agrees, then I know the AI-generated response was accurate" (I6). Because the process is invisible in the product, the manner of production becomes less relevant: one student described using ChatGPT an hour before a deadline, submitting on time, and receiving full marks (I1).

Efficiency is the most reported motive, covering workload reduction, freed resources for tasks judged more worthwhile, and balancing study with paid work, community service, or leisure. Across all three locations the students framed study above all as professional qualification, with enculturation happening through completing a series of assignments whose success is the collectively visible indicator of progress. The authors read this as a far-reaching *learnification* of the educational discourse, and as the condition that makes an output-oriented, efficiency-seeking tool reasonable ([[student-engagement]], [[motivation]], [[self-regulated-learning]], [[assessment]]).

The comparison is the point. If Indonesian, Malaysian, and German students appeal to strikingly similar ideas about using generative AI, the authors argue, that cannot be attributed to the availability of a new technology alone; it points to a dominant epistemic regime that students align with across national and disciplinary boundaries. Diffusion then looks horizontal rather than top-down: a shift in practice configurations that escapes conventional [[governance]].

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Treat students' instrumental framing as the design problem rather than as a failure to engage critically: make the meaning of an assignment explicit alongside the artifact it produces, so that efficiency cannot silently stand in for understanding.
- **Instructional designers.** Build tasks where the process is visible in the product — drafts, annotations, and revision notes carried alongside the submission — because the study found that invisible processes stop mattering to students.
- **Administrators.** Do not expect rules and guidelines to change practice on their own. The students in this study knew the public guidance and still framed AI as an instrument; governance has to address what assignments reward.
- **Faculty developers.** Treat prompting as a taught skill and make epistemic regimes an object of discussion, since prompting was learned informally and disciplinary standards were invoked as often by students as by instructors.

## Limitations

- The sample is 29 volunteers recruited by public call, and the authors state it is not representative; a more detailed analysis is needed.
- Episodic interviews report what students say they do, not observed behavior; the authors treat the accounts as attempts to articulate experience and legitimately intelligible positions rather than objective records.
- All interviews were conducted in the participants' national or local languages and then transcribed, anonymized, and translated into English by the research teams and trained student assistants, so translation sits inside the evidence chain.
- One institution per country, each chosen for a different historical path and a different discipline mix, so the comparison rests on three cases rather than a matched sample.
- The authors describe their own account as coarse-grained and not doing justice to the diversity of positions taken; they also note that the German subsample drew only women respondents, for reasons unknown.
- No model versions are reported, so the account describes student practice during the 2024–2025 interview window rather than any specific generation of the technology.
