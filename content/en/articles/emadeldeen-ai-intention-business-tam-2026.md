> **Synthesis:** A survey of 521 undergraduate business students at private universities in Cairo, Egypt, tests an extended [[technology-acceptance-model]] in which job relevance, self-efficacy, perceived resources, and anxiety feed perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, which then shape attitude and behavioral intention to use AI. Usefulness and ease of use remain the central drivers of attitude, but the added antecedents matter too: confidence in one's own ability, the fit of AI with future work, and available resources and institutional support all strengthen those perceptions. Contrary to expectations, anxiety is linked to slightly higher perceived ease of use and shows no reliable indirect path to intention. Attitude is the strongest route to intention, with usefulness next. In a developing economy, the authors conclude, institutional support and access to technological resources are decisive for adoption, while anxiety plays a weaker, context-dependent role.

## Key Findings
1. **Usefulness and ease of use still drive attitudes.** Students who see AI as useful and easy to use form more positive attitudes toward it, and attitude is the strongest route to intention.
2. **Confidence in one's own ability matters.** [[self-efficacy]] raises both perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness, so students who believe they can use AI are more likely to value it and intend to use it.
3. **Resources and institutional support matter.** Perceived resources — tools, training, and infrastructure — strengthen both perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness, and reach intention through those beliefs.
4. **Job relevance feeds usefulness.** Students who see AI as applicable to their future work perceive it as more useful, which then supports attitude and intention.
5. **Anxiety runs the other way here.** Rather than deterring use, [[anxiety-and-stress|anxiety]] is linked to slightly higher perceived ease of use, while its indirect routes to attitude and intention are not significant.
6. **The model fits, but not perfectly.** Overall fit is acceptable on the main residual index, though several other fit indices fall short of preferred cutoffs.
7. **All but one proposed path is supported.** Every hypothesized relationship is significant except the expected negative effect of anxiety on perceived ease of use.

## What the study measured

The questionnaire was sent by email and answered on Google Doc during Spring 2024 and Fall 2024. It returned 521 responses out of 1,250, a response rate of 41.76%. Participants were undergraduate business students at private universities in Cairo, Egypt, drawn from a population of 18 private universities in the city that have schools of business. Majors included Accounting, Finance, Marketing, Management, Information Technology Systems, and Economics. The sample was almost evenly split: 264 students (50.67%) were female and 257 (49.33%) were male. Accounting was the largest major at 34.16%; 35.51% were in their second year and 31.29% in their final year. A total of 65.07% said they were not willing to pay for AI applications, and 45.49% reported non-daily use.

The instrument held 42 items across eight constructs, answered on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Four constructs were treated as antecedents — [[self-efficacy]], anxiety, job relevance, and perceived resources — and four as endogenous outcomes: perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, attitude, and behavioral intention. Sampling adequacy was confirmed by a Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin value of 0.911, and Bartlett's test of sphericity was significant at the 1% level (χ² = 2,799.342). A pilot version went to 20 randomly selected academics and professionals; six completed questionnaires came back, leading only to minor wording revisions.

The authors analyzed the data with structural equation modeling in Smart PLS 4.1. Reliability was strong, with Cronbach's alpha values all above 0.7. Convergent validity was adequate for most constructs, except perceived resources, whose average variance extracted was 0.476 — just below the 0.5 threshold — after two low-loading items were deleted.

## What the model found

All hypothesized paths were significant at p < 0.05. The strongest relationship in the model ran from attitude to behavioral intention (0.723, t = 20.892, p = 0.000). Perceived usefulness fed attitude (0.463), as did perceived ease of use (0.352); ease of use also fed perceived usefulness (0.349), and usefulness reached intention directly (0.102, p = 0.007). The authors read these as the core TAM pathways and as evidence that attitude is a key mediator of adoption.

The added antecedents were also significant. [[self-efficacy]] affected perceived ease of use (0.431) and perceived usefulness (0.175); perceived resources affected both (0.366 and 0.181 respectively); and job relevance affected perceived usefulness (0.273). Indirect routes carried much of this influence: usefulness to attitude to intention (0.335), ease of use to attitude to intention (0.255), resources to ease of use to attitude (0.137), and self-efficacy to ease of use to attitude (0.150). The authors treat these as evidence that confidence, resources, and job fit work through students' beliefs about AI rather than acting directly on intention.

Anxiety was the exception. The expected negative path to perceived ease of use was rejected: the observed effect was positive (0.077, t = 2.127, p = 0.033), significant at the 5% level. The paper suggests that worry about AI may push students to learn it rather than avoid it, especially when academic and professional relevance is salient.

Its indirect routes were not significant — anxiety to ease of use to usefulness (0.020, p = 0.132), anxiety to ease of use to attitude to intention (0.015, p = 0.116), and anxiety to ease of use to usefulness to intention (0.002, p = 0.223). Overall fit was acceptable but imperfect: the standardized root mean square residual was 0.059 for the saturated model and 0.082 for the estimated model, chi-square was 2,737.803 versus 2,938.103, and the normed fit index was 0.806 versus 0.791, below the 0.90 cutoff.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Present AI tools as relevant to business tasks and future careers, since job relevance strengthened perceived usefulness in this sample.
- **Instructors.** Build short, low-stakes practice that lets students experience AI as easy to use, because ease of use fed both attitude and usefulness.
- **Institutions.** Offer training and clear instructions that raise students' confidence with AI, since self-efficacy raised both perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness.
- **Institutions.** Ensure reliable access to AI tools, technical support, and infrastructure, since perceived resources shaped students' beliefs and intentions in a setting where access is uneven.
- **Researchers.** Treat [[anxiety-and-stress|anxiety]] as context-dependent rather than assuming it suppresses adoption; it did not reduce perceived ease of use here.

## Limitations
- The judgmental sample comes only from private universities in [[higher-ed|Cairo]], so results may not generalize to public universities, where resources and infrastructure differ, or to other regions.
- All outcomes came from [[self-report-measures|self-reported]] questionnaire data, raising the possibility of common method bias; the authors note that objective logs or assignment metadata would strengthen the evidence.
- The cross-sectional design captures relationships at one point in time and cannot establish direction or within-individual change.
- The study did not compare economic, social, or cultural environments, so its account of anxiety and resources may be specific to one developing-economy setting.
- Convergent validity for perceived resources fell slightly below the 0.5 threshold (0.476) after two low-loading items were removed.
