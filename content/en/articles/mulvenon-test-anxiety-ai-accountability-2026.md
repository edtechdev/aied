> **Synthesis:** Sean W. Mulvenon (2026) revisits the multi-stakeholder [[theories-and-frameworks|framework]] of [[anxiety-and-stress|test anxiety]] he and his colleagues set out in 2005, asking what survives when assessment moves from episodic high-stakes testing to the continuous, data-driven evaluation that [[adaptive-learning|adaptive systems]], real-time [[feedback]], and [[generative-ai|generative AI]] make possible. It is a conceptual manuscript, not an empirical study: it re-reads the 2005 findings — student anxiety overstated, teachers the most stressed group, external pressure rather than anxiety tied to weaker performance — against recent AI-in-education and [[learning-analytics]] literature. Its contribution is to name algorithmic accountability as a new pressure system and to propose an AI-Mediated Multi-Stakeholder Anxiety Model built from stakeholders, AI systems, and the pressure mechanisms of immediacy, visibility, and data permanence. The central claim is that AI does not eliminate test anxiety but redistributes it into more diffuse, persistent, and system-driven forms, with consequences for [[learning-design|instructional design]] and [[teacher-role|teacher roles]].

## Key Findings

1. **The 2005 framework still explains the distribution, not the mechanism.** Mulvenon and colleagues found student anxiety overstated, teachers the most stressed group, and external pressure rather than anxiety tied to weaker performance.
2. **Assessment pressure shifted from episodic to continuous.** Adaptive testing, real-time feedback, and predictive analytics embed evaluation in everyday learning, so stress no longer peaks and subsides around midterms and finals.
3. **Algorithmic accountability is proposed as a new pressure system.** Automated systems that evaluate performance, inform decisions, and enforce standards add accountability that interacts with, and can challenge, human-centered judgment.
4. **Three mechanisms carry the pressure.** Immediacy (fast feedback cycles), visibility (data opened to more stakeholders), and data permanence (records that outlast the moment) convert episodic stress into embedded pressure.
5. **Anxiety is redistributed rather than removed.** Students may face less one-shot test stress alongside greater cognitive load and "invisible anxiety", while teachers confront continuous monitoring and constrained autonomy.
6. **Parents and administrators are drawn into continuous oversight.** Real-time analytics move [[parents-and-families|parents]] to ongoing monitoring and expectation escalation, while [[administrator|administrators]] gain predictive tools alongside [[cognitive-offloading|overreliance]] risk.
7. **The model is proposed, not tested.** The paper advances constructs and mechanisms but measures none, and calls for longitudinal, comparative, and quasi-experimental research to validate them.

## From Episodic Testing to Continuous Evaluation

Mulvenon et al. (2005) surveyed students, parents, teachers, principals, and counselors, finding those closest to the test were not always the most anxious. The reinterpretation keeps that lens but argues the conditions changed: where [[assessment]] was once episodic [[summative-assessment|high-stakes testing]] in discrete events, AI-mediated environments distribute it across time as continuous, data-rich interaction. Table 1 contrasts the regimes — human judgment against algorithmic evaluation, delayed against immediate [[feedback]], point-in-time snapshots against longitudinal high-frequency data. The shift, the paper argues, produces "invisible anxiety": a diffuse, ongoing performance vigilance tied to no identifiable event and rarely reported in conventional terms. The evidence is literature-based: [[learning-analytics]] and [[feedback]] research suggesting frequent [[formative-assessment|formative evaluation]] normalizes assessment while sustaining cognitive demand — what the paper calls evaluation fatigue.

## Algorithmic Accountability as a New Pressure System

Algorithmic accountability here means the growing reliance on automated systems to evaluate performance, inform decisions, and enforce standards, plus the expectation that they stay transparent, fair, and aligned with educational goals. The argument is not that algorithms are uniquely harmful but that they relocate judgment: evaluation is increasingly co-mediated by [[human-in-the-loop-ai|automated processes]] that shape what is measured and how performance is read. Where traditional assessment located stress in visible events, AI-mediated systems make pressure structural — embedded in [[visualization|dashboards]], [[learning-analytics|analytics]], and [[student-modeling|performance profiles]]. Many models remain "black boxes", so transparency and [[explainable-ai|explainability]] become accountability problems. Quantification privileges what is easily measured over what matters educationally: [[creativity]] and [[critical-thinking]] can be marginalized, [[bias-mitigation|biased]] data can reinforce inequalities, and [[privacy]] becomes a question of [[governance]].

## The AI-Mediated Multi-Stakeholder Anxiety Model

The model extends the [[theories-and-frameworks|original framework]] with three components. Stakeholders retains students, teachers, parents, and [[administrator|administrators]] but reconceives their roles: students work with [[adaptive-learning|adaptive platforms]], teachers interpret [[learning-analytics|performance data]], parents follow analytics dashboards, and administrators feed [[student-modeling|predictive models]] into decisions. AI systems — adaptive testing, real-time [[feedback]], predictive analytics, and automated evaluation — functions not as tooling but as a mediating structure shaping what is measured. Pressure mechanisms names immediacy, visibility, and data permanence as the pathways turning episodic stress into continuous pressure. As [[theory-development-aied|theory development]] the model is deliberately schematic: it specifies constructs and leaves them to be operationalized and tested, reporting no measurement model and no data.

## Reframing Anxiety Across Stakeholders

Applied to each group, the reframing is uneven. Students may see acute test anxiety fall as evaluation spreads across low-stakes interactions, yet carry more sustained cognitive load and less visible vigilance; the paper ties this to a blurred boundary between learning and testing and to the [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulation]] demands continuous feedback imposes. Teachers move from periodic evaluation to continuous data-informed monitoring, with [[teacher-ai-competency|data interpretation]] added to their work and discretion potentially constrained when algorithmic suggestions act as implicit directives — the broader [[teacher-role|teacher role]] shift. [[parents-and-families|Parents]] gain real-time access and may escalate expectations; [[administrator|Administrators]] gain predictive tools but face [[governance|accountability governance]] pressure and classification error from models trained on limited data.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Build deliberate "off-grid" moments into AI-supported courses, where students reflect and experiment without immediate evaluation, because the paper argues continuous assessment sustains invisible anxiety even as acute test anxiety falls.
- **Instructors.** Treat feedback timing as a design decision, not a default: the paper recommends strategic delays and [[self-assessment]] over assuming immediacy always helps.
- **Assessment professionals.** Audit what continuous data systems measure and keep human judgment in high-stakes calls about grading, placement, and teacher evaluation, because quantification privileges easily measured constructs over [[creativity]] and [[critical-thinking]].
- **Administrators.** When predictive models inform intervention, require transparency about data quality and monitor for overreliance and misclassification, the institutional risks named alongside [[privacy]] and [[educational-policy-ai|data-use policy]].

## Limitations

- The paper is a conceptual argument with no new empirical data: it proposes the AI-Mediated Multi-Stakeholder Anxiety Model but measures none of its constructs, so its core claims remain untested.
- The 2005 framework is extended, not tested — the manuscript reinterprets Mulvenon et al.'s original findings against recent literature rather than collecting new evidence, so its redistribution claims are argued, not demonstrated.
- No tool vintage is specified: the systems it cites are generic categories (adaptive testing, real-time feedback, predictive analytics), not named [[edtech-platform|platforms]] or dated model versions, so its claims are tied to no inspectable system.
- The proposed mechanisms — immediacy, visibility, and data permanence — remain inferential, and validating them, the paper states, requires longitudinal, comparative, and quasi-experimental studies it does not conduct.
