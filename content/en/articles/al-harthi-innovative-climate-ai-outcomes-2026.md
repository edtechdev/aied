> **Synthesis:** Al-Harthi, Al-Mahdy, Al-Alawi and Elwakil ask whether an innovation-supportive academic climate reaches pre-service teachers' perceived benefits from [[generative-ai|AI-supported learning]] directly, or whether it operates through their general academic engagement. Drawing on Study Demands–Resources Theory, they surveyed 540 fourth- and fifth-year pre-service teachers at one Omani university and fitted a covariance-based structural equation model linking perceived innovative climate, academic engagement, and five self-reported AI-supported learning outcomes. Innovative climate was positively associated with engagement, and engagement with perceived outcomes; the direct path from climate to outcomes became nonsignificant once engagement entered the model, while the indirect path through engagement remained significant and the total association stayed positive. The data are cross-sectional and entirely self-reported, the three constructs were measured with adapted short scales at one time point, and the structural model was just identified, so the result identifies a statistical mediation pattern rather than a demonstrated causal chain.

## Key Findings

- **Climate connects to engagement.** Pre-service teachers who perceived a more innovation-supportive program reported higher general academic engagement, and engagement in turn was associated with stronger perceived AI-supported learning outcomes.
- **The direct path dissolved.** The total association between perceived innovative climate and perceived AI-supported outcomes was positive and significant, but the direct association was not significant once engagement was included; the authors read this as indirect-only statistical mediation.
- **A substantial share of variance.** The model explained about 36% of the variance in academic engagement and about 22% of the variance in perceived AI-supported learning outcomes.
- **Perceived outcomes ran high.** Perceived AI-supported learning outcomes recorded the highest average of the three constructs, ahead of innovative climate and academic engagement.
- **The measurement held up.** Confirmatory factor analysis supported the three-factor model, loadings, reliability, and convergent and discriminant validity were satisfactory, and a robust-estimator sensitivity analysis left the findings unchanged.
- **General constructs, not AI-specific ones.** Engagement was measured as vigor, dedication, and absorption in academic work generally, and the climate items concern overall program innovativeness, so neither scale directly measures AI-specific experience.

## Design, instruments and analytical model

The study used a cross-sectional quantitative survey of fourth- and fifth-year pre-service teachers in the College of Education at Sultan Qaboos University, Oman. The target population was 897 students; 540 responded, a response rate of 60.2%, with 281 fourth-year (52.0%) and 259 fifth-year (48.0%) students, 210 men (38.9%) and 330 women (61.1%). Specializations were Arabic language and Islamic studies (167, 30.9%), English language education (74, 13.7%), science and mathematics (157, 29.1%), and social studies (142, 26.3%). Respondent distributions by sex and year closely matched the official cohort, although the authors note that this does not exclude nonresponse bias on unobserved characteristics.

Three five-point measures were translated into Arabic by back-translation and adapted to [[teacher-education|teacher education]]. Perceived innovative climate used six items adapted from the Open Innovation Climate Measure; general academic engagement used three items adapted from the ultra-short Utrecht Work Engagement Scale, covering vigor, dedication, and absorption; perceived AI-supported learning outcomes used five items adapted from Jeilani and Abubakar, addressing academic performance, information retention, completion efficiency, critical thinking and problem solving, and the learning experience. No expert-panel review or pilot study was conducted. Confirmatory factor analysis gave a good fit; standardized loadings ranged from 0.610 to 0.831, Cronbach's alpha from 0.785 to 0.877, composite reliability from 0.791 to 0.879, and average variance extracted from 0.548 to 0.559. Discriminant validity held under both the Fornell-Larcker criterion and heterotrait-monotrait ratios of 0.365 to 0.602. Harman's single-factor test and a one-factor confirmatory model argued against a dominant single factor. Because multivariate normality failed, the authors used maximum likelihood estimation with 5000 bias-corrected bootstrap resamples and repeated the analysis with a robust estimator.

## What the structural model showed

Average scores were highest for perceived AI-supported learning outcomes (3.881), followed by innovative climate (3.494) and academic engagement (3.407). Latent correlations were positive throughout: innovative climate with engagement (0.599) and with perceived outcomes (0.355), and engagement with perceived outcomes (0.462). The path from innovative climate to engagement was strong and significant, as was the path from engagement to perceived outcomes. The remaining direct path from climate to outcomes was positive but not significant once engagement was in the model, and its confidence interval came close to zero on the negative side. The indirect path through engagement was significant, as was the total association, giving the indirect-only pattern the authors report. The model explained 35.8% of the variance in engagement and 22.3% of the variance in perceived outcomes. The robust sensitivity analysis produced the same coefficients; its scaled approximate fit indices were favorable.

The authors interpret the pattern through Study Demands–Resources Theory: an innovation-supportive climate acts as a contextual resource, engagement is the [[motivation|motivational mechanism]] that converts it into [[learning-gains|learning benefits]], and the outcome is what students attribute to AI rather than verified achievement. They position the work as extending innovation-climate research beyond innovative behavior toward [[student-engagement|engagement]] as an outcome-bearing mechanism, while conceding that related studies usually measure engagement within AI tasks rather than general academic engagement.

## Interpretation, and what it cannot settle

The authors argue that perceived AI-supported benefits are embedded in the interaction between contextual resources and students' active investment, not in technological access alone. That reading is consistent with their data, but the design is cross-sectional, so the temporal order of climate, engagement and perceived outcomes is assumed rather than observed. All three constructs came from one [[self-report-measures|self-report questionnaire]], which leaves common method bias and socially desirable responding as live alternatives even though Harman's test and the one-factor model did not support a dominant single factor. The sample is one college in Oman, the engagement scale indexes general academic work rather than engagement during AI tasks, and the climate scale measures overall program innovativeness rather than AI-specific institutional support. Prior AI experience was not independently verified, so responses may blend experienced and anticipated benefits, and the authors advise treating the outcome measure as perceived or expected benefit rather than demonstrated improvement.

For practice, the implications are program-level: encourage experimentation, openness to new ideas, knowledge sharing and supportive learning experiences; create opportunities for active participation; and have pre-service teachers critically evaluate AI output and use tools purposefully. The authors also situate the study in a wider push for [[ai-literacy]] and [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulated learning]] while maintaining critical reflection on AI outputs and professional agency.

## What this means for practice

- **Teacher educators.** Build experimentation, openness to ideas, and knowledge sharing into the program climate; the climate-to-engagement path was the strongest in the model.
- **Faculty developers.** Design activities that require active participation with AI rather than access alone, since engagement carried the association to perceived benefits.
- **Instructional designers.** Have pre-service teachers critically evaluate AI-generated content and use tools purposefully, because procedural or passive use is linked elsewhere to lower-level gains.
- **Researchers.** Document actual AI-use experience and verify [[learning-gains|learning gains]] with performance measures; perceived benefits are not demonstrated achievement.
- **Administrators.** Invest in supportive academic environments, but treat the mediation pattern as associative until longitudinal and multi-institution studies test it.

## Limitations

- The cross-sectional design cannot establish temporal ordering or causality, so the mediation pattern is statistical rather than longitudinal.
- All three constructs were collected through one self-report questionnaire at a single time point, leaving common method bias and socially desirable responding possible.
- Fourth- and fifth-year students at one Omani university limit generalization to other programs, institutional structures, and national contexts.
- Engagement was general vigor, dedication, and absorption, and the climate measure concerns overall program innovativeness rather than AI-specific support; prior AI experience was not verified, so responses may mix experienced and anticipated benefits.
- The adapted measures lacked independent expert review and pilot testing, and multivariate non-normality, while addressed by a robust estimator, means global-fit statistics rely on that correction.
