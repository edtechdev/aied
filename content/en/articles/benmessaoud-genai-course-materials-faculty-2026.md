> **Synthesis:** BenMessaoud, Brewer, Buchenot, Jones, Longtin and Scherzinger (2026) surveyed 56 instructors at a large public Midwestern research university and coded the 16 syllabi, policy statements and assignments those instructors volunteered, to see how institutional guidance on [[generative-ai|generative AI]] actually reaches students. The faculty are neither enthusiastic nor hostile. 62% (n = 35) use GenAI at least weekly, 66% (n = 37) call student use ethical "in certain scenarios" when no rule is given, and 77% (n = 43) say it supports learning outcomes in certain scenarios. The course documents are thinner than the attitudes: a permission and acknowledgement statement is robust in 59% of them, 12 of 16 say nothing about [[privacy|data privacy]], and one mentions the university's own data policy, even though data management is the one thing the institution's binding policy regulates. The authors read the pattern as a [[educational-policy-ai|policy]] failure rather than a faculty one, since 71% of respondents say institutional guidelines shaped their course materials "none" or "a little", and they close with a call for clearer frameworks and faculty development rather than stricter enforcement.

## Key Findings

- **Use is widespread and mostly administrative.** 62% (n = 35) reported using GenAI at least weekly, 7 of them (12%) on most days of the week, and 38% (n = 21) never; the most common context was administrative work (n = 26), ahead of teaching (n = 25) and research (n = 18).
- **The institution's guidance barely reaches the syllabus.** 71% said the guidelines shaped their course materials "none" (n = 22) or "a little" (n = 18); satisfaction was mostly indifference, with 52% (n = 29) neither satisfied nor dissatisfied, 20% (n = 11) somewhat dissatisfied and 11% (n = 6) extremely dissatisfied.
- **Ethical judgment is conditional rather than permissive.** Asked about a scenario in which no explicit rules are given, 66% (n = 37) said student use is ethical "in certain scenarios", while 16% (n = 9) answered yes, 13% (n = 7) no and 5% (n = 3) that they were not sure.
- **The same conditional answer holds for learning.** 77% (n = 43) said GenAI supports learning outcomes "in certain scenarios", with 9% (n = 5) answering yes and 9% (n = 5) no, so the modal instructor is reserving judgment rather than endorsing or banning.
- **A permission statement is the one element most documents carry.** Of the 16 artifacts, 8 (59%) stated acceptable use robustly, 4 (25%) in limited form and 4 (25%) not at all, which is the permission and acknowledgement disclaimer the authors' rubric was built to find.
- **Data privacy is the largest gap and the area policy covers most directly.** 12 of 16 documents (75%) said nothing about GenAI and data privacy and 4 (25%) touched it only in limited form; a single document mentioned the university's data privacy policy.
- **Citation rules, ethics discussion and revision clauses are sparse.** Citation guidance was robust in 3 documents (19%) and limited in 6 (38%); discussion of AI ethics was absent from 7 (44%); and 13 of 16 (81%) contained no suggestion that a course policy might need revising.
- **The institutional fix is described as unfinished.** A [[governance|faculty-governance]] task force convened in spring 2024 recommended an ethics framework, a research data policy, and revisions to the student code of conduct, syllabi and promotion guidelines, and the authors note that only some of those recommendations have been implemented.

## How the study was done

The design is explicitly [[mixed-methods-research|mixed methods]]: a Qualtrics survey of instructors of record at one large public Midwestern research university, combined with a content analysis of course documents. Recruitment ran from September 2023 to May 2025 through email, the learning management system, newsletters, faculty governance bodies and faculty networks, using convenience and snowball sampling, and the invitation promised about 10 minutes of work. 56 responses were collected; 47 answered the demographic items, which were mostly White (35, 74%), evenly split between men and women (22 each), and concentrated in liberal arts (16, 34%), medicine (9, 19%) and business (8, 17%), with 30 (64%) on the tenure track.

Participants were asked to upload between one and four documents from the 2023–2024 and 2024–2025 academic years that addressed GenAI, and 16 artifacts arrived: nine syllabi, six policy statements and one assignment. An image of a room was also uploaded and excluded. The authors built a rubric from the university's data privacy and GenAI statements, its teaching guidance and Hodgson's (2023) ethics-of-practice document, trained on a pilot round, and then coded every document independently across eight themes — acceptable use, repetition of policy, ethics, the possibility of revision, plagiarism, citation rules, data privacy broadly, and the university's own data policy. Each theme was marked present robust, present limited or completely absent, with disagreements resolved by discussion and reliability reported as percentage agreement rather than a statistic, so no agreement figure appears in the paper.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors:** write the AI statement as a course-specific permission and acknowledgement clause instead of a link to the university page. The documents here show what the alternative produces: a clause that says use is allowed or not allowed, and silence on citation, [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]] and what happens to student work once it is uploaded.
- **Faculty developers:** run the session as a syllabus workshop, not a policy briefing. The gap between 71% saying guidance shaped their materials "none" or "a little" and 52% being neither satisfied nor dissatisfied suggests instructors are not refusing the guidance so much as finding nothing in it they can paste into a week-one document.
- **Administrators:** a binding data-security rule is not teaching guidance. If the only enforceable policy sorts data into public, university-internal, restricted and critical categories and lists which tools may receive them, expect a syllabus that is silent on both ethics and citation — which is where 7 of 16 documents landed on ethics and 7 of 16 on citation.
- **Instructional designers:** put the privacy paragraph in the template. The one gap that is both the institution's stated priority and almost entirely absent from course materials is the one a template can close without requiring an instructor to become an expert in vendor data practices.

## Limitations

- One large public Midwestern research university supplies every participant, and the authors say the policies, resources and attitudes at other institutions, especially private ones, may not resemble it.
- The sample underrepresents education and allied health, the two fields the authors say are likely to engage GenAI in unique ways through professional ethics, clinical simulation and pedagogical innovation.
- The survey was voluntary, so instructors who were comfortable with GenAI were likelier to answer, and colleagues who feared institutional consequences may have stayed away, which the authors name as selection bias.
- Only 16 documents arrived from 56 respondents, and the paper does not report how many instructors uploaded them, so the document findings describe a small, self-selected subset rather than the institution's syllabi.
- The authors concede that themes missing from the documents may exist in presentations and in-class activities that were never collected, and that the technology is moving faster than the policy cycle the paper is asking institutions to shorten.
