> **Synthesis:** Fidalgo and Hashem (2026) argue that responsible generative-AI feedback depends less on technical access than on faculty pedagogical judgment and students' capacity to judge the feedback itself. They distinguish students' [[evaluative-judgment|evaluative judgment]] of feedback quality from [[metacognition|metacognitive calibration]], which requires comparing confidence and self-judgment with independently evaluated performance. The paper proposes a conceptual design cycle in which students record an initial judgment and response, check confidence, treat AI feedback as one source among others, seek human mediation when needed, revise and document their decisions, and later recalibrate against independent evidence. It links the cycle to assessment integrity, institutional policy and faculty development. The article reports no empirical data: it offers a vocabulary and a proposed sequence, and it explicitly leaves empirical testing to future work.

## Key Findings
1. **Feedback access is not the same as learning.** Detailed AI comments can leave students unsure what to change or why, and can make a product look stronger than the underlying understanding.
2. **Faculty readiness is primarily pedagogical.** It combines technical understanding with task design, feedback literacy, evaluative judgment, ethical awareness, assessment competence and knowledge of institutional policy.
3. **Students must judge the feedback itself.** Generative AI can sound confident while being inaccurate, generic or weakly aligned with criteria, so acceptance, questioning, rejection and further investigation are all legitimate responses.
4. **Calibration needs an independent criterion.** Confidence and self-judgment must be compared with lecturer-assessed work, validated answers, later unaided performance or another defensible standard, not with the AI output.
5. **Human mediation is selective, not constant.** Checkpoints, sample reviews, peer discussion, short reflections and decision logs keep judgment in the process without requiring lecturers to review every student interaction.
6. **Integrity means explaining decisions.** A disclosure of whether AI was used is not enough; students should be able to say which suggestions they accepted or rejected and what they take responsibility for.
7. **The cycle remains a proposal.** The authors state that whether structured AI feedback improves calibration is a conceptual claim requiring empirical testing.

## The vocabulary behind the proposal

The paper defines its terms carefully. AI feedback here means comments, explanations, suggestions, alternative wording and guidance generated or supported by generative AI in response to student work; rule-based correction, predictive dashboards and adaptive tutoring are outside the focus. [[feedback|Feedback]] supports learning only when students understand it, judge its relevance and use it to decide what to do next.

Evaluative judgment is the capacity to make informed judgments about the quality of work and, in this setting, about the credibility and usefulness of the feedback. It is related to feedback literacy but not identical: feedback literacy concerns how students understand and use feedback, while calibration concerns how accurately their self-judgments align with independently evaluated performance. The authors warn against confusing AI feedback with the evidence used to assess calibration. AI comments may inform revision; they are not the standard against which accuracy is judged.

Faculty readiness is described as a set of connected capacities. Lecturers need to decide when AI feedback is appropriate, what limits apply, what students should produce before receiving it, what evidence of learning should be visible and what students should do afterwards. These decisions are framed as pedagogical, ethical and institutional at once, because AI feedback touches assessment design, authorship, transparency, fairness and academic integrity.

## The proposed design cycle in concrete terms

The authors say the cycle follows seven stages, operationalized in a table linking each stage to student action, lecturer role, evidence, criterion and intended outcome. It begins with initial judgment about the task, criteria and knowledge being assessed, then an initial response produced before AI support where independent production matters. A confidence check records quality or completeness judgments before external feedback replaces them. AI feedback enters as one source, to be annotated, questioned or rejected against task goals, course criteria and disciplinary standards.

Human mediation follows when feedback conflicts with the criteria, the task is high stakes, the comments are generic or inaccurate, or students cannot justify their choices. It can be built from checkpoints, peer discussion, sample reviews and targeted support rather than exhaustive teacher review. The next stage combines revision, documentation and responsibility: students explain what changed, how AI feedback shaped the decision and what remains their own. The final stage is recalibration over time, revisiting earlier confidence after feedback and revision and comparing later judgments with independently evaluated performance such as later assessed work, an unaided task or a transfer task.

The discussion extends the proposal to academic integrity, university policy and faculty development. Detection and penalties are treated as insufficient because a submission can improve while judgment does not. Policies should connect AI feedback with assessment integrity and lecturer judgment; development should move beyond short tool workshops toward communities of practice, assessment clinics, peer review of AI-integrated tasks and discipline-specific cases. The authors also name wider responsible-AI issues — privacy, platform governance, bias, accessibility, equity, intellectual property and commercial use of student work — as conditions the paper does not resolve.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Ask students to record a confidence judgment and a short draft before they use AI feedback, so there is something independent to compare against later evidence.
- **Instructors.** Treat AI comments as provisional: have students mark which suggestions they accept, question or reject and require a reason tied to the task criteria.
- **Instructors.** Build human mediation through checkpoints and peer discussion rather than attempting to review every AI interaction, especially in large classes.
- **Faculty developers.** Use discipline-specific cases and assessment clinics to help lecturers decide where AI feedback belongs in a task and when it should be withheld.
- **Institutions.** Write policies that make thinking visible, support disclosure with explanation, and protect the lecturer's role in academic judgment.

## Limitations

- **No empirical data.** The article reports no sample, instrument, participants, outcomes or measured effect; it presents a conceptual cycle and calls for testing in real teaching contexts.
- **Unproven mechanism.** Whether the cycle improves confidence–performance calibration, feedback uptake, revision depth, error detection, later unaided performance, retention, transfer or lecturer workload is proposed, not demonstrated.
- **No comparative test.** Structured AI-feedback activities are not compared empirically with open-ended AI use, so claims about reducing uncritical acceptance remain conjectures.
- **Partial scope.** The focus on responsible pedagogical use leaves privacy, platform governance, bias, accessibility, equity, intellectual property and commercial reuse of student work to other work.
- **Unknown disciplinary fit.** The authors ask for tests across disciplines, assessment types and levels of student expertise; the paper itself provides no such evidence.
