> **Synthesis:** This F1000Research policy brief (version 1, awaiting peer review) argues that [[ai-education|AI in education]] in Sub-Saharan Africa should be judged by its effect on student [[well-being]], [[agency|learner agency]] and resilience rather than by adoption rates or efficiency. Written from an educational psychology perspective by five authors based at Kampala International University and the University of South Africa, it proposes a learner-protection agenda covering age-appropriate safeguards, teacher capacity, [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] in curricula, learner data protection, equitable access, culturally relevant AI systems and student participation in AI governance. It is an advocacy document rather than an empirical study: the paper reports no sample, no data collection and no effect sizes, and states that there is no data associated with the study. Its regional scope is stated throughout, and it names digital inequality, weak regulatory capacity, thin data-protection systems, language and cultural under-representation, and uneven institutional preparation as conditions that intensify the risks in the region. Its central recommendation is a shift from an adoption agenda to a learner-protection and learner-development goal.

## Key Findings

1. **The brief reports no original data; its content is an argued policy agenda, not measured outcomes.** The paper cites no sample, instrument, intervention or effect size, and states that "There is no data associated with this study."
2. **Well-being, agency and resilience are proposed as the constructs for evaluating educational AI.** Well-being is defined through psychological safety, belonging, emotional health and supportive learning relationships; agency through autonomy, critical judgment, voice, informed decision-making and [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulated learning]]; resilience through learners' capacity to adapt to technological disruption, misinformation, academic pressures and shifting employment environments.
3. **Student well-being should become a standard criterion for evaluating AI systems in education.** Ministries, institutions and providers should examine AI's influence on anxiety, academic pressure, motivation, self-esteem, attention, social interaction and school belonging, and include psychosocial indicators in routine assessment of effectiveness.
4. **AI literacy is framed as ethical, psychological and social, not merely technical.** Learners should understand how AI systems generate information and where their limits lie, misinformation and algorithmic bias, attribution of AI-assisted work, privacy and responsible use.
5. **High-stakes academic and developmental decisions must keep meaningful human judgment.** The brief calls for human oversight, transparency and appeal processes for admissions, progression, discipline and psychological profiling, and warns against replacing independent reasoning with AI-generated replies.
6. **Equity and cultural representation are treated as preconditions, not add-ons.** Public investment should prioritize rural and low-income populations, refugees, learners with impairments and girls; equity impact assessments should precede large-scale adoption; and procurement should weigh language inclusivity, cultural relevance and evidence of algorithmic fairness.

## From technology adoption to learner protection

The brief is organized as an introduction, policy implications, recommendations and conclusions. Its stated question is not whether African schools, colleges and learners use AI at all, but whether they use it in ways that foster human development. The authors are explicit that they are not calling for rejecting AI or limiting useful innovation; they argue for educational contexts in which AI assists human teaching, amplifies rather than replaces autonomous reasoning, protects psychological and informational safety, and extends learner agency.

The agenda is anchored in existing continental policy: the African Union Continental Artificial Intelligence Strategy, adopted in 2024, and the Continental Education Strategy for Africa 2026 to 2035. It also cites UNICEF guidance that AI systems affecting children must protect privacy and safety, ensure fairness and inclusion, promote development and well-being, be transparent and accountable, and act in the child's best interests. It notes that many AI tools used by African learners are built outside the continent and trained mostly on data, languages and cultural contexts that may not represent African societies, raising questions about algorithmic bias, linguistic exclusion, data ownership and whose knowledge is valued.

## Age-differentiated safeguards and the teacher relationship

Two design commitments run through the policy implications. The first is differentiation by developmental stage: children and teenagers differ from adults in cognitive maturity, emotional regulation and vulnerability to digital influence, so education authorities should develop separate recommendations for primary, secondary and postsecondary education covering AI-assisted learning, [[assessment|assessment]], [[automated-assessment|automated feedback]], data gathering and [[conversational-ai|conversational systems]]. Particular care is urged where AI provides emotional companionship, counselling-type help or authoritative guidance. The second is that teacher and student relationships stay central: automated teaching, feedback and assessment should supplement rather than replace human engagement, and AI should mainly reduce administrative workload, support differentiated instruction and strengthen feedback while teachers retain professional judgment. Teacher education should combine AI literacy, digital pedagogy and educational psychology.

## Equity, data protection and assessment reform

Equity is handled as a governance requirement rather than a distribution problem. Access to energy, internet connectivity, digital devices, AI platforms and digitally equipped teachers is unequal, which the brief says could worsen existing inequities; it calls for publicly subsidized or institutionally licensed AI platforms so that quality AI-assisted learning does not depend on household income. On data, regulations should specify what learner data can be collected, for how long, by whom, and whether it can be used commercially. On assessment, the brief urges realistic, oral, practical, reflective and [[process-oriented-assessment|process-based forms]] that reveal real student knowledge, with learners able to question significant AI-supported decisions. Resilience is proposed as an explicit educational goal built on self-regulation, adaptability, critical thinking, digital citizenship, problem-solving and help-seeking.

## What this means for practice

- **Policymakers.** Define appropriate AI use separately for primary, secondary and postsecondary education in national guidelines, and reserve admissions, progression, discipline and profiling decisions for human judgment with an appeal route.
- **Administrators.** Adopt explicit institutional policies naming permissible, limited and prohibited uses of [[generative-ai|generative AI]] and stating when disclosure of AI assistance is required.
- **Instructors.** Design assessments toward oral, practical, reflective and process-based evidence, and pair AI feedback with your own so that it supplements rather than substitutes for teaching contact.
- **Administrators.** Add motivation, anxiety, academic stress, social interaction and technology dependence to routine monitoring of any AI deployment, with counselling and psychosocial support funded alongside it.
- **Policymakers and institutions.** Require equity impact assessments before large-scale adoption and build learner consultation into governance, since the brief argues legitimacy depends on responsiveness to learners' lived experience.

## Limitations

- The paper is a policy brief with no primary data, no pilot, no implementation study and no reported effect sizes: every recommendation is an argued position rather than a tested intervention. The brief concedes that children in the Global South are under-represented in both research and decision-making about AI systems.
- It is published as version 1 with the status awaiting peer review, and the DOI resolves to that version of record, so no reviewer reports or post-review revisions exist yet.
- The scope is explicitly Sub-Saharan Africa and the brief does not claim transferability elsewhere; nor does it report whether the continental strategies and UNICEF guidance it cites have been implemented.
- The recommendations name psychosocial constructs to monitor but give no measurement instruments, indicators, thresholds, cost estimates or timelines.
