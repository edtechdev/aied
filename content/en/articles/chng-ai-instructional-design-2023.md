> **Synthesis:** Ch'ng (2023) argues, conceptually rather than empirically, that [[generative-ai|generative AI]] should be folded into every phase of the ADDIE [[learning-design|instructional design]] cycle instead of being bolted on at the production stage. Working through analysis, design and development, implementation and evaluation, the paper pairs each phase with a class of tools — text generators, image generators, video and voice synthesis, presentation builders, question banks — and contrasts what a designer does by hand with what a designer does alongside a machine. Its organizing distinction is assisted versus autonomous intelligence: tools that speed up human decisions now, and systems that make those decisions later. From that it predicts new roles, including the [[prompt-engineering|prompt engineer]], and a shift in the subject matter expert's job from writing content to validating it. It closes by arguing that assessment should move from recall toward [[authentic-assessment|authentic performance]], and that [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] rather than prohibition is the realistic institutional response.

*No primary data: this is a conceptual contribution.*

## Key Findings

- **ADDIE is the organizing frame, and AI enters phase by phase.** The paper takes the analysis, design, development, implementation and evaluation cycle as given and asks what changes in each. It notes that the models still in use emerged in the 1980s — ADDIE, Dick and Carey, Gagné's nine events, Gerlach and Ely, rapid instructional design — and that each has been reshaped by earlier technology waves, so AI is treated as the next such wave rather than a break with the field.
- **Analysis becomes continuous and multi-group.** Manual data collection, surveys, interviews and contextual reading are described as the costly part of the phase. AI is claimed to automate data gathering and analysis, which the author says speeds the work, improves accuracy and lowers resource demands; [[human-ai-collaboration|assisted intelligence]] also lets analysis cover many user groups rather than one target audience, which the paper treats as the precondition for adaptive design.
- **Design and development is where the paper is most concrete.** A comparison table sets current practice against AI-collaborative practice for content, images, video, audio, presentation decks, assessment and question banks. Content moves from subject matter expert drafting to generation with ChatGPT, Jasper, Writesonic or Cowriter plus grammar tools such as Grammarly, Quillbot and ProWritingAid; images move from licensed or commissioned artwork to Midjourney, Adobe Firefly, Stable Diffusion or Craiyon; video from studio production to Synthesia, D-ID, Fliki or Peech; audio from voice talent and studio time to Murf, Play.ht, Resemble AI or Descript.
- **The savings are asserted as production facts.** Video creation is said to drop below 15 minutes once the content is ready, a voice clip to "a matter of seconds," and automatic captions to a minor edit because accuracy does not reach 100 percent. None of these figures comes from a timed comparison; they are the author's summary of what the tools advertise.
- **Copyright is flagged, not resolved.** The paper cites the U.S. Copyright Office position that AI-generated images are not eligible for protection because they are not the work of human authorship, then leaves the licensing question open. It is the one place where [[legal-issues-and-risks|legal risk]] is named rather than assumed away.
- **Two kinds of intelligence, two time horizons.** Following a PwC report, the paper separates assisted intelligence, which helps a person perform a task faster or better, from autonomous intelligence, which automates decision-making without human intervention. Assisted use is presented as the shorter-term gain across ADDIE; autonomous use as the longer-term direction, especially where large models train and learn independently.
- **The division of labor is complementary in principle.** Machines are assigned data processing, computation and real-time analysis; humans are assigned creativity, critical thinking, emotional intelligence and contextual awareness. The paper borrows the human–robot collaboration framing to argue that the pairing should maximize each side's strengths, and it concedes that research on what the human role actually becomes in AI-specific design contexts is still missing.
- **New roles, and a changed job for the subject matter expert.** Four roles are proposed: AI content strategist, content accessor, learning experience designer and AI technology specialist, with the prompt engineer added later as the person who translates instructional needs into prompts a model can act on. The sharpest claim is that the expert stops being a content creator and becomes a content accessor whose validation prevents "garbage in, garbage out" — a quality-control job rather than a writing job.
- **The institutional response is criticized as inconsistent.** The paper lists universities that banned ChatGPT after its release — the University of Hong Kong, Cambridge, Sciences Po, RV University — and asks why Grammarly and Quillbot were left alone. Its own answer is that institutions were not ready and bought time; its recommendation is to teach ethical use and disclosure instead of prohibition. [[williams-coding-curriculum-critique-2026|Williams and Prishtina's (2026) critique]] takes up this argument about how AI is framed in curriculum work.
- **Evaluation should shift from recall to evidence-based performance.** The paper links a move away from memory-based testing to [[constructivist|constructivist]] design, [[authentic-assessment|authentic assessment]] and Knowles's andragogy, arguing that higher education should treat adult learners as self-directed rather than child-like recipients. [[self-directed-learning|Self-directed learning]] is presented as the disposition both students and instructors now need.

## How the study was done

There is no study. The paper is a position piece: it advances an argument about how instructional design practice should absorb AI, organizes that argument around ADDIE, and illustrates it with a table of tools and a set of predicted role changes. The evidence base is the cited literature plus vendor capability claims, not collected data. The author states that no living entities were involved and that ethics review was therefore not applicable, which is accurate for a paper of this kind.

The method is best described as theoretical analysis in the service of practice recommendations. ADDIE supplies the structure, a comparison table supplies the concrete detail, and the closing sections draw implications for designers, institutions and training. No search strategy or inclusion criteria are reported, so the tool lists are illustrative examples rather than a survey.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructional designers.** The phase-by-phase map is usable as an adoption checklist even without the evidence behind it: decide, for each of analysis, design, development, implementation and evaluation, which decisions stay with a person and which can be delegated. The paper's own answer is that assisted use is the near-term move and autonomous use the thing to plan for.
- **Media producers.** Treat generated images, video and voice as drafts with unresolved rights. The copyright paragraph is short but consequential: if AI images carry no human authorship, a course built on them has no protectable asset, and the paper offers no license strategy to replace the one it removes.
- **Subject matter experts, team leads and faculty developers.** The content accessor reframing is the practical claim worth testing. If experts spend their time validating rather than drafting, review capacity becomes the bottleneck, and the paper's own quality argument — that only a person can confirm accuracy, relevance and fit — implies review has to be resourced, not assumed. The paper also notes that higher education subject experts often lack formal instructional design training even though they build the materials, so if AI is added to that workflow, [[professional-training|professional training]] has to cover design fundamentals and tool use together, or it will teach prompting to people who cannot yet judge whether the output is well designed.
- **Assessment designers.** The paper's question bank example shows where [[automated-assessment|automated assessment]] becomes attractive: large item pools, difficulty levels, and different question sets per student. Its evaluation argument pushes the other way, toward performance tasks that a generator cannot complete on a student's behalf. The two claims sit uneasily together, and readers should choose deliberately rather than adopt both.
- **Institutions writing guidance.** The inconsistency critique is the most transferable point. A rule that bans one generator while permitting grammar and paraphrasing tools is hard to defend to students, and the paper's alternative — explicit teaching of [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]] with AI, plus disclosure — is the position most programs have since moved toward.

## Limitations

- The paper presents no data of any kind. Claims about speed, cost, accuracy and quality are drawn from tool vendors and prior commentary, so they establish what the author believes is possible, not what has been measured.
- The tool inventory is a 2023 snapshot of the ChatGPT release period. Product names, prices and capabilities have moved, and several of the companies listed have changed what they offer.
- ADDIE is accepted as the field's spine without engaging the long-standing critique that linear models fit poorly with iterative, agile or platform-based development. A reader who does not use ADDIE will find the phase structure less useful than the role analysis.
- The copyright discussion is limited to the U.S. position and to images. It does not address text, audio, jurisdiction, institutional licensing or the indemnities some vendors now offer. The paper also does not discuss student data, consent or [[privacy|privacy]] when course material and learner information are uploaded to third-party tools, which is the risk most institutions now treat as central.
- The claim that AI [[equity-in-ai-education|widens access]] to creative work is asserted rather than examined. There is no analysis of cost, connectivity, language coverage or the skills needed to use the tools well, all of which determine whether access actually widens.
- The role predictions are speculative. No employer data, job-posting analysis or practitioner survey supports the claim that AI content strategists and technology specialists are emerging as distinct occupations.
