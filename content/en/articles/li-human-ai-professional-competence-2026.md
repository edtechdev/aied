> **Synthesis:** Li and Torrisi-Steele (2026) introduce Human–AI Professional Competence (HAPC) — the capacity to integrate disciplinary expertise and artificial intelligence through appropriate [[cognitive-offloading|cognitive allocation]], critical [[critical-thinking|verification]], [[experiential-learning|experiential judgment]], and accountable human decision-making — and test it with an explanatory [[mixed-methods-research|mixed-methods]] design: 560 [[learners]] surveyed on six multi-item constructs and 240 higher-education [[teacher-role|faculty]] answering 18 open-ended questions. The results challenge the assumption that more AI use means more competence. Higher-order cognitive autonomy was independently predicted by [[adult-learning|adult learning]] readiness, [[self-regulated-learning|learning autonomy]], [[agency|cognitive agency]] and ownership, and experiential judgment, each significant, while AI cognitive delegation did not independently predict it. Delegation became more compatible with retained capability only when cognitive ownership was stronger. Faculty themes converged: delegate execution, not judgment; treat explainability as evidence of ownership; use experience as a verification resource; and keep [[ethics|professional responsibility]] nondelegable. The study reframes [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] as necessary but insufficient, and argues that [[professional-training|workforce preparation]] should teach calibrated delegation rather than tool proficiency.

*Explanatory mixed-methods study; the 560 [[learners]] and 240 faculty members are independent, complementary samples rather than matched respondents, and the quantitative strand is cross-sectional.*

## Key Findings

- **Delegation alone did not predict competence.** AI cognitive delegation was weakly and negatively related to the other five constructs and did not independently predict cognitive-level autonomy once the human capabilities were included.
- **A configuration of human capabilities predicted higher-order autonomy.** Adult learning readiness, learning autonomy, cognitive agency and ownership, and experiential judgment each made significant independent contributions to the model, which explained 32.2% of variance.
- **Ownership conditions delegation.** The interaction between delegation and cognitive agency and ownership was small but significant, indicating delegation is more compatible with retained higher-order capability when learners keep ownership of the work.
- **Exposure was not competence.** Daily or near-daily generative-AI users reported the highest cognitive agency and delegation, yet their higher-order autonomy was not significantly greater.
- **Faculty drew a functional boundary.** Participants located competence in deciding which evidence matters, interpreting relationships, and defending conclusions, not in whether AI could perform an activity.
- **Ownership requires explainability.** Faculty described ownership as the ability to explain, reconstruct, verify, defend, and transfer AI-assisted work, and treated an inability to answer "why" as a warning sign of cognitive outsourcing.
- **Some cognition stays nondelegable.** Professional responsibility, ethical judgment, contextual interpretation, and consequential decision-making were reserved for humans regardless of AI accuracy.

## From AI literacy to professional competence

The paper's starting point is that [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] is foundational but incomplete for professional work. Knowing how to use AI and evaluate its outputs does not establish whether a person can decide which cognitive activities to delegate, which to keep, how to coordinate human and artificial contributions, when to reject a recommendation, and who remains responsible for the judgment. The authors define HAPC around four elements — cognitive allocation, critical verification, experiential judgment, and accountable human decision-making — and organize it around strategic autonomy, cognitive ownership, experiential judgment, and higher-order cognitive autonomy. Recent evidence supports the distinction: Human–AI Collaboration Competency correlates moderately with AI literacy (r = .53), indicating overlap without redundancy. The workforce backdrop sharpens the stakes — the Future of Jobs Report 2025, based on more than 1,000 employers representing over 14 million workers, reports that 86% of surveyed employers expect AI and information-processing technologies to transform their businesses by 2030, even as analytical thinking remains the most sought-after core skill.

## What the learner data show

Six constructs were measured on a seven-point response metric: Adult Learning Readiness, AI-Supported Learning Autonomy, Cognitive Agency and Ownership, AI Cognitive Delegation, Experiential Judgment, and Cognitive-Level Autonomy, with Cronbach's alpha from .840 to .911. Descriptively, respondents reported relatively high readiness (M = 5.16, SD = 0.97), experiential judgment (M = 5.26, SD = 0.93), agency and ownership (M = 5.08, SD = 0.97), and cognitive-level autonomy (M = 4.97, SD = 0.98), while delegation was lower (M = 4.45, SD = 1.00). Bivariate correlations formed a coherent competence pattern, with the strongest association between agency and ownership and cognitive-level autonomy (r = .447), while delegation was weakly and negatively related to the other constructs (rs = −.074 to −.111). In regression, readiness predicted learning autonomy (β = .412, p < .001, 17.0% of variance), and readiness and autonomy predicted agency and ownership (27.0% of variance) while delegation did not (β = −.038, p = .303). The authors are careful to note that the [[quantitative-research|quantitative]] strand is cross-sectional, so these are associations, not demonstrated developmental progression.

## How faculty draw the boundary

The qualitative strand generated four themes. First, delegate execution, not judgment: faculty accepted AI for information organization, preliminary literature synthesis, data cleaning, descriptive analysis, brainstorming, classification, drafting, and generation of alternatives, but located competence in determining which evidence mattered, interpreting relationships, weighing alternatives, and establishing evaluation criteria. Second, cognitive ownership requires explainability — the ability to explain, reconstruct, verify, defend, and transfer AI-assisted work, with a polished product treated as insufficient evidence. Third, experience becomes a verification resource, letting professionals recognize technically plausible but contextually inappropriate output, though participants also flagged path dependency and overconfidence as liabilities. Fourth, some cognition remains nondelegable: [[ethics|professional responsibility]], ethical judgment, contextual interpretation, consequential decision-making, explanation, and final disciplinary judgment stay with humans because identifiable professionals must remain answerable for them. The analysis was [[qualitative-research|reflexive thematic analysis]] of 240 responses, emphasizing interpretive accountability rather than an intercoder-agreement coefficient.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Teach allocation decisions explicitly — what to do independently, what to delegate, what requires collaboration, what must be verified, and when to override — rather than teaching prompting efficiency alone.
- **Instructional designers.** Build tasks where learners must explain, reconstruct, and defend AI-assisted work, because a polished product was treated as insufficient evidence of competence.
- **Faculty developers.** Address the boundary directly: help instructors distinguish delegable execution from nondelegable judgment, and treat experience as a verification resource rather than an infallible one.
- **Curriculum designers.** Develop AI literacy alongside strategic autonomy, cognitive ownership, verification, experiential judgment, and higher-order cognitive autonomy, and preserve space for essential cognitive activities performed independently.
- **Administrators.** Measure AI readiness through delegation boundaries, verification protocols, override authority, and accountability rather than through frequency of tool use.

## Limitations

- The documentation provides no complete sampling frame or response-rate calculation, so generalization beyond the participating Chinese educational settings should be cautious.
- All quantitative measures are [[self-report-measures|self-reported]], capturing perceived rather than directly demonstrated competence.
- HAPC was not measured as a single construct; Cognitive-Level Autonomy is one measured higher-order outcome, not a proxy for HAPC as a whole.
- Faculty responses are professional perceptions rather than observational evidence of workplace behavior, and the report contains no quotation-level evidence, which limits direct reader appraisal of the themes.
- The quantitative design is cross-sectional, so the proposed model's directional arrows are hypotheses for longitudinal or structural testing rather than established effects.
- The 560 learners and 240 faculty members were complementary rather than matched samples, so integration was conceptual rather than respondent-level.
