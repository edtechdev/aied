> **Synthesis:** Grothe-Hammer, Hammer and Berkel-Otto (2026) ask not whether students use [[conversational-ai|AI chatbots]] but what they actually do with them, and what not using them now means. Drawing on 15 focus groups with 61 [[teacher-education]] students at a German university, and analyzing the transcripts with practice theory and structuration theory, they identify five practices: using the chatbot as a support tool, as a learning facilitator, as sole author (copying generated text), as first author (modifying generated text), and as second author (supplying their own ideas and letting the AI write them up). Non-use emerges as a practice in its own right, and for some students it has become a source of pride and self-validation rather than a deficit. The study's central message is that [[academic-integrity|authorship and originality]] are already negotiated in everyday practice, and that students still need their own academic competencies to judge the accuracy and quality of what the tool returns.

*Exploratory [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] study of 15 focus groups; the five practices are empirically reconstructed divisions of labor between students and chatbots, not legal authorship categories, and the authors state that the typology is a basis for guidelines rather than a prevalence estimate.*

## Key Findings

1. **Five practices of use, not one.** Students use chatbots as a support tool, a learning facilitator, a sole author, a first author, or a second author, and the difference between them is who supplies the thinking.
2. **Co-authorship is common, not just copy-pasting.** The first-author and second-author practices show students supplying ideas, notes, or arguments and letting the tool write them up, which raises the originality question the authors say is still unsettled.
3. **Non-use is a practice with its own meanings.** Some students avoid chatbots because they lack the competencies to use them or fear [[ai-detection|detection]]; others treat non-use as proof of their own ability and a source of pride.
4. **Fear narratives can chill legitimate use.** Narratives about detection and sanctions discourages some students from using chatbots even for uses that may be formally permitted, which the authors read as a chilling effect of perceived surveillance.
5. **Students still need academic competencies.** Participants were aware that chatbots make mistakes, and the authors conclude that students must be able to assess the accuracy and quality of AI-generated output.
6. **Norms are unsettled and ambivalent.** Narratives of widespread normalized use coexist with narratives of stigma and detection within the same groups, a state the authors call normative ambivalence.

## A practice-theoretical account

The paper's framing is sociological rather than cognitive. Using Giddens's structuration theory, the authors treat chatbot use as a practice organized by three dimensions: allocative resources (the technology and what it can produce), authoritative resources (the students' own competencies), and the meaningful and normative dimensions of signification and sanction. The five practices are the empirical result of crossing what the tool supplies with what the student supplies. The framework matters for how the findings should be read: the typology is not a measure of how often each practice occurs but a map of what the practices are and how they relate to the competencies and norms around them. It also explains why non-use belongs in the same analysis — not using the tool is itself a practice shaped by the same resources, narratives, and sanctions.

## Originality, authorship, and the student's own competence

Two findings cut against the simpler stories. First, the sole-author practice (copy-pasting) is only one of five, and co-authorship practices in which the student supplies the substance are common; the originality question is therefore about where the thinking came from, not about whether a tool was opened. Second, students' own academic competencies remain necessary across every practice: they are what allows a student to notice a hallucinated reference, a weak argument, or a misaligned claim. The authors' conclusion is that the continued acquisition of academic competencies is not made redundant by chatbots, and that the ability to evaluate output is the load-bearing skill.

## What this means for practice

- **[[teacher-role|Instructors]].** Write assignment briefs that distinguish the practices explicitly — which of the five uses are permitted for a given task — rather than issuing a blanket ban the typology shows students will interpret unevenly.
- **Instructional designers.** Build the [[ai-ed-evaluation|evaluation of AI]] output into the task itself, since the study finds that students' own judgment of accuracy and quality is what the practices depend on.
- **[[administrator|Administrators]].** Treat [[academic-integrity|academic-integrity]] guidance as an explanation of authorship and originality, not only as a detection regime, because fear narratives alone appear to chill permitted use without clarifying the boundary.
- **Students.** Treat the chatbot's output as something you must be able to defend in your own words, because every practice in the typology ultimately requires that competence.

## Limitations

- The study is a single-site qualitative study of 61 teacher-education students at one German [[higher-ed|university]], so the typology describes this group rather than the [[learners|student population]].
- The authors drew the 15 interviews from a larger dataset of 34 focus groups with 121 students, and the selection rationale is that these interviews postdate the arrival of generative chatbots.
- Focus-group accounts are retrospective and socially situated; the practices are [[self-report-measures|self-reported]] and may not match what students do when unobserved.
- The typology is not a prevalence measure and cannot say how common each practice is.
- The analysis was conducted in German and translated for publication, with no formal back-translation, which the authors note as a constraint on the interpretation of the interview language.
