> **Synthesis:** Bello and Lateef argue that artificial intelligence, open data and open science can widen access to knowledge in higher education, especially where subscription costs, weak infrastructure and language exclusion keep institutions at the edge of scholarly communication. They map AI to discovery, comprehension, learning support and research visibility, then pair each opportunity with a condition: governance, infrastructure, digital literacy and institutional policy determine who actually benefits. Open science contributes reuse, transparency and participation through open access, open data, open peer review, open educational resources, citizen science and repositories. Their framing combines Knowledge Democratisation Theory with Diffusion of Innovation Theory, and their recommendations target universities, governments and libraries. The article reports no empirical data of its own: it is a narrative review of literature published between 2016 and 2026, with no new sample, instrument or measured outcome.

## Key Findings
1. **Knowledge access is defined broadly.** The paper treats access as the ability to find, understand, use, adapt and contribute to knowledge, spanning physical, digital, linguistic, economic, cognitive and social dimensions.
2. **AI is assigned four access roles.** The review describes intelligent discovery, comprehension support, adaptive learning and research visibility, alongside assistive and translation tools.
3. **Open science adds a participation agenda.** Open access, open data, open methods, open peer review and citizen science are presented as ways to make knowledge reusable and verifiable, not merely visible.
4. **The democratizing promise is conditional.** The authors repeatedly warn that unequal capacity, algorithmic bias, privacy risks and commercial infrastructure can turn AI into another marker of institutional inequality.
5. **Libraries are cast as central actors.** Academic libraries are described as repository managers, digital literacy facilitators and hubs for AI-assisted discovery rather than as custodians of books alone.
6. **Governance comes before adoption.** The paper calls for ethical AI policies, data management rules, infrastructure investment, capacity building and international partnerships as prerequisite conditions.

## The vocabulary of the proposal

The review defines open science through cited work: Vicente Saez and Martinez Fuentes (2018) describe transparent and accessible knowledge shared through collaborative networks, while UNESCO (2021) frames it as making scientific knowledge openly available, accessible and reusable while making production more inclusive. Open data rests on the FAIR principles Wilkinson et al. (2016) set out: data should be findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable. The paper extends knowledge accessibility beyond journal articles to datasets, metadata, research instruments, code, educational resources, repositories, multilingual tools and inclusive platforms, including screen reader compatibility, captioning, alternative text and local-language resources for persons with disabilities and multilingual users.

Two theories organize the argument. Knowledge Democratisation Theory treats knowledge as a public good and assumes that barriers are produced by economic, institutional, linguistic and technological systems rather than being natural. Diffusion of Innovation Theory explains uneven adoption through perceived usefulness, compatibility, complexity, trialability, observability, leadership support and policy incentives; the paper cites Menzli et al. (2022) on open educational resource adoption and Jin et al. (2025) on university generative-AI policies. A literature matrix lists the works the review draws on, including Wilkinson et al. (2016), McKiernan et al. (2016), Piwowar et al. (2018), Zawacki Richter et al. (2019), UNESCO (2021), Ouyang et al. (2022), Nguyen et al. (2023), Crompton and Burke (2023), Panke (2024), Wang et al. (2024) and Jin et al. (2025). The authors state that retrieval was not intended to be exhaustive.

## What the review says can be done

On the opportunity side, the paper lists intelligent search systems, semantic indexing, automated translation, adaptive learning platforms, assistive technologies, research recommendation systems, metadata generation, generative summarization and [[learning-analytics|learning analytics]]. Open data and open science are said to strengthen transparency, reproducibility, collaborative research, open access publishing, citizen participation and institutional repository development. On the constraint side, the review names digital inequality, algorithmic bias, data privacy risks, weak governance, limited institutional readiness, inadequate digital literacy and the continued dominance of commercial knowledge infrastructures. It notes that article processing charges can move costs from readers to authors and that automated captions or translation may fail for local languages and accents.

The recommendations are organizational rather than instructional. Universities are asked to adopt ethical AI frameworks covering privacy, transparency, accountability, bias, academic integrity and human oversight; to distinguish open, restricted and confidential data; to fund open access publishing; and to audit AI tools for bias, accessibility, data protection and language inclusiveness. Governments are asked to invest in broadband, digital libraries, repositories and assistive technologies. Libraries are asked to improve metadata, persistent identifiers, multilingual abstracts and AI-assisted indexing. The review also proposes South–South collaboration built on shared low-cost tools, policy templates and local-language resources, with technology transfer rather than donor dependency. A figure presents these elements as a strategic implementation model for inclusive AI and open science in higher education.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Build AI literacy into teaching so students can evaluate generated summaries, translations and recommendations rather than treating retrieval tools as neutral.
- **Librarians.** Lead repository deposit, metadata quality and multilingual discovery work, and offer training that connects access tools to actual research and study tasks.
- **Administrators.** Pair any AI adoption with data policies that separate open, restricted and confidential information, and with funds that keep open publishing from depending on individual grants.
- **Policymakers.** Treat connectivity, repository infrastructure and assistive technology as shared capacity, and favor partnerships that transfer skills instead of supplying platforms.

## Limitations

- **Narrative review without systematic search or appraisal.** No protocol, search string, screening counts or formal quality appraisal are reported. The authors explicitly say the review is not an exhaustive retrieval, which limits claims about coverage and balance.
- **No empirical data.** There are no participants, instruments, intervention or measured outcomes; the claims are interpretations of cited literature rather than estimates of effect.
- **Heterogeneous evidence base.** Conceptual, theoretical and empirical work from education, information science, digital scholarship, policy and ethics are synthesized without a common appraisal rubric or effect-size comparison.
- **No tested implementation.** The strategic model and recommendations are not evaluated in any institution, so whether they reduce knowledge inequality remains untested.
- **Context dependence.** The discussion is written from a Nigerian and under-resourced-institution perspective, but no local data are presented to show which of the proposed conditions matter most in practice.
