> **Synthesis:** Duong and Chen followed 47 Vietnamese [[secondary-education|high school]] EFL learners through an 11-week study with 8 weeks of writing support from a chatbot built around [[self-determination-theory|Self-Determination Theory]], [[self-regulated-learning|Self-Regulated Learning]] and [[self-efficacy]] Theory. Higher- and lower-proficiency groups both showed gains in intrinsic motivation, [[motivation|extrinsic motivation]] and writing self-efficacy from pre- to post-questionnaire, and the magnitude of change did not differ significantly between groups. Interviews with ten learners described reduced [[anxiety-and-stress|anxiety]], clearer guidance and supportive interaction, although stronger students emphasized refinement while less proficient students emphasized help getting started. The authors frame the results as exploratory associations during chatbot-supported writing, not causal effects: there was no randomized control group, and the design relied heavily on self-report.

## Key Findings
1. **Two proficiency groups shared one classroom.** Twenty-five higher-proficiency and 22 lower-proficiency eleventh-grade students used the same chatbot under the same instructor.
2. **The chatbot was positioned as a writing partner.** Students were told to use it for guidance, feedback, reflection and revision rather than to generate complete essays.
3. **All three affective measures increased.** Intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation and writing self-efficacy rose from pre-test to post-test in both groups.
4. **Self-efficacy showed the largest change.** Model-estimated gains in confidence were bigger than gains in either motivation measure.
5. **Proficiency did not change the pattern.** The time-by-proficiency interactions were not statistically significant for any outcome.
6. **Learners valued different kinds of help.** Higher-proficiency students described efficiency and argument refinement; lower-proficiency students described reduced hesitation and clearer guidance.
7. **The evidence is exploratory.** With no control group and mostly [[self-report-measures|self-report data]], the study cannot isolate the chatbot's causal contribution.

## What the study measured

Participants were 47 eleventh-grade students aged 16 in an intensive IELTS writing course at one Hanoi high school, selected by convenience sampling; 23 were female and 24 male, and none had prior experience with AI [[conversational-ai|chatbots]]. The higher-proficiency group of 25 had IELTS writing scores of 6.0-7.0, and the lower-proficiency group of 22 had scores of 4.0-5.0. In week 1 students completed a pre-questionnaire and received platform training; weeks 2 through 9 involved weekly chatbot-supported writing tasks; week 10 brought the post-questionnaire; and week 11 brought semi-structured interviews with ten randomly selected students, five per group.

The writing assistant delivered feedback at three levels: linguistic refinement at the micro level, idea development and organization at the macro level, and goal-setting, reflection and next-step planning at the process level. The 16-item questionnaire adapted from Pintrich and colleagues used a seven-point scale and covered intrinsic goal orientation, extrinsic goal orientation and self-efficacy. Internal consistency at pre-test and post-test was 0.82 and 0.77 for intrinsic motivation, 0.81 and 0.78 for extrinsic motivation, and 0.94 and 0.90 for self-efficacy. Interviews were coded with intercoder agreement reported as Cohen's kappa of 0.85.

Separate linear mixed-effects models treated time, proficiency and their interaction as fixed effects with a student random intercept. For intrinsic motivation, the time effect was b = 0.69, SE = 0.12, 95% CI [0.45, 0.93]; estimated change was 0.69 for higher-proficiency students with d z = 1.12 and 0.82 for lower-proficiency students with d z = 1.44, a difference described as small and not significant at g = 0.21. For extrinsic motivation, the time effect was b = 0.47, SE = 0.13, 95% CI [0.21, 0.73], p = 0.001. For self-efficacy, the time effect was b = 1.07, SE = 0.10, 95% CI [0.87, 1.27], p < 0.001, with estimated changes of 1.07 for higher-proficiency students at d z = 2.10 and 0.95 for lower-proficiency students at d z = 1.89.

## What the interviews added

The open-ended post-questionnaire item produced themes of enhanced confidence, increased engagement and greater interest, with 15 students in each group mentioning increased confidence. Across the ten interviews, the three themes were heightened interest in writing, reduced anxiety through supportive interaction and strengthened confidence. Higher-proficiency students connected the tool with saving time on vocabulary searches, refining arguments and validating structure; lower-proficiency students described hesitation about starting, lack of ideas and fear of asking questions in class. Several described the chatbot as a safe, non-judgmental interlocutor whose text-based replies were less intimidating than real-time interaction. All interviewees reported greater confidence in producing coherent, well-supported texts.

The authors align these experiences with the theories that shaped the tool: competence and autonomy in Self-Determination Theory, cyclical planning and reflection in Self-Regulated Learning, and mastery experiences in Self-Efficacy Theory. They do not claim direct measurement of those theoretical constructs. Instead, the findings describe how students perceived chatbot support and how questionnaire scores moved during the same period. The paper's practice guidance follows that framing: use the chatbot as a supplement to [[teacher-role|teacher]] feedback and embed it in cycles of drafting, feedback and reflection.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Frame chatbot use as guidance and revision support, explicitly prohibiting whole-essay generation.
- **Instructors.** Keep teacher feedback alongside chatbot feedback, especially for students who value human interaction and detailed judgment.
- **Instructors.** Vary the support emphasis: lower-proficiency writers may need help generating ideas and starting, while stronger writers may want help refining arguments and organization.
- **Curriculum designers.** Embed chatbot use in drafting, feedback and reflection cycles that preserve student ownership of the final text.
- **Researchers.** Add a comparison group and collect interaction logs, revision histories and writing-performance measures before claiming causal effects.

## Limitations
- There was no randomly assigned control or comparison group, so change cannot be attributed specifically to chatbot support.
- The study relied on self-report questionnaires and interviews rather than [[student-engagement|behavioral engagement]] or writing-development measures.
- Chatbot use was not systematically tracked, so frequency of use, prompt complexity and depth of feedback engagement are unknown.
- Theoretical constructs such as autonomy, relatedness and self-regulation informed the design but were not directly measured.
- The sample was small, came from one Vietnamese IELTS writing course, and the authors call for controlled studies to establish causal effects.
