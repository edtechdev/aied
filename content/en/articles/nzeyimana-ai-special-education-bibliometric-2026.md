> **Synthesis:** Nzeyimana and Hussein (2026) map six decades of research on [[ai-education|artificial intelligence]] in [[special-education]] using a bibliometric analysis of 977 Scopus-indexed publications from 1964 to 2024. They identify three phases — sparse output until the late 1990s, gradual growth from 2000 to 2010, and sharp acceleration after 2010 — and a research base concentrated in a small number of countries, led by the United States with 121 publications (12.4%) and China with 88 (9.0%). Keyword co-occurrence yields seven thematic clusters, from human–computer interaction and adaptive environments to [[machine-learning|machine learning]], [[assistive-technology|assistive technology]] and [[llm|large language models]]. The analysis maps the field's structure and dependencies rather than evaluating whether any tool improves outcomes, and its central practical message is that teacher preparation, [[ethics|ethical]] [[governance|governance]] and contextual validation remain thin relative to technical production.

## Key Findings
1. **Output accelerated sharply after 2010.** Publication counts stayed low until the late 1990s, rose gradually through 2010, then climbed steeply, reaching their highest annual levels in the most recent years.
2. **Research production is concentrated.** The United States accounts for 12.4% of the 977 publications (n = 121), China 9.0% (n = 88), the Russian Federation 4.6% (n = 45), India 3.6% (n = 35), and the United Kingdom and Spain 3.1% each (n = 30).
3. **Collaboration follows the same geography.** A small group of countries occupies central positions in the coauthorship network, with the United States as a major hub and China, the United Kingdom and Germany also prominent.
4. **Seven thematic clusters organise the literature.** They cover human–computer interaction and [[active-learning|active learning]]; virtual, augmented and adaptive environments; [[educational-nlp|natural language processing]] and [[neurodiversity|autism]]; machine learning and computer vision; ethics, sustainability and [[higher-ed|higher education]]; large language models and [[learning-analytics|learning analytics]]; and assistive technology and [[accessibility|accessibility]].
5. **Highly cited work often comes from outside special education.** Foundational machine-learning, computer-vision and manufacturing-AI papers are widely referenced, reflecting the field's methodological dependence on adjacent domains.
6. **The literature is more technical than [[pedagogy|pedagogical]].** Technology-oriented clusters are denser than pedagogy-focused ones, which the authors read as an emphasis on system development over instructional integration.
7. **The practical implications target teachers and governance.** The authors call for [[educational-development|professional development]] in data interpretation, accessibility and ethical decision-making, and for research that connects tools to classroom practice.

## What a bibliometric map can and cannot show

The study analyses publication trends, keyword co-occurrence, citation patterns and international collaboration using VOSviewer. It does not assess study quality, effect sizes or implementation outcomes, and the authors are explicit that bibliometric indicators reflect patterns of scholarly communication rather than measures of educational effectiveness. That distinction is the right one to keep in view: a cluster of keywords about [[adaptive-learning|adaptive learning]] tells you that researchers are writing about adaptive learning, not that adaptive learning works for students with disabilities.

The citation finding is more interesting than it first looks. The most-cited paper in the dataset is a manufacturing machine-learning review with over 1,000 citations, and several other highly cited works come from general machine learning, computer vision and financial analytics. The authors treat this as a genuine finding about the field's theoretical dependencies rather than as contamination. Researchers in AI and special education draw heavily on methods developed elsewhere, which helps explain both the field's technical sophistication and its relative weakness on pedagogical integration.

## Where the gaps are

The authors name four: limited emphasis on pedagogical integration, educator readiness, ethical governance, and cross-contextual validation. The geographic concentration reinforces the last of these. With more than half of the corpus attributed to countries outside the top ten, but collaboration and citation centrality held by a small group, the contexts represented in the literature are narrower than the population the field serves. Infrastructure — connectivity, devices, technical support — varies widely across regions, and the paper notes that this variation limits adoption in low-resource settings.

For [[inclusive-learning]], the implication is that the evidence base is not yet strong enough to support tool-by-tool recommendations. What it can support is a shift in research emphasis: from cataloguing platforms and algorithms to studying how tools are embedded in classrooms, supported by teachers and adapted to diverse learners.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Ask for evidence that a tool works with learners like yours, not just that it exists. The reviewed literature is richer in technical description than in classroom trials.
- **Administrators.** Fund teacher preparation and support alongside procurement. The authors identify educator readiness as one of the field's main gaps, not as an implementation detail.
- **Researchers.** Connect technical work to instructional integration and report context. The field's citation base is interdisciplinary; its pedagogical evidence base is not yet.
- **Policymakers.** Treat access and infrastructure as part of the intervention. A tool that assumes connectivity and devices will not serve the students the field is meant to include.

## Limitations

- The corpus is Scopus-indexed only, which may underrepresent regional journals, non-English outlets, and work indexed in ERIC or Web of Science.
- Bibliometric indicators describe publication and citation patterns, not learning outcomes or implementation quality.
- Keyword co-occurrence depends on authors' indexing choices and cannot capture the substance of individual studies.
- The geographic concentration in the dataset may reflect database coverage as well as actual research production.
- The analysis identifies clusters and gaps but does not test explanations for them.
