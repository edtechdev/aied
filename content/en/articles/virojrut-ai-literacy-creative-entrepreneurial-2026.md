> **Synthesis:** Virojrut and colleagues surveyed 506 Generation Z business students in Thailand and used partial least squares structural equation modeling to test how [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] relates to creative thinking, learning achievement and entrepreneurial readiness. All six hypothesized direct paths were positive and significant. AI literacy was most strongly associated with creative thinking, less strongly with learning achievement, and only modestly with entrepreneurial readiness. Creative thinking and learning achievement were each linked to readiness, and indirect paths through these variables were significant, including a sequential path from AI literacy through creative thinking and learning achievement. The authors read the pattern as a capability-building process and extend Human Capital Theory by treating AI literacy as digital human capital. The design is cross-sectional and self-reported, so causal claims are limited.

## Key Findings
1. **AI literacy was the strongest predictor of creative thinking.** It also showed smaller but significant associations with learning achievement and entrepreneurial readiness.
2. **Creative thinking mattered twice.** It was associated with both learning achievement and entrepreneurial readiness, making it a bridge between literacy and later outcomes.
3. **Learning achievement predicted readiness.** Students reporting stronger achievement motivation and learning strategies were more likely to report entrepreneurial preparedness.
4. **Indirect paths were significant.** AI literacy related to readiness through creative thinking alone, through learning achievement alone and through both in sequence.
5. **The model explained substantial variance in readiness.** The three predictors together accounted for a larger share of variance in readiness than in creative thinking or learning achievement.
6. **Measurement refinement removed weak indicators.** Four items fell below the loading threshold, and the retained indicators covered technical knowledge, confidence and ethical awareness, among others.
7. **The sample was specific.** All respondents were Thai Generation Z undergraduates in business programs, recruited purposively, and the sample was predominantly female.

## Design and measurement

The study used a cross-sectional questionnaire design and PLS-SEM in SmartPLS 4. Participants were undergraduate Generation Z students in business-related programs at public and private institutions in Thailand, recruited through university networks, online learning platforms and student organizations. Cochran's formula with a 95% confidence level and a margin of error of ±5% set a minimum of 384 participants; 506 valid responses were obtained. The final sample comprised 146 male respondents (28.90%) and 360 female respondents (71.10%), with 310 respondents (61.30%) in public institutions and 196 (38.70%) in private ones. The largest specializations were logistics management (24.70%), management (21.30%), human resource management (19.60%) and retail business (16.40%). Most respondents reported a cumulative GPA between 3.01 and 3.50 (52.80%), followed by 2.51–3.00 (31.40%) and 3.51–4.00 (11.50%).

The questionnaire had 36 items on a five-point Likert scale: 8 for AI literacy, 8 for creative thinking capability, 8 for learning achievement and 12 for entrepreneurial readiness. Three experts assessed content validity with the Index of Item-Objective Congruence, using a minimum acceptable value of 0.50, and a pilot test with 30 individuals found reliability above 0.70 for all constructs. Four indicators — problem-solving skills, blended learning, external factors and entrepreneurial passion — were excluded for outer loadings below 0.70. The retained model used three indicators for AI literacy, four for creative thinking, two for learning achievement and five for entrepreneurial readiness. Retained loadings ranged from 0.761 to 0.835, and VIF values ranged from 1.318 to 1.783, below the stated threshold of 3.30.

## What the structural model found

The measurement model passed the stated reliability and validity criteria. In the structural model, AI literacy explained 46.8% of the variance in creative thinking capability (R² = 0.468). AI literacy and creative thinking jointly explained 42.1% of the variance in learning achievement (R² = 0.421). AI literacy, creative thinking and learning achievement together explained 61.2% of the variance in entrepreneurial readiness (R² = 0.612).

All six direct paths were positive and significant. AI literacy predicted creative thinking (β = 0.684, t = 16.683, p < 0.001), learning achievement (β = 0.287, t = 4.348, p < 0.001) and entrepreneurial readiness (β = 0.118, t = 2.360, p = 0.018). Creative thinking predicted learning achievement (β = 0.471, t = 7.359, p < 0.001) and entrepreneurial readiness (β = 0.326, t = 5.344, p < 0.001). Learning achievement predicted entrepreneurial readiness (β = 0.401, t = 7.035, p < 0.001).

Mediation analyses confirmed the indirect pathways. AI literacy had an indirect effect on entrepreneurial readiness through creative thinking (β = 0.223, t = 4.956, p < 0.001) and through learning achievement (β = 0.115, t = 3.594, p < 0.001). Creative thinking had an indirect effect through learning achievement (β = 0.189, t = 4.846, p < 0.001). The sequential path from AI literacy through creative thinking and learning achievement was significant (β = 0.129, t = 4.300, p < 0.001), and the total indirect effect of AI literacy was positive (β = 0.467, t = 7.532, p < 0.001).

The discussion interprets AI literacy as a form of digital human capital whose effect unfolds through a layered process: literacy supports [[creativity|creative thinking]], which supports learning and opportunity recognition, which in turn supports [[career-development-and-readiness|entrepreneurial readiness]]. The authors recommend embedding ethical awareness and critical evaluation of AI-generated information into business curricula and using project-based and experiential learning rather than isolated tool training.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Teach AI literacy as more than tool operation: include technical knowledge, confidence and ethical awareness in business and entrepreneurship courses.
- **Instructors.** Pair AI activities with creative tasks that require fluency, flexibility, originality and elaboration, since creative thinking carried much of the association with readiness.
- **Assessment designers.** Use competency-based assessment of real-world problem solving and business idea development rather than relying only on traditional examinations.
- **Institutions.** Treat AI literacy, creativity and learning achievement as connected targets; the paper argues that readiness does not emerge from technology training alone.
- **Researchers.** Replicate the model with probability samples and other disciplines before generalizing beyond Thai business students.

## Limitations

- **Cross-sectional self-report.** The data cannot support strong causal inference or show how the four constructs change over time; social desirability and response tendencies may influence answers.
- **Sampling and composition.** Purposive recruitment limits generalization, and female respondents made up 71.1% of the sample, which matters because entrepreneurial attitudes and readiness may vary by gender.
- **Context bound.** The sample consists of Thai business undergraduates; STEM students and students in other countries or educational systems may differ in AI exposure and entrepreneurial context.
- **Individual-level focus.** University policy, institutional support, digital infrastructure, government programs and regional innovation ecosystems were not modeled.
- **Construct coverage.** A cross-sectional path model of questionnaire items cannot establish that AI literacy caused the observed differences in achievement or readiness.
