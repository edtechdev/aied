> **Synthesis:** A brief research report from a master's-level health technology course describes students' experiences of generative AI used in group work and exam assignments. Two surveys drew 49 baseline and 33 follow-up responses, and 11 students took part in semi-structured interviews. In the end-of-course survey, 74% reported using AI during the course and 26% reported not using it. Students described using AI for prototyping, writing, visual design, idea generation and problem-solving, and perceived benefits for efficiency, creativity and [[collaborative-learning]]; some called it an extra group member. The same accounts raised concerns about over-reliance, hallucinations, reliability, privacy and the cost of advanced tools, together with a perceived trade-off between productivity and independent thinking. The authors conclude that generative AI was experienced as a valuable but complex learning resource and that critical, responsible use and [[ai-literacy]] matter. The evidence is self-reported experience from one course, not measured learning outcomes.

## Key Findings
1. **Use was widespread but not universal.** Most respondents reported using generative AI during the course, while a substantial minority reported not using it.
2. **Students used AI across many tasks.** Prototyping, writing support, brainstorming, visual design, information search and presentation development all appeared in the accounts.
3. **Perceived benefits clustered around efficiency, creativity and collaboration.** Students reported time savings, faster idea generation and support for group interaction across disciplines.
4. **Some students described AI as a group member.** The phrase was used particularly where a team lacked knowledge in an area, suggesting a shift from tool to collaborator.
5. **Critical concerns were as prominent as benefits.** Over-reliance, reduced effort, unreliable outputs, hallucinations and privacy questions recurred in both survey and interview material.
6. **Verification was presented as a student responsibility.** Participants said outputs needed to be checked against other sources before being trusted.
7. **The design supports experience, not effectiveness.** The study reports perceived educational value from a single case and has no performance-based measures.

## What the study measured and how

The case was the Health and Technology master's course (10 ECTS) at OsloMet University in the autumn semester of 2024. The course enrolled 49 students; one withdrew, leaving 48 participants, of whom 87.2% were in regular study programs and 12.8% were exchange students. Students worked in teams of 3–8 on future health technology interventions, supported by eight mentors with backgrounds spanning biomedical engineering, design, digitalization, neuropharmacology, radiography, physiotherapy, and science and technology. Generative AI was encouraged in group work and exam assignments, and the course included four dedicated lectures on responsible use of conversational and generative AI in professional contexts.

Data came from a baseline survey answered by all 49 students, an end-of-course survey answered by 33, and 11 individual interviews conducted at the end of the course. Interviewees were selected purposively across working groups; seven were women. Interviews lasted between 40 and 60 minutes, were audio-recorded and were conducted by an author not involved in teaching the course. The research team combined insider and outsider perspectives and held reflective discussions throughout the analysis.

Survey data were analyzed descriptively. Interview data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke, with inductive coding, iterative comparison and collaborative discussion rather than a search for inter-coder agreement. The analysis produced four themes: AI as a learning trajectory; AI as a multifunctional learning companion; enhancing learning through efficiency, creativity and collaboration; and critical engagement with AI, including risks and tensions. Saturation was not sought as a formal criterion; the authors judged the dataset rich enough to support coherent themes.

## What the findings report

In the end-of-course survey (n = 33), 74% reported AI use during the course and 26% reported no use. Writing support, idea generation and brainstorming, prototyping and solution development, visual and presentation support, and information search and comprehension were all frequently reported purposes. Increased efficiency, support for creativity, enhanced collaboration in group work and, for several students, personalized learning and feedback were the frequently reported or noted benefits; reliability issues involving hallucinations were a frequently reported challenge.

Interview extracts show how these categories played out in practice. Students described using conversational tools to refine prompts during prototyping, to improve the clarity and structure of written text, to generate images for future solutions, and to support economic estimation when they lacked prior knowledge. One student said AI was easier than Google Scholar for finding material; another said it helped formulate interview questions. Several described AI as an "extra group member" filling knowledge gaps, and as helpful when a group felt stuck during brainstorming.

The critical theme concentrated on the tension between efficiency and depth. Students said heavy trust in generated text could reduce learning, that they questioned their own contribution to group work, and that outputs had to be double-checked against other sources. Participants identified hallucinations as a significant issue that cannot be fully prevented. Survey responses also raised [[privacy]] questions about applications collecting student information, and the cost of advanced tools was identified as a barrier with implications for equity. The discussion connects these findings to sociotechnical views of learning and to [[hallucination-risk]], arguing that the value of generative AI depends on students' ability to evaluate outputs critically rather than simply on access to tools.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Teach verification as part of AI use: the students in this case explicitly called for checking generated claims against other sources.
- **Instructors.** Build AI use into authentic group projects and discuss it as a subject of inquiry, since students encountered it as both tool and topic.
- **Institutions.** Address access and cost when introducing advanced AI tools, since expense was named as a potential barrier.
- **Institutions.** Include privacy and handling of sensitive information in guidance for health technology courses working with patient-related scenarios.
- **Researchers.** Replicate the case in other settings with larger cohorts and performance-based measures before claiming effects on critical thinking or skill development.

## Limitations
- The study is a single master's-level course in one institution, and the authors state that its results show how AI may be integrated in similar settings rather than evidence of scalability or systemic impact.
- Only 11 students were interviewed; the dataset was purposively varied but small, and reflexive thematic analysis does not aim for formal saturation.
- Survey responses fell from 49 at baseline to 33 at the end of the course, limiting direct individual-level comparison.
- Findings are based primarily on self-reported perceptions, not direct measures of learning outcomes or skill development; the authors describe the results as perceived educational value.
- The hybrid nature of group work and the interdisciplinary course structure may have shaped students' experiences in ways that would not transfer to other formats.
- Generalizability is further limited by the rapid evolution of generative AI technologies and by the absence of longitudinal or performance-based data.
