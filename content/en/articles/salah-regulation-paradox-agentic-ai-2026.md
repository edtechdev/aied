> **Synthesis:** Salah and colleagues ask whether an AI agent that plans, remembers and acts on a student's behalf is helping that student become a more capable self-regulated learner or quietly taking over the very acts of planning, monitoring and reflection that build that capacity. Their answer is a Position paper built on a distinction between technical autonomy, meaning what a system can do on its own, and [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] agenticity, meaning whether the learner grows more capable. They name four mechanisms by which well-meant support can displace regulation: regulatory displacement, metacognitive laziness, reduced [[desirable-difficulties|productive struggle]] and epistemic [[trust-calibration|over-trust]]. They propose bounded autonomy as a governance principle that limits the scope, timing, transparency, contestability and duration of AI intervention and gradually hands decision authority back to the learner. The proposal is a framework for designing and judging agentic AI, not a measured result. This article reports no empirical data: it presents no sample, instrument, outcome or test of the principle it advances.

## Key Findings

1. **Agentic AI is defined by where initiative sits, not by the model.** The paper treats systems that hold persistent goals, memory and multi-step action as a shift in the location of initiative. A reactive tool can substitute for a product, while an agentic system can substitute for the regulatory activity behind that product.
2. **Pedagogical agenticity is the central criterion.** A system earns educational value when its initiative builds learner capability rather than merely finishing a task. A stronger essay is not evidence of learning if the planning and revision that develop writing were done by the system.
3. **The regulation paradox separates four mechanisms.** Regulatory displacement moves planning, monitoring or evaluation away from the learner; metacognitive laziness reduces active checking; reduced productive struggle removes useful difficulty; epistemic over-trust accepts system judgment without scrutiny. Each calls for a different safeguard.
4. **Self-regulated learning is proposed as the standard of judgment.** The authors read agentic support against the three phases of forethought, performance and reflection, and argue that [[personalized-learning|personalization]] is a weaker test than better goal quality, realistic planning, calibrated confidence, appropriate [[help-seeking]], monitoring accuracy and transfer to tasks without AI.
5. **Bounded autonomy is the governance principle offered in response.** It is defined as limiting the scope, timing, transparency, contestability and duration of intervention to the learner's developing regulatory capacity, so that a system may act proactively while its authority stays restricted and returns over time.
6. **Implementation is tied to people and institutions.** Responsible use depends on learner capability, teacher agency, institutional readiness and evidence that regulatory skills transfer to tasks completed without AI. Success is to be judged by the responsibility returned, not by the responsibility assumed.

## The proposal in concrete terms

The paper's vocabulary does specific work. [[agentic-ai|Agentic systems]] are set apart from conventional prompt-response generation, assistive and [[adaptive-learning|adaptive systems]] by degree and location of initiative rather than by the underlying model. "Pedagogical agenticity" then asks whether that initiative is directed toward learning development or only toward task completion. The authors state three contributions: distinguishing technical autonomy from pedagogical agenticity, separating the mechanisms of the regulation paradox, and proposing bounded autonomy as a way to allocate and return decision authority.

Bounded autonomy is presented as a governance principle, not another name for familiar ideas. [[scaffolding|Scaffolding and fading]] describe how assistance is given and withdrawn, but not how much authority a proactive agent may hold or how that authority returns. Co-regulation and hybrid human-[[regulation|AI regulation]] describe shared regulation; [[productive-failure|productive failure]] protects learning through difficulty; [[explainable-ai|explainable AI]] makes recommendations understandable. The Agency Allocation Framework names where decision authority sits. Bounded autonomy gathers these around one question: what may the AI decide, under what conditions, and when must that authority return to the learner? The authors draw on the detect-diagnose-act account of hybrid regulation, in which a system may detect difficulty, interpret the learner's state and decide whether action is needed; sometimes the correct action is to wait.

The design rules follow from that question. The learner authors and justifies the final goal and plan, while system proposals stay visible and editable. Hints come before answers, and the learner predicts and attempts before feedback appears. [[self-assessment]] comes before system evaluation, and system explanations remain contestable and can be rejected. Consequential learning decisions stay primarily with learners, and AI involvement declines as regulatory capacity develops. The aim, in the authors' words, is not effortless learning but better-regulated effort.

The paper organizes these rules by [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulated learning]] phase. During forethought, appropriate support clarifies goals, identifies prerequisites and presents possible plans, with regulatory displacement as the main risk. During performance, support detects [[misconceptions]], offers graduated hints, prompts self-explanation and recommends strategy revision, with [[metacognition|metacognitive laziness]] and reduced productive struggle as the risks. During reflection, support compares intended and actual progress and prompts attribution of outcomes, with epistemic over-trust as the risk. Across the sequence, evidence of stronger self-regulated learning is described as better goal quality, realistic planning, calibrated confidence, appropriate help-seeking, persistence, monitoring accuracy, strategy revision and [[reflection|reflection quality]]; the strongest evidence is transfer to unsupported tasks.

## What the argument rests on

This is a Position paper. The authors say they draw purposively on literature directly related to agentic AI, self-regulated learning, [[agency|learner agency]], metacognition, human-AI regulation and [[higher-ed|higher education]] governance, integrating foundational theory with recent empirical studies, reviews and international frameworks. They describe the purpose as analytic synthesis rather than exhaustive or systematic coverage. The argument therefore advances by conceptual connections rather than by new measurement.

Several theories supply those connections. [[self-determination-theory|Self-determination theory]] identifies autonomy and competence as important psychological needs, so AI may support competence through feedback and autonomy through informed choice when the learner keeps meaningful control. Heutagogy frames the learner as the main agent and treats capability, meaning effective action in unfamiliar and unsupported situations, as the goal. The Fogg Behavior Model, applied by others to language-model use, is used here to suggest that continuous [[prompt-engineering|prompting]] and lowered effort requirements may encourage overuse or dependence; the authors state that this possibility is not yet demonstrated and requires direct empirical examination. UNESCO's framework is cited as identifying five competency areas for [[educational-development|teacher development]]: a human-centered mindset, the [[ethics|ethics of AI]], AI foundations and applications, AI pedagogy, and AI for professional learning.

Governance is described at multiple levels. Instructor agency covers defining outcomes, designing tasks, setting acceptable AI use, deciding when support should be reduced and judging independent performance. Institutional readiness covers which systems are adopted, what data are collected, how decisions are audited, who remains accountable and whether access is [[equity-in-ai-education|equitable]]. The paper warns that without these responsibilities being addressed, bounded autonomy remains an interface feature. Future research is asked to compare levels of system autonomy using [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal]] and withdrawal designs and to trace how decision authority moves among learners, teachers, institutions and AI systems.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Define acceptable AI use at the level of each task and state in advance when support will be reduced, so that students know which decisions remain theirs.
- **Instructors.** Require the learner to author and justify the goal and plan, and keep system proposals visible and editable; offer hints before answers and wait for a learner prediction and attempt before feedback appears.
- **Instructors.** Put learner self-assessment before system evaluation and keep system explanations contestable and rejectable, so that students practice judging the advice they receive.
- **Researchers.** Compare hint-based tutors, planning assistants and autonomous task managers with longitudinal and withdrawal designs, and test whether regulatory skills transfer to tasks completed without AI.
- **Institutions.** Decide which systems are adopted, what data are collected, how decisions are audited, who is accountable and whether access is equitable; build teacher development around [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]], [[learning-design|instructional design]], [[critical-thinking|higher-order thinking]] and confidence in pedagogical use.

## Limitations

- The piece is a Perspective with no empirical component: no sample, instrument, outcome or data collection, and no test of the principle it proposes.
- Literature selection is purposive. The authors describe the aim as analytic synthesis rather than exhaustive or systematic coverage, so the result is not a [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|systematic review]] and its selection criteria are not specified.
- Bounded autonomy is stipulated rather than operationalized. The paper names the dimensions of scope, timing, transparency, contestability and duration but gives no thresholds, measurement rules or schedule for returning authority.
- The central transfer claim is untested here. The authors state that possible overuse or dependence under continuous prompting requires direct empirical examination and call for longitudinal and withdrawal designs.
- No levels of system autonomy are compared in the article. The paper notes that hint-based tutors, planning assistants and autonomous task managers are not pedagogically equivalent, but offers no comparison to establish which forms work better.
