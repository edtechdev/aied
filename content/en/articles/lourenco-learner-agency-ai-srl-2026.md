> **Synthesis:** This theoretical analysis reframes [[self-regulated-learning]] as a coordinated, recursive system in which task appraisal, [[metacognition|metacognitive]] judgment, planning, [[motivation|motivational]] beliefs and decision-making inform one another. It asks how information generated by one regulatory process is interpreted and used by others, and how AI redistributes responsibility for those operations. The central distinction is between distributed regulation, where functions are shared but the learner keeps authority over interpretation and consequential decisions, and delegated regulation, where substantive regulatory work is transferred to the system. A four-layer model places learner regulation, AI-mediated support, epistemic evaluation and adaptive control in a recursive arrangement. The article reports no empirical data; its distinctions and three propositions are offered as frameworks requiring empirical validation.

## Key Findings
1. **Coordination is the unit of analysis.** Regulatory processes are treated as functionally interdependent rather than as separate predictors of learning.
2. **Four dimensions organize the analysis.** Monitoring, planning, belief-based interpretation and decision-making capture complementary aspects of cognitive regulation.
3. **Three principles guide integration.** Functional interdependence, temporal recursiveness and regulatory decision-making explain how information circulates and becomes action.
4. **Regulatory effectiveness is not task success.** A learner may complete work with substantial AI help without exercising the capacity to regulate learning independently.
5. **Distributed and delegated regulation differ by responsibility.** The same system can support agency or displace it depending on who interprets, evaluates and decides.
6. **Temporary delegation is not necessarily problematic.** Intentional, reversible use with retained oversight and the ability to revise or reject output remains distributed regulation.
7. **Three testable propositions are offered.** They concern alignment among regulatory components, the intention-to-action gap, and conditions under which AI support preserves [[agency|learner agency]].

## The coordination problem

The authors draw on Zimmerman, Pintrich, Winne and Hadwin, Nelson and Narens, and Bandura to argue that monitoring, planning, beliefs and decisions form a system. Monitoring produces information about perceived knowledge or performance, but that information does not itself regulate action; its significance depends on interpretation. Plans are revised in response to feedback, and [[self-efficacy]] beliefs shape whether a difficulty prompts persistence or withdrawal. Volitional control and procrastination matter because a learner may value a task and plan it yet fail to start or sustain it. [[help-seeking]] is offered as an example of adaptive regulation when it clarifies understanding, and of less productive substitution when it replaces independent analysis.

The review searched Scopus, Web of Science and ERIC between September and October 2026. Within it, 22 articles published between 2018 and 2025 were selected for conceptual synthesis, alongside foundational and 2026 sources; the reference list comprises 46 publications. Selection was based on thematic relevance, conceptual or empirical contribution, and alignment with the article's objectives. Interpretation relied on conceptual comparison rather than [[quantitative-research|quantitative]] aggregation.

## The proposed framework

The four-layer model begins with learner regulation: goals, task representation, [[prior-knowledge|prior knowledge]], motivational beliefs, metacognitive knowledge and strategic intentions. The second layer is the AI-mediated support environment, where systems may provide information, explanations, feedback, strategy alternatives, planning suggestions or analytic representations of performance. The article distinguishes systems with access to learning records from general-purpose [[conversational-ai|chatbots]], which ordinarily depend on information the learner supplies and cannot be assumed to monitor actual performance independently.

The third layer is epistemic evaluation. AI outputs enter the learner's system as claims requiring appraisal against task requirements, prior knowledge, evidence and alternative sources. Fluent responses are not reliable evidence in themselves. The fourth layer is adaptive control, where the learner maintains, modifies, rejects or replaces a strategy. AI can inform control without exercising it. Motivational commitment and volitional control then help translate an evaluated recommendation into sustained action.

Distributed regulation occurs when cognitive functions are shared while interpretation and consequential decisions remain with the learner. Delegated regulation occurs when goal formulation, strategic choice, evaluation or judgment are progressively transferred to the system. The authors treat this as a conceptual framework, not an established dichotomy; the same tool can operate in either mode depending on task design, prompts, expertise and metacognitive support. [[cognitive-offloading|Cognitive offloading]] is therefore judged by what is externalized and what remains cognitively active. Calibrated epistemic trust means confidence proportionate to evidential quality, relevance and verifiability.

The three propositions pair with empirical tests. The first expects regulatory effectiveness to depend on alignment among appraisal, judgment, commitment, decision and action, and suggests process-oriented studies using trace data or think-aloud protocols. The second expects monitoring and planning to guide action when commitment and volitional control are present, suggesting [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal]] and diary studies of planned versus enacted behavior. The third expects distributed regulation to preserve active monitoring while progressive delegation reduces it, suggesting experimental and mixed-method studies of [[prompt-engineering|prompting]], [[scaffolding]], calibration and epistemic trust.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Design tasks that state explicitly which functions may be externalized and which learners must perform themselves.
- **Instructors.** Require learners to justify choices, verify claims and explain subsequent adaptations rather than only produce an AI-supported answer.
- **Institutions.** Treat [[teacher-role|teacher]] guidance, assessment criteria and norms as part of the regulatory environment, not as secondary to the tool.
- **Researchers.** Test the propositions with process data that observe regulation as it unfolds, rather than inferring it from final outcomes.

## Limitations
- The analysis is theoretical and conceptual rather than empirical and does not establish causal relationships.
- The distinctions between augmentation and replacement, and between distributed and delegated regulation, require empirical validation rather than being fixed categories.
- The literature selection was guided by conceptual relevance rather than exhaustive coverage or effectiveness estimation.
- General-purpose chatbots and data-informed learning environments have different access to performance evidence, limiting universal claims about AI support.
- Rapid changes in [[generative-ai|generative AI]] constrain the stability of technology-specific conclusions.
