> **Synthesis:** Anders and Akopyan (2026) report a phenomenological study of five faculty members in the English and Communication Program at the American [[higher-ed|University]] of Armenia about their experience of [[generative-ai|generative AI]] in teaching and learning. Hour-long, semi-structured interviews produced five categories and eleven themes, dominated by concerns about [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]] and student learning alongside what the authors call a lack of training, understanding, and experience. Faculty described shifting [[assessment]] toward in-class, AI-resistant tasks, doubted the quality and [[creativity]] of AI output, and believed using AI to give [[feedback]] would be inappropriate, yet still expressed optimism about the technology's role. All five knew university resources existed but none had attended the available training sessions, and all reported having used only the free version of ChatGPT. The study argues that [[teacher-role|faculty]] need structured support, hands-on training, and leadership guidance before [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] can develop.

*Phenomenological [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] study using face-to-face, in-depth, open-ended semi-structured interviews with five of 19 available faculty, conducted in the final month of a spring semester. Ten open-ended questions plus probing; audio recorded, transcribed, and coded inductively by two researchers following Braun and Clarke's thematic analysis.*

## Key Findings

1. **Academic integrity dominated the concerns.** All five faculty worried about cheating; two reported using Turnitin [[ai-detection]] software and interviewing or penalizing students for misconduct.
2. **Assessment practice shifted in response.** Instructors moved toward in-class quizzes, project- and problem-based work, and in-class writing, and one abandoned online discussion forums because students used AI for both initial and peer responses.
3. **Quality concerns ran alongside the integrity worries.** Faculty doubted that AI output was creative or high quality, and one described a student's admitted AI use on a low-stakes creative writing assignment as angering and disappointing.
4. **Using AI for feedback felt wrong.** One instructor called it a disservice; another said they believed they should do it but had not, capturing the conflict the authors found across the sample.
5. **Training was known about but unused.** Every participant knew university AI resources existed, yet none had attended any of the offered training sessions, citing intimidation, uncertainty about departmental policy, or a belief they could figure it out alone.
6. **Experience was shallow.** All participants reported using only the free version of ChatGPT, and the preferred support formats were hands-on basic workshops, short asynchronous courses, and training within regular program meetings.
## What the interviews reveal

The study situates itself against a literature that has mostly measured faculty attitudes through surveys. Its contribution is the texture of lived experience: the same instructor who worries about cheating also says AI "can be a great help" and that it may be "a move in the right direction"; the same department that treats AI as a threat also sees it as the future. The authors read this as a mix of reservation and optimism rather than resistance, which matters because it suggests faculty are not refusing the technology so much as waiting for a coherent institutional stance.

Three categories carried most of the weight. First, concerns about academic integrity and student learning produced concrete changes in practice: more in-class assessment, assignments designed so AI could not be used, and in one case the abandonment of online forums. Second, quality concerns centered on creativity and depth, with faculty reading AI output as shallow and worrying that shared tools would homogenize student writing — a particular worry in a program devoted to individual voice. Third, a lack of training, understanding, and experience left faculty unable to see how AI might serve course preparation, content presentation, or assignment design while still meeting student learning outcomes.

A smaller category captured positivity — a general sense that AI is a good thing and the future — and a fifth captured [[self-directed-learning|self-directed]] improvement: faculty knew resources existed. Those two sit awkwardly beside the finding that nobody attended training. The authors explain the gap through the interviews themselves: people felt intimidated, unsure whether AI use was even appropriate, uncertain about the department's policy, or confident they already understood the technology well enough.

## Why support did not take

The most actionable finding is the mismatch between what the university offered and what faculty would accept. Training sessions were available and known about, yet attendance was zero across the sample. The reasons were not disinterest but readiness: potential attendees felt they lacked the background to learn from the session, or were still forming their own position, or questioned whether a consensus on [[educational-policy-ai|AI policy]] existed to anchor the training. Meanwhile the format mattered — participants asked for hands-on basic workshops, short asynchronous modules, and training embedded in regular program meetings rather than standalone sessions.

For faculty developers, the implication is that availability is not uptake. The authors argue leadership must actively encourage use and remind faculty of resources while shaping them to fit actual needs. They also press a reframing task: many faculty understood generative AI primarily as a cheating tool, and [[educational-development|professional development]] must show its uses in preparation, presentation, and assessment design if that perception is to shift. Without that, [[ai-literacy]] development stalls at the level of suspicion.

## What this means for practice

- **Faculty developers.** Offer formats faculty say they will use: hands-on basics, short asynchronous units, and sessions inside regular program meetings. Do not assume advertised training will be attended.
- **Administrators.** Make the institutional stance explicit. Uncertainty about whether AI use is permitted, and the absence of a departmental policy, was itself a barrier to engagement.
- **Instructors.** Redesign assessment deliberately rather than reactively. The moves reported here — in-class writing, [[project-based-learning|project-based]] tasks, assignments that resist delegation — are starting points, not solutions.
- **Researchers.** Treat faculty attitude as layered. A single survey score misses the coexistence of concern and optimism, and the reasons training fails to reach the people it targets.

## Limitations

- Five faculty from one program at one university were interviewed, so the findings describe a small, specific context and cannot be generalized statistically.
- As a phenomenological study, the results capture lived experience and perception rather than measured effects on teaching or student learning.
- Participants were drawn from a pool of 19 faculty in a single English and Communication Program, which limits disciplinary range.
- The study reports on faculty who had used only the free version of ChatGPT at the time of interview, so their judgments reflect an earlier generation of the technology.
- The analysis relies on two researchers' inductive coding and inter-coder agreement; the authors report reliability checks but provide no independent audit of the theme structure.
- The study offers no outcome data on whether the recommended training or leadership actions changed behavior.
