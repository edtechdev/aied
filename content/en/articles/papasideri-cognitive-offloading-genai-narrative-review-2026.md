> **Synthesis:** Papasideri, Chatziioannou and Palaiologos (2026) review theoretical and empirical work published between 2016 and 2026 on [[cognitive-offloading|cognitive offloading]] — delegating mental effort to an external aid — now that [[generative-ai|generative AI]] can absorb reasoning, synthesis, evaluation and creative generation rather than only lookup and arithmetic. They present the paper as a narrative literature review, not a systematic review or meta-analysis, and as a synthesis of the current state of evidence rather than an exhaustive census: it reports no PRISMA-style record flow, pools no effect sizes, and offers no new data. Its argument is that the reviewed studies associate heavier offloading with weaker delayed retention, thinner [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulated learning]] and lower self-reported [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]], with [[executive-function|executive function]] as the third outcome domain, and that these costs depend on how the tool is used.

## Offloading moves up the cognitive ladder

Cognitive offloading is the use of physical or digital action to reduce a task's internal demands: writing a reminder instead of rehearsing it, or using a calculator instead of mental arithmetic. Decades of work on notebooks, calculators and search shows offloading can be adaptive, freeing resources for higher-order work while costing the representations that go unpracticed. Generative AI changes that scope: where search engines offload fact retrieval and calculators computation, large language models can offload reasoning, argument construction, evaluation and creative generation — the processes that memory research and [[cognitive-psychology|cognitive psychology]] treat as drivers of durable learning.

Two traditions anchor the review. The extended-mind thesis holds that cognition can incorporate external tools as functional components, not mere aids. The [[metacognition|metacognitive]] model treats offloading as a value-based decision: a person weighs the expected cost of holding information internally against the cost and reliability of an external aid, and offloads when the external option seems more efficient. Its best-known corollary, the "Google effect," is that information expected to be retrievable externally is less well encoded and recalled, though people remember where to find it.

A model built for discrete subtasks, where the person keeps control of the problem structure, needs a further distinction: "cognitive surrender," or adopting an AI output as one's own with minimal scrutiny, relinquishing control of the reasoning rather than delegating a bounded step. The review contrasts this with "dependent" offloading, which displaces deep processing and skill development, and "autonomous" offloading, which uses AI as support while preserving engagement.

## What kind of evidence the review assembled

The authors chose a narrative review because the base is young and fast-moving, with most primary studies published between 2023 and 2026. Studies came from targeted database searches combining terms for cognitive offloading, generative AI, large language models, ChatGPT, memory, self-regulated learning, metacognition, critical thinking and executive function, with reference lists hand-searched.

Inclusion required an empirically measured memory, learning, metacognitive or executive-function outcome in relation to GenAI or LLM use; peer review, or a rigorous recent preprint; or a theoretical or meta-analytic synthesis contextualizing GenAI-specific findings. Work on only economic, ethical or technical dimensions was excluded. The review is not an exhaustive census: it reports no PRISMA-style record flow, and pools no effect sizes given heterogeneous designs and measures.

## Memory: supplied content is encoded less durably

The clearest finding concerns memory for offloaded content: extending the Google effect, several studies report that material supplied by an LLM is less durably encoded than material a person generates or retrieves. In a cluster-randomized study of [[k-12|senior high school]] students in Davao City, structured GenAI use raised both self-regulated learning strategy scores and offloading, but offloading was negatively associated with performance on a two-week delayed retention assessment; the direct effect of AI use on retention was not reliable once offloading was accounted for, suggesting offloading itself is the mechanism.

A mathematics-learning experiment points the same way: GenAI assistance reduced the time students spent on problems and the durable knowledge built from them, a pattern the authors call cognitive surrender rather than classical offloading. A review of digital-technology effects reports convergent behavioral and neural evidence, including altered prefrontal and anterior cingulate activation with heavy reliance on external aids for memory-dependent tasks.

## Better output, thinner process

A second cluster concerns learning processes rather than storage. The review adopts "metacognitive laziness" — habitual avoidance of the effort needed to monitor and regulate one's own learning — and reports that GenAI use reduced engagement in key self-regulated learning processes even where short-term performance improved. That dissociation — better output, weaker process — recurs across the literature.

A large-scale meta-analysis of learning outcomes in [[higher-ed|higher education]] found an overall positive GenAI effect significantly moderated by cognitive level and instructional context: effects were smaller for higher-order skills (analyzing, evaluating, creating) than lower-order ones (remembering, understanding, applying), and substantially larger when use was instructor-guided rather than unstructured. A second meta-analysis found effects across cognitive, competency and affective dimensions moderated by discipline and integration mode.

Field research finds [[conversational-ai|conversational]] GenAI reduces cognitive effort and metacognitive activity relative to search, associated with weaker downstream knowledge and skill acquisition, especially when learners lose the tool after relying on it. Creativity evidence is mixed: a meta-analysis found small consistent output gains alongside reduced idea diversity, a possible homogenizing effect even where quality improves.

## Executive function, persistence, and critical thinking

The most direct neurophysiological evidence comes from an EEG study at the MIT Media Lab comparing [[writing-education|essay writing]] under three conditions: unaided, search-engine-assisted and LLM-assisted. Across 54 participants aged 18–39 from 5 Boston-area universities, connectivity measured by dynamic directed transfer function analysis was strongest in the brain-only group, intermediate under search and weakest under LLM assistance. When LLM users were reassigned to the unaided condition in a fourth, crossover session, they showed under-engagement relative to those who had practiced unaided from the start — accumulated "cognitive debt." The review treats this as provisional, citing critique of sample size, analytic transparency and reproducibility.

A multi-country cross-sectional survey reported a significant negative correlation between GenAI use frequency and [[self-report-measures|self-assessed]] critical thinking, mediated by offloading, with younger and more AI-dependent users lowest. A related cross-country randomized experiment found the relationship is not fixed: prompting protocols requiring users to justify or evaluate AI output reduced offloading and were associated with deeper reflection and higher argument quality. Experimental work on persistence likewise found AI access reduced willingness to persist on later unaided problems and lowered independent performance once help was withdrawn.

## Four boundary conditions

Four moderators recur. First, instructional structure: guided or [[scaffolding|scaffolded]] use, with prompts requiring planning, monitoring and evaluation, produces better learning and lower offloading costs than unstructured use. Second, task complexity: benefits are more reliable for recall and comprehension than for analysis, evaluation and creation, where offloading risk is greatest. Third, mode of offloading, with autonomous use preserving engagement and dependent use or surrender most consistently associated with reduced learning and retention. Fourth, user characteristics: younger and less experienced users show greater vulnerability.

The authors read this as a qualified offloading-cost hypothesis that a metacognitive-control account fits better than technological determinism: active engagement with AI output, not use itself, decides the consequence. They conclude that the umbrella term may be too coarse for the GenAI era, and that the practical question is under what conditions of [[learning-design|task design]], guidance and metacognitive engagement AI supports rather than supplants learning.

## Key Findings

1. Generative AI extends offloading into reasoning, synthesis, evaluation and creative generation — the processes that drive durable learning.
2. In a cluster-randomized high school study, offloading predicted weaker two-week delayed retention while the direct AI effect was not reliable once offloading was accounted for.
3. In a mathematics experiment, AI assistance cut study time and the knowledge built from problems — cognitive surrender rather than classical offloading.
4. A higher-education meta-analysis found positive overall effects that were smaller for higher-order skills and larger under instructor guidance.
5. In a 54-participant EEG study, connectivity was weakest under LLM assistance, and users who returned to unaided writing in a fourth session showed under-engagement — cognitive debt.
6. A multi-country survey found GenAI frequency negatively correlated with self-assessed critical thinking, mediated by offloading and strongest in younger users.
7. Structured prompting that demands justification or evaluation reduced offloading and improved argument quality, showing that interaction design shapes the outcome.

## What this means for practice

- **Require an unaided attempt first.** A student with no draft, estimate or explanation has nothing to compare the model's output against, and the evidence ties weaker retention to that delegation.
- **Build justification into the prompt.** Protocols asking learners to defend or critique an AI answer reduced offloading and improved argument quality, so the requirement belongs in the task, not an honor code.
- **Teach autonomous versus dependent use.** [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] should name the difference between using a model to check one's reasoning and accepting its output as a substitute for it.
- **Schedule AI-free retrieval.** Practice without the tool at intervals, and delayed rather than immediate quizzes, test what offloading leaves unpracticed; [[retrieval-spacing-interleaving|retrieval practice]] is the mechanism the memory findings point to.
- **Grade the process, not only the artifact.** Because output quality and durable learning can diverge, [[assessment-validity|assessment]] should weigh drafts, reasoning steps and verification over the polished product.

## Limitations

- Several key studies rely on self-report measures of critical thinking and offloading tendency, which are susceptible to social-desirability and introspection biases.
- Neurophysiological work uses small samples, limiting statistical power and generalizability.
- Most designs are cross-sectional, survey-based or short-duration experiments, which precludes strong causal claims about long-term cognitive change.
- Operationalizations of "offloading," "dependency" and "metacognitive engagement" vary across studies, complicating comparison and precluding pooling.
- Generative systems evolve quickly, so findings based on earlier model generations may not generalize to current or future tools.
- The review is a narrative synthesis rather than a systematic one, with no record flow or pooled effect sizes, and its evidence base skews toward educational and knowledge-work settings in higher-income countries.
