> **Synthesis:** A survey of 381 full-time students at a technical university in Uzbekistan compared how undergraduates and graduate students report using [[generative-ai|generative AI]] alongside attention, cognitive load, [[motivation|motivation]], [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulation]] and ethical autonomy. Graduate students scored higher on six of seven scales, most sharply on ethics and academic autonomy (d = 1.47) and AI use (d = 1.29); cognitive load did not (p = .296). Ethics (β = 0.446), self-regulation (β = 0.279) and motivation (β = 0.178) predicted the authors' exploratory Neuro-AI Pedagogical Competence scale (R2 = 0.799), but use frequency did not (β = 0.030, p = .399). Academic stage appears to shape how students regulate and justify AI use, not just how often they use it, though overlapping scales keep the model exploratory.

## Key Findings

1. **Graduate students reported far heavier and far more uniform AI use than undergraduates.** The narrow spread suggests habitual, purposeful use rather than occasional experimentation.
2. **Ethical awareness and academic autonomy produced the widest gap between the two groups.** Undergraduate scores were uneven, not uniformly low.
3. **Self-regulation and Neuro-AI pedagogical competence moved together**, with graduate students ahead on both.
4. **Perceived cognitive load was the one construct that did not differ**, so felt overload looks like individual variation rather than a property of program stage.
5. **Attention was the weakest area across the whole sample**, and graduate students reported fewer attentional difficulties than undergraduates on the reverse-coded scale.
6. **Use frequency did not survive as a predictor once ethical awareness entered the model**, which the authors treat as evidence that judgment matters more than exposure.

## A level gap in judgment, not in felt workload

The pattern is distributional, not just about means. Graduate standard deviations on ethics, self-regulation and competence were roughly a third of the undergraduate values, with interquartile ranges compressed into the top of the scale while undergraduates reached the minimum. That heterogeneity matters more than the average: a bachelor's cohort holds students who appraise AI output critically alongside those who report almost none of that disposition.

The cognitive load result is easy to misread as a failure. The scale did not track academic level (p = .296), which the authors read as overload following individual variation rather than program stage; they also concede that it blends perceived overload with AI-assisted understanding, limiting how far the null can be pushed.

## Why frequency of use drops out of the model

Students who use [[llm|AI tools]] more often report stronger [[metacognition|metacognitive]] and ethical profiles, yet once those are in the model, frequency adds nothing. The authors read this through self-determination theory: internalized regulation and ethical commitment, not exposure, co-vary with competence.

The reading needs caution: predictors and outcome come from one self-report instrument with overlapping items. Two items, one on the AI Use scale and one on the competence scale, are near-identical about organizing academic work with AI, and the authors flag this overlap as a likely contributor to R2 = 0.799. What the study supports is descriptive: self-reported [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] in the broad sense clustered with self-regulation and ethics, not usage intensity. It does not support a causal claim that teaching ethics raises competence.

## An instrument still under construction

The competence scale is both contribution and weak link. The prefix signals awareness of one's own attention, memory and load during AI work, not neurological measurement; no neurophysiological data were collected. Of the six items retained, only one directly references attention, memory and learning characteristics; the rest restate AI use and study regulation captured elsewhere. Discriminant validity is unestablished and no confirmatory factor analysis was run.

The reliability reporting is transparent: the 30-item instrument spans seven scales with alpha from 0.717 to 0.895, four items were dropped after principal axis factoring, and retained loadings run from 0.622 to 0.930. As a first operationalization of [[academic-integrity|academic responsibility]] it is useful; as a validated construct it is not there yet.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors:** Treat ethical reasoning as a teachable undergraduate target: undergraduates averaged 3.527 (SD = 1.003) against 4.627; build appraisal tasks around the line between support and substitution.
- **Instructors:** Do not read heavy AI use as competence. Frequency predicted nothing once self-regulation and ethics were controlled, so require planning, monitoring and justification routines, not prompt counts.
- **Designers:** Differentiate AI-integrated tasks by level: scaffolded, structured tasks early, more autonomous ethics-focused tasks at postgraduate level.
- **Administrators:** Fund attention and workload support separately from tool access: digital attention was the lowest scale (M = 2.642) and cognitive load did not differ by level, so buying more licenses fixes neither.
- **Researchers:** Treat the scale as a draft: run confirmatory factor analysis on an independent sample and drop items duplicating the predictors before comparing cohorts.

## Limitations

- Cross-sectional, self-report, single institution: 381 full-time students at one technical university over one semester, compared on a balanced subsample of 302.
- Construct overlap inflates the regression: R2 = 0.799 is association among overlapping scales, not a differentiated model.
- Scale weaknesses: cognitive load mixes overload with AI-assisted understanding, ethics has three items with no behavioral validation, and one attention item needed reverse coding.
- Social desirability likely inflates self-reported ethics on a topic as sensitive as academic integrity, and no performance measure anchors any scale.
- The methods section reports exemption from formal ethics board approval while the ethics statement reports approval; either way, independent instrument review is undocumented.
