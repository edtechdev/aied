> **Synthesis:** Orsava, Ma, Cecchini and Kum (2026) mapped 35 peer-reviewed studies of [[higher-ed|undergraduates]]' direct use of artificial intelligence, published between 2022 and 2024. Use clusters around assignment help, [[language-learning|language learning]] and on-demand support, and students consistently value speed and accessibility. [[motivation|Motivational]] benefits are the most consistent reported outcome, while evidence on [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]] and creativity remains contested. Recurring concerns include inaccurate outputs, [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]], weaker human contact and uneven literacy. The review proposes an AI Engagement Mode Framework in which outcomes depend on whether students direct the tool or delegate to it, and whether it supplements or displaces human instruction. It closes with a research agenda dominated by the need for [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal]] and adequately powered experimental work.

## Key Findings

1. **The evidence base is recent and narrow.** Thirty-five studies published between 2022 and 2024 were included, with 29 of them appearing from 2023 onwards.
2. **Design limits dominate the field.** Twelve of the 35 studies were purely cross-sectional surveys, and 31 of 35 drew on single-institution convenience samples.
3. **Coursework support is the main use.** Idea generation, outlines, grammar correction and research facilitation for homework and written assignments were the most frequently reported applications.
4. **Motivational benefits are the most consistent outcome.** Multiple studies associate AI use with participation, positive academic emotions and engagement, though the measures used vary widely.
5. **Cognitive outcomes remain unsettled.** Some experiments report gains in critical or [[creativity|creative thinking]] and others report no difference; the proposed offloading risk was not directly tested in the included undergraduate samples.
6. **Concerns repeat across contexts.** Inaccuracy and fabricated citations, integrity ambiguities, diminished human interaction and uneven [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] appear throughout the map.
7. **A post-hoc framework reframes the mixed results.** Crossing agentic versus substitutive use with additive versus displacing context yields four engagement modes with different evidence profiles.

## How the review was done and what it mapped

The search was run on 25 November 2024 across Scopus, Web of Science and MEDLINE (Ovid), which returned 518, 265 and 60 journal articles respectively; the [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|PRISMA]] flow describes the starting pool as 843 records. After 221 duplicates were removed (212 by Covidence and nine manually), 622 unique records were screened by four reviewers working in independent pairs. That excluded 446 irrelevant studies and left 176 for full-text review, of which 35 were retained. Data were charted against four questions: patterns of use, factors shaping adoption, educational outcomes, and emergent concerns and gaps. No formal quality appraisal was carried out and no review protocol was registered in advance; the authors follow the stages set out by Arksey and O'Malley and their later refinement by Levac and colleagues.

The 35 studies span more than 20 countries. China and Hong Kong contributed five studies and Turkey four, while the United States and Saudi Arabia contributed three each, South Korea and Ghana two each, and Canada and Australia one each. Sample sizes ranged from seven to 842 students, most commonly between 50 and 200. ChatGPT was examined in over half the studies; the rest considered Gemini, Grammarly, narrative-AI platforms, bespoke educational [[conversational-ai|chatbots]] or 'AI in general'. The authors describe the dominance of cross-sectional [[self-report-measures|self-report]] and single-site convenience sampling as the field's most fundamental constraint.

On adoption, six studies used technology-acceptance frameworks. Two reported significant positive effects of both perceived usefulness and [[technology-acceptance-model|perceived ease of use]] on intention, two found neither to be a direct predictor, and the remaining two used different constructs or frameworks. In one cited study, 40% of students either somewhat or strongly disagreed that they were competent with AI technology. The review notes that broader research outside undergraduate samples suggests women may be approximately 20% less likely to engage with [[generative-ai|generative AI]], but that the two undergraduate studies it found did not show a significant gender difference. Intersectional demographic analyses were absent.

## The engagement mode framework

The AI Engagement Mode Framework is presented as a post-hoc interpretive heuristic derived from cross-cutting patterns, not as a validated theory and not as a guide used during extraction. Its first dimension, cognitive locus, asks whether the student directs the AI as a thinking partner (agentic) or hands over cognitive output (substitutive). Its second dimension, relational context, asks whether the tool supplements human instruction (additive) or replaces it (displacing).

Crossing the two yields four modes. Agentic-additive use, such as practicing writing alongside teaching, shows the most consistent motivational and skill gains. Agentic-displacing use, such as homework help that substitutes for instructor feedback, shows the strongest efficiency benefits but also the strongest integrity concerns. Substitutive-additive use, including institutionally supplied teaching-assistant bots, shows motivational benefits but carries dependency risk. Substitutive-displacing use, where AI generates whole submissions, concentrates the risks of [[cognitive-offloading|cognitive offloading]], integrity violations and weak literacy. The authors argue that policy should scaffold the first mode and signal the risks of the fourth rather than impose blanket restrictions or uncritical promotion.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Ask what students are using a tool for, not merely whether they use one; the same tool can scaffold learning in one mode and replace it in another.
- **Instructional designers.** Build visible thinking and instructor feedback into tasks so that AI supplements teaching rather than displacing it.
- **Institutions.** Pair access with explicit teaching about accuracy, integrity and literacy; the review treats literacy as a foundational requirement, not an optional add-on.
- **Researchers.** Prioritize longitudinal designs, adequately powered experiments lasting at least one term, intersectional analyses and studies of speech-based and [[multimodal]] tools.

## Limitations

- **No formal quality appraisal.** Findings from methodologically weaker studies carry the same weight, and study-level risk of bias was not assessed.
- **Self-report and cross-sectional dominance.** Social desirability bias, short interventions and novelty effects limit what the apparent positive consensus can establish.
- **Coverage gaps.** Only English-language journal articles from three databases were included, with ERIC and PsycINFO explicitly excluded; conference papers and gray literature were out of scope.
- **Bounded time window.** The November 2024 search captures the first wave of peer-reviewed evidence after the public release of ChatGPT and excludes later work.
- **Unvalidated framework.** The four engagement modes were derived after charting and have not been tested independently; the article itself was subject to a correction, noted as 10.3389/feduc.2026.1947574.
