> **Synthesis:** Hansson asks how learners experience an AI-supported vocational education model that combines AI-generated instructional content, solar-powered tablets, offline learning, community-based practical training and entrepreneurship education for disadvantaged young adults in rural Kenya. The evaluation used a qualitative design: 37 semi-structured interviews formed the primary data, supported by descriptive questionnaire responses from 188 students and examination records from 528 unique students. Analysis followed the M-COPE framework, covering mobile affordances, conditions, outcomes, pedagogy and ethics. Learners adapted readily to the technology and combined home study with practical sessions at community learning hubs, and many reported income-generating activity during or shortly after the program; examination records showed a high pass rate. The author is explicit that causal attribution is impossible: there is no baseline measurement, no comparison group and no longitudinal follow-up, and the income figures are self-reported. The study therefore describes a feasible delivery model and how learners experienced it, not the effect of the model. The principal challenges were contextual rather than technological — sharing tablets among several learners, scarce practical equipment, long travel distances and the absence of tutor support.

## Key Findings

- **Access came from the device, not the network.** The solar-powered tablet runs entirely offline, with content loaded by USB and navigation by remote control, so learners in homes without reliable electricity or internet could study at home.
- **Sharing was the main friction.** Each tablet was typically shared with two to four other learners, interrupting continuity, and nearly half of surveyed students named travel to the hub as a major challenge.
- **Learners adapted despite limited prior experience.** Most had little or no previous computer experience, yet reported heavy weekly use of the tablets, and the pace of videos drew more complaint than the technology itself.
- **Examination performance was strong.** Of the 528 students who sat final examinations, 97.3 percent passed and 35 percent achieved the highest grade, though the absence of baseline data limits interpretation.
- **Income gains were self-reported.** Before enrolling, most participants earned little; during or after training, reported monthly incomes rose, but the design cannot attribute the change to participation.
- **Human support did the teaching.** With no employed tutors, peer explanation at the hub became the main way difficult material was clarified, and learners asked for tutor support.

## The model and how it was evaluated

The program ran through a vocational college in Kenya. Each learner received a solar-powered device — a solar panel, three LED light bulbs and a tablet operated by remote control — and course content was transferred by USB, removing any need for internet access. Tutorial videos were conceptualized and produced with the [[generative-ai|generative AI]] platform Elai: prompts specified the procedure to be demonstrated, the intended learning content and the structure of the explanation, and each course comprised 30–50 short videos presented by AI-generated avatars, produced first in English and translated into Kiswahili. Selected YouTube videos supplemented them, and local staff reviewed all materials for vocational relevance and local appropriateness before release. Learners then practiced skills through hands-on training at community learning hubs, which also served as the social setting for peer support.

The evaluation used purposive sampling across learners recently enrolled, currently studying and finished with their examinations. Thirty-seven semi-structured interviews, individual or in pairs and lasting about 20–30 minutes, were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim; thematic analysis combined deductive coding against the five M-COPE dimensions with inductive coding. An 18-item questionnaire collected demographic, participation and self-report outcome data from 188 students, and aggregated examination records covered 528 unique students across two cohorts. The quantitative material was descriptive only: no hypothesis testing and no causal estimation. The study sits at the intersection of [[vocational-education|vocational education]], [[video-education|video-based instruction]] and [[digital-divide|access to technology]] in a low-resource setting.

## Conditions: sharing, distance and cost

All participants lived in rural communities with limited access to secondary schools, vocational colleges, qualified technical teachers and employment. Eighty-eight percent visited the learning hubs daily during opening hours or weekly; the remaining 12 percent came monthly because of work or household responsibilities such as fetching water. Nearly half identified travel as a major challenge, and 17 percent lived 10–20 km or more from the training center. Practical sessions depended on hub equipment, and shortages of tools, materials and workspace constrained hands-on learning. Students also asked for more opportunities to practice their vocational skills outside supervised sessions.

Free tuition was repeatedly described as a decisive advantage, since nearby institutions charged fees beyond most participants' means; participation nonetheless carried an opportunity cost, as many reduced time in casual employment while attending. Questionnaire responses indicated heavy engagement: 13 percent used the tablets 1–5 hours per week, 20 percent 5–10 hours, 33 percent 10–15 hours, 7 percent 15–20 hours and 27 percent more than 20 hours. Female students made up 73 percent of the examination cohort and often described the program as a route to security for their families. The author treats these conditions as part of the intervention rather than background, since technology alone did not remove the need for equipment, practice, peers or instructional support.

## Outcomes: examinations, income and skills

Participants associated the training with confidence, self-employment and improved household conditions. Seventy-three percent expected to earn a higher income after completing the course. Reported earnings shifted upward: before enrolling, about 30 percent earned between USD 10 and 25 per month and only around 10 percent reported monthly earnings up to USD 40; after, or in some cases before, completing the training, 20 percent remained in the lowest band, 60 percent reported USD 25–40 and 20 percent USD 40–80. Not all skills became income: 32 percent said they applied their vocational skills mainly for household purposes, with pastry graduates the largest group. Entry barriers differed by specialization — hairdressing and pastry allowed quick self-employment, while tailoring required a sewing machine and materials before a business could start.

The examination records cover the 528 students who sat final assessments: 97.3 percent passed and 35 percent achieved the highest grade, with difficulty concentrated in the ICT course. Most had completed secondary education, but 28 percent had only primary-level education and 5 percent had no formal schooling. The author cautions that these records describe completers who participated in assessment and cannot establish that the program caused the results. The [[summative-assessment|examination data]] and the [[self-report-measures|self-reported income]] are complementary rather than confirmatory, and neither was analyzed inferentially.

## Where the model strained

Several pedagogical problems recurred. Some materials were too theoretical or abstract for learners with only primary education, and students asked for tutors who could explain difficult concepts; the model deliberately employed none, so peers filled the gap. Video pace drew repeated complaint, and the institution held limited information about learners beyond age and prior schooling, so materials could not always be matched to varying needs. Content was not always adapted to local conditions: the pastry course assumed an electric oven while many learners cooked over open fires. Learners also enrolled in several courses at once, treating vocational competence as a portfolio rather than preparation for a single occupation, and some women took plumbing alongside pastry.

Ethically, the offline design minimized privacy risk: learners could not upload files, install applications or store personal data, so nothing identifiable remained when a tablet changed hands, and no participant raised privacy concerns. The ethical issues were instead unequal access — too few tablets, uneven equipment, distance and absent tutors. The author also notes that free tuition and novel devices may have produced a Hawthorne effect, and that high female participation does not by itself demonstrate gender-equitable outcomes. For [[adult-learning|adult learners]] in this context, [[self-directed-learning|self-direction]] and [[collaborative-learning|peer learning]] carried much of the instructional load.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Pair AI-generated video with supervised practical sessions at a community hub; in this model the hub and its peer culture did much of the teaching.
- **Instructional designers.** Localize generated content before release: the pastry course assumed an electric oven while many learners cooked over open fires.
- **Administrators.** Plan for device sharing and travel: most learners shared one tablet with two to four others, and nearly half named the journey to the hub as a major barrier.
- **Teachers.** Structure peer explanation when no tutor is employed; learners in this program already taught one another at the hub.
- **Researchers.** Add baseline measures and a comparison group before attributing gains to a program; income changes here were self-reported.

## Limitations

- A single program across geographically diverse regions, with no baseline measurement, comparison group or longitudinal follow-up, so causal claims are impossible.
- Free tuition and novel solar devices may have produced a Hawthorne effect, biasing accounts toward the favorable.
- Only a limited set of vocational courses, whose practical training depended on hub equipment, so findings may not transfer to other specializations.
- Only active learners and completers were studied; the experiences of those who dropped out are unknown.
- Income and outcome data were self-reported and descriptive, providing little evidence about long-term sustainability.
