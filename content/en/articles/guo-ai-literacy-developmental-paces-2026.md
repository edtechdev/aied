> **Synthesis:** [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] research has produced dozens of frameworks and little agreement on what the construct contains, and Guo, Ali and Wong argue that much of the disagreement is developmental. Their integrative review screened 2,745 records and analyzed 118, sorting the frameworks into nine categories and 35 subcategories, then reading three shifts across them: toward the human attributes of the learner, toward frameworks built from learners' own conceptions as well as expert synthesis, and toward interdisciplinary work that carries higher-order thinking. From the same evidence they propose PACES — Progressions Across Cognition, Ethics, and Skills — which maps ten developmental considerations onto four school bands and treats psychosocial enablers as conditions for moving between them. Their audit of the K–12 instrument set finds nine validated measures, one of them for early childhood, and none that captures the process of working with AI.

*An integrative review synthesizes published studies rather than collecting new primary data. Its claims describe the reviewed literature, and the framework it proposes is a provisional roadmap the authors ask others to test and localize.*

## Key Findings

1. Searches returned 3,992 records; after 1,244 duplicates and 3 erroneous exports were removed, 2,745 were screened and 118 included — 83 journal articles (70.34%), 31 conference papers (26.27%) and 4 reports (3.39%), all published since 2016 inside a 2014–2024 window. Studies that used AI to improve subject outcomes (236) or focused on educators (23) were excluded.
2. Inductive content analysis produced nine categories and 35 subcategories, read as three shifts: human-centered reorientation, bidirectional construction of frameworks, and interdisciplinary rebalancing.
3. The human-centered shift moves from technical content to learner attributes. Psychological frameworks treat AI literacy as a unidimensional predictor of AI readiness and confidence, and then of intention to use AI; the review finds AI readiness conceptually unstable and the measures mostly short self-reports.
4. The second shift pits expert synthesis against local calibration. In early childhood and primary education, implementable, context-rich frameworks are rare and most remain top-down and conceptual.
5. The third threads higher-order thinking through subjects and adjacent literacies, but it carries a secondary-school bias; primary education is largely untouched.
6. Ten developmental considerations distribute across four bands, with mapped evidence of 5 studies for early childhood to lower primary, 27 for upper primary, 26 for lower secondary and 11 for upper secondary. PACES maps them onto cognition, ethics and skills; attitudes, the human–AI relationship, autonomy and self-efficacy, and agency and metacognition are psychosocial enablers, not literacy content.
7. Nine validated instruments exist for K–12. Coverage tilts toward secondary settings, AI4KGA is the only early-childhood tool, no fully validated primary measure exists, and none captures process: three are multiple-choice knowledge tests under item response theory, the rest self-report scales under classical test theory.

## Three shifts in how the field frames AI literacy

The earliest frameworks supplied a technical spine — canonical AI topics and terminology aligned to textbooks or standards — which later compressed into the five big ideas and then into user-facing competencies centered on everyday practice and misconceptions. Practitioners working with younger children prefer the compact three-dimensional frameworks, because those translate content into what learners should know, how they might use it, and why ethical or safe practice matters. The triad recurs, but labels such as concepts, knowledge or cognitive aspects carry different indicators from one framework to the next.

The psychological turn explains why confidence is so often mistaken for competence. Frameworks built on the theory of planned behavior, the technology acceptance model and self-determination theory make AI literacy a background predictor of AI readiness, confidence and perceived behavioral control, which then predict intention; the review reads that literature as suggestive, because AI readiness oscillates between attitude and behavioral control and blurs into self-efficacy. Bloom's taxonomy supplied the cognitive ladder, but ethics sits partly outside its levels, which encourages treating ethical reasoning as an afterthought. Computational-thinking and digital-literacy frameworks pull [[ai-literacy]] toward human–tool interaction while leaving the boundaries among the three literacies unclear.

The second shift sets expert synthesis against local calibration. Context-specific frameworks bifurcate into top-down models and locally adapted university–school partnerships, the second concentrated in secondary schooling. Bottom-up studies start from children's conceptions and show how much of the construct sits in the human–AI relationship: children engage through play, treat AI as a [[student-ai-interaction|social companion]], attribute limited empathy while imagining human-like emotions, and voice ethical concerns that call for oversight. The third shift rebalances AI literacy across subjects and adjacent literacies with higher-order thinking threaded through, but the evidence carries a secondary-school bias, and primary education is largely untouched.

## Ten considerations across four bands

The review uses four educational bands as a pragmatic structure, not rigid age cutoffs, and derives ten considerations that change across them, each grounded in what [[developmental-psychology|developmental research]] says the band can support.

In the earliest band, learning is tied to sensory experience and social interaction through play-based, project-based and embodied designs — storytelling, robots, LEGO, Leap Motion, and non-programming tools such as Google Teachable Machine. Story-based approaches build conceptual understanding and positive attitudes, and drawing works as a developmentally sensitive assessment because children can express understanding through narrative imagining before formal technical language.

Upper primary adds familiar preconceptions: students arrive with media-shaped, fragmentary notions and readily anthropomorphize AI, so embodied [[metaphors-and-analogies|analogy]] and unplugged role play — students rotating through developer, tester and AI roles — meet them where they are. Tangible artifact creation follows through block-based programming in Scratch or MIT App Inventor. Many learners still struggle to reach Bloom's evaluate and create levels, so reflection and critique must be staged rather than assumed; [[collaborative-learning|peer discussion]] and structured dilemma discussion extend the band into [[ethics]].

Lower secondary shifts to participatory simulation: students enact AI workflows and rotate through development roles, with selected complexities deliberately black-boxed to avoid overload. Artifact work moves from making something with AI to interpreting and improving an AI-enabled system, and collaborative interdisciplinary settings are where autonomy and motivation rise.

Upper secondary organizes around agency and critique — youth-participatory action research, co-design and critique of ethics frameworks, and team projects on situated community problems that pair computational thinking with inquiry into social consequences. The authors' summary is a rhythm, not a ladder: build intuition early, stage critique deliberately, open technical complexity as autonomy and collaboration rise.

## Psychosocial enablers as conditions, not content

PACES separates four learner-side conditions from the literacy itself. Attitudes toward AI appear as early as early childhood and plausibly sustain engagement. The human–AI relationship becomes salient in primary school, where anthropomorphism shapes emotions and ethical stances before children can evaluate output; the review treats epistemological understanding and **epistemic vigilance** — error detection, reasoning verification, source cross-referencing — as the lever at that stage. [[self-efficacy|Autonomy and self-efficacy]] rise as learners collaborate with humans and AI in interdisciplinary settings, and [[agency]] and [[metacognition]] matter most at secondary level, where learners are positioned as creators. The review cites evidence that students often rely on AI rather than learn from it, and that literacy improves when they decide when and how to use it, appraise task demands, delegate deliberately and own the outcome. Enablers are stage-relevant conditions for progression, not interchangeable indicators of literacy.

## What the validated instruments can and cannot show

Nine validated instruments span early childhood to upper secondary, and the distribution is the first finding: most target secondary settings, AI4KGA is the only early-childhood tool, and primary settings rely on short adapted scales whose few items constrain construct coverage. Operationalization varies with where scholars place attitudes — folded into literacy, or treated as adjacent constructs such as self-efficacy — and the choice cascades into measurement: attitudinal constructs are self-reported and analyzed with classical test theory, content-oriented ones often tested with [[item-response-theory|item response theory]].

Curriculum aims and assessment choices also part ways. The AI-CI instrument covers technical concepts only, while its curriculum measures self-reported attitudes and career interest with scales adapted from non-AI contexts. In the AIKEE line only the knowledge instrument was newly developed. Four of the nine instruments did incorporate student interviews during development, and AI4KGA used visual illustrations and assessor-read items to reduce reading confounds.

The gap pressed hardest is process: no validated K–12 instrument captures how learners use, interrogate and revise with AI. The recommendations follow — anchor instruments to a more discriminating ladder such as SOLO rather than Bloom, which blurs creating with AI and creating AI; add [[process-oriented-assessment|process evidence]] through analytic rubrics and evidence-centered design, as the DRIVE framework does for dialogic interaction; assess ethics through scenarios scored on choice and justification rather than agreement with abstract principles; and build psychosocial items from lived interaction rather than adapting psychological scales wholesale.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Match the task to the band rather than to the tool: sensory and story-based work early, analogy and artifact making in upper primary, simulation and structured critique in lower secondary, team projects with explicit accountability in upper secondary.
- **Curriculum designers.** Sequence AI literacy across years instead of adding a standalone module, and embed ethics in the tasks rather than appending a unit on principles. The evidence for higher-order work concentrates in secondary settings, which argues for staging critique earlier rather than deferring it.
- **Assessment designers.** Pair [[self-report-measures|self-report]] with process evidence — interaction rubrics, revision logs, evidence-centered tasks — and score ethical reasoning on the justification, not only the choice. For young children, prefer drawing, observation and teacher ratings to reading-heavy items.
- **School and program leaders.** Treat PACES as a provisional roadmap, not a validated scope-and-sequence. Its upper rungs assume device-rich secondary provision and its earliest rungs rest on the thinnest evidence, so local piloting with observable indicators is the honest first step.

## Limitations

- The evidence window closes in December 2024, so the reviewed studies predate the generative-AI tools that dominate current K–12 practice.
- No study in the reviewed literature follows the same learners across educational levels. PACES describes an ordering inferred from different cohorts, not a demonstrated developmental trajectory.
- The evidence is uneven: the mapped studies run 5, 27, 26 and 11 across the four bands, and validated instruments are scarcer still — one early-childhood tool and no fully validated primary-level measure.
- PACES is a design proposal. The authors call it a provisional roadmap and invite localization and testing; the review reports no trial of the framework itself.
