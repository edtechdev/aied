> **Synthesis:** Guo, Ali and Wong read 118 studies of [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] in [[k-12|K–12]] and conclude that much of the disagreement about what the term means is really a disagreement about the learner. A framework written for a second grader and one written for a senior differ in kind: the first should be sensory, narrative and playful, while the second should involve judging output, weighing consequences and owning decisions. The review sorts the field into nine categories and 35 subcategories, reads three shifts across them — toward the human attributes of the learner, toward frameworks built with students as well as experts, and toward interdisciplinary work that carries higher-order thinking — and turns the result into PACES, a ten-consideration progression across four school bands. It also audits the instruments used to measure the construct: nine validated measures exist for K–12, one for early childhood, and none captures the process of working with AI.

## Key Findings

1. **AI literacy changes in kind with the learner, not only in difficulty.** [[early-childhood-elementary-ai-education|Early-childhood work]] builds on sensory play and stories, while upper-secondary work builds on critique, collaboration and accountability for decisions made with AI.
2. **The field has moved from AI content to the learner.** Recent frameworks center what students can do, think and feel about AI, and psychological models increasingly fold confidence and attitudes into literacy rather than treating them as separate outcomes.
3. **Most frameworks are written from the top down.** Expert syntheses dominate early-childhood and primary education, where implementable, classroom-tested designs are rare; studies that start from children's own ideas surface play, anthropomorphism and the human–AI relationship.
4. **Ethics is too often bolted on.** In the common knowledge-use-ethics triad and in Bloom-based ladders, ethics sits beside or after the cognitive levels, which invites teaching it as a final unit instead of as part of every judgment.
5. **The teaching moves shift by band.** Sensory and story-based work early; analogy, unplugged role play and tangible artifacts in upper primary; simulation and structured critique in lower secondary; team projects with real audiences and accountability in upper secondary.
6. **Attitudes, autonomy and metacognition are enablers, not the construct.** The review keeps them separate from literacy itself — as conditions that help a learner move to the next level, not as evidence of what the learner can do.
7. **The instruments cannot see process.** Nine validated K–12 measures exist, three are knowledge tests and the rest self-report; none captures how a student questions, revises or delegates to AI, and ethics is measured by agreement with abstract principles.

## What changes with the learner

The review's developmental argument is not that younger children need simpler AI content. It is that the capacities a task loads change: a five-year-old works through symbolic play and sensory experience, a nine-year-old through concrete mechanisms, a fifteen-year-old through systems and consequences. [[developmental-psychology|Developmental research]] therefore constrains what a task can ask, and [[metacognition|metacognition]] is the clearest example — self-assessment accuracy, a capacity most instruments assume, is still forming in the early grades.

Two consequences follow for practice. First, [[student-ai-interaction|children's relationship with AI]] is part of the curriculum, not a distraction from it: primary students readily treat a chatbot as a social companion before they can evaluate what it says, so epistemic vigilance has to be taught alongside the tool. Second, a single district-wide standard mis-serves both ends of the range. The band-specific evidence is also thinnest exactly where the stakes are highest: five studies cover early childhood to lower primary, against 27 for upper primary.

## The progression, band by band

The teaching moves the review derives are concrete. In the earliest band, [[storytelling-in-education|stories]], drawing and sensory play carry the concepts, and drawing doubles as assessment because a child can show understanding before writing it. Upper primary keeps the [[embodied-learning|embodied]] and playful register but adds [[metaphors-and-analogies|analogy]] — comparing human and AI capacities — and unplugged role play in which students take the parts of developer, tester and AI, then build tangible artifacts with block-based tools.

Lower secondary moves to participatory simulation: students enact an AI workflow, with selected complexity deliberately black-boxed to avoid overload, then interpret and improve an AI-enabled system rather than just use it. Upper secondary organizes around [[agency|agency]] — youth-participatory research, critique of ethics frameworks, and [[project-based-learning|team projects]] on real community problems. Across all four bands the authors' summary is a rhythm, not a ladder: build intuition early, stage critique deliberately, and open technical complexity as autonomy and [[collaborative-learning|collaboration]] rise. [[scaffolding|Scaffolding]] shrinks as the band rises, but it never disappears.

## Where the assessment falls short

The instrument audit is the part of the review a program leader should read first. Nine validated measures span the bands, most of them secondary, and only one serves early childhood; primary settings rely on short adapted scales. The three knowledge tests use [[item-response-theory|item response theory]], while the attitudinal and self-reported measures use classical test theory — a split that follows from where each framework puts attitudes, not from what each instrument can establish.

None of the nine captures process: how a learner steers, questions and revises with AI. [[self-report-measures|Self-report]] dominates, and the [[assessment-validity|validity]] limits are the familiar ones — construct under-representation, social desirability, and self-judgment that outruns the learner's metacognitive development. The review's remedies point [[process-oriented-assessment|assessment]] toward performance evidence, such as rubrics scoring the dialogue and revision rather than the final artifact; toward scenario-based ethics tasks scored on reasoning as well as choice; and toward psychosocial items written from learners' actual interactions instead of adapted adult scales.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Plan AI literacy by developmental band rather than by tool: story and sensory work early, analogy and making in upper primary, simulation and critique in lower secondary, team projects with real audiences later. The point is what learners can do with support, not withholding AI until a grade level.
- **Curriculum designers.** Sequence AI literacy across years in the [[curriculum-design|curriculum]] instead of adding a standalone module, and put [[ethics]] inside the tasks rather than in a closing unit. The evidence for higher-order work concentrates in secondary settings, which argues for staging critique earlier, not deferring it.
- **Assessment designers.** Pair self-report with process evidence — dialogue rubrics, revision logs, evidence-centered tasks — and score ethical reasoning on the justification, not only the choice. For young children, prefer drawing, observation and teacher ratings to reading-heavy items.
- **Educational software developers.** Do not ship a single "AI literacy score." Stage hints and scaffolds by band, log the exchanges that show how a student questioned or revised output, and report process evidence separately from confidence.
- **Administrators.** Treat PACES as a provisional roadmap, not a validated scope-and-sequence. Its upper rungs assume device-rich secondary provision and its earliest rungs rest on the thinnest evidence, so fund a primary pilot with observable indicators before adopting a district-wide sequence.

## Limitations

- The search window closes in December 2024, so the reviewed studies predate the current generation of [[generative-ai|generative-AI]] tools.
- No study in the reviewed literature follows the same learners across educational levels. The four-band progression is an ordering inferred from different cohorts, not a demonstrated trajectory.
- The evidence is uneven: the mapped studies run 5, 27, 26 and 11 across the bands, and validated instruments are scarcer still — one early-childhood tool and no fully validated primary-level measure.
- PACES is a design proposal. The authors call it a provisional roadmap and invite localization and testing; the review reports no trial of the framework itself.
