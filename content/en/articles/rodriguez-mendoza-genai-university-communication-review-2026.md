> **Synthesis:** Rodriguez-Mendoza and colleagues reviewed 20 studies of [[generative-ai]] in university institutional communication, separating deployed services from prototypes, surveys, and conceptual proposals. Chatbots and virtual assistants, content generation, and admissions guidance were the most frequently coded applications, while explicit [[ai-use-disclosure]] appeared in only one study under the review's dedicated transparency code and stakeholder [[trust]] was directly coded in only two. The review finds support for context-dependent use in university information services but no general evidence that AI improves institutional trust or reputation. Most appraisal judgments were Unclear because [[primary-education|primary]] reports lacked methodological detail. The authors argue that technical accuracy, service experience, perceived message credibility, and trust in the university are distinct outcomes that should not be pooled into a single claim of effectiveness.

## Key Findings
1. **The corpus was small and heterogeneous.** Twenty studies survived a multi-stage selection process spanning 2022 to the October 2026 search cutoff.
2. **Conversational systems dominated the applications.** Chatbots and assistants were coded most often, followed by institutional content generation and admissions guidance.
3. **Implementation status varied widely.** Only two studies were coded as verified real-world implementations; others were pilots, prototypes, self-reports, or proposals.
4. **Governance was unevenly reported.** Source checking, [[human-in-the-loop-ai|human review]], and institutional guidance recurred, while accountability allocation was not coded in any study.
5. **Disclosure evidence is easy to overread.** One study documented explicit public-facing disclosure; a second recommended transparency without documenting an implemented workflow.
6. **Trust evidence was narrow.** Only two studies were coded as directly measuring stakeholder trust, and neither established a general causal gain in university reputation.
7. **Reporting quality limited inference.** Most domain-level appraisal judgments were Unclear, reflecting incomplete descriptions rather than confirmed methodological failures.

## What the review searched and coded

Four Scopus and Web of Science batches run on October 26, 2026 returned 533 records before deduplication. After removing 86 duplicates (74 identified by Covidence and 12 manually), 447 records were screened by title and abstract and 418 were excluded. All 29 reports sought for full-text review were retrieved; nine were excluded, leaving 20 unique studies. Stored screening decisions showed complete agreement between the two recorded reviewers at both title/abstract and full-text stages, with Cohen's kappa of 1.00, although the authors caution that these votes cannot establish blinding or timing.

The review used a 37-field extraction template and a review-specific ten-domain appraisal covering aim–design alignment, sampling, source traceability, [[assessment-validity|measurement validity]], analytical rigor, GenAI identification, outcomes, transparency, [[ethics]], and proportionality of conclusions. Each of the 20 studies received 10 consensus judgments, for 200 in total: 63 were Yes (31.5%), 16 No (8.0%), 109 Unclear (54.5%), and 12 Not applicable (6.0%). Unclear outnumbered other ratings in several domains, most notably measurement and coding validity, where no study received a Yes. The tool is explicitly not presented as a validated risk-of-bias instrument, and no summed quality score was used.

The corpus included eight design-science or system-evaluation studies, five cross-sectional [[quantitative-research|quantitative]] studies, four content or document analyses, and one each of experimental or quasi-experimental, [[qualitative-research|qualitative]], and comparative observational work. Application categories were non-exclusive: chatbots and virtual assistants appeared in 11 studies (55.0%), institutional content generation in nine (45.0%), admissions information and guidance in eight (40.0%), marketing and public relations in six (30.0%), and personalized messaging in six (30.0%). Implementation-status codes ranged from two verified real-world implementations and four pilots to four laboratory prototypes, five [[self-report-measures|self-reported]] or website-reported cases, two conceptual proposals, and two other categories.

## What the outcomes actually show

Reported governance mechanisms included accuracy verification or source attribution in seven studies, human review in six, institutional policies in six, AI-use disclosure or labeling in two, privacy and [[privacy|data protection]] in four, and bias or fairness safeguards in one; no study was coded for accountability or responsibility allocation. The review stresses that a coded mention is not evidence of implementation. The one documented public-facing disclosure concerned an operational university library assistant that self-identified, referenced sources, and referred users to human [[librarians]]. An admissions [[conversational-ai|dialogue system]] reported 1012 unique users and 12,940 messages in a graph-grounded service, but the review preserves a discrepancy in the primary paper's generator description rather than assigning uncertain benchmark results. An EduChat result of 97.69% concerned an intent classifier, not the correctness of generated answers.

The two studies coded as directly examining stakeholder trust differed substantially. In an experiment with 120 participants, human-created promotional material scored higher than AI-generated material on immediate perceived trust (4.38 vs. 3.52; t(118) = 6.96, p < 0.001; Cohen's d = 1.27); engagement and accessibility perceptions also favored human text, while stated conversion intention did not differ (p = 0.164). A survey of AI-assisted institutional emails measured recipient reactions and reading intentions in a nonexperimental design. A social-media study reported lower mean engagement for AI-assisted content (2711.89) than for other content (2782.95) with a non-significant t-test, and its platform subtotals conflicted with its methods section. The review therefore draws no pooled engagement or reputation conclusion.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Teach students that a fluent institutional answer is not automatically an authoritative one; show how to trace claims to official sources.
- **Instructors.** Use the distinction between message credibility and institutional trust when discussing AI-generated communications with students.
- **Administrators.** Restrict assistants to version-controlled official sources and document citation coverage before extending them to higher-stakes guidance.
- **Administrators.** Disclose automated assistance and test a human escalation path, following the operational library case rather than relying on policy statements alone.
- **Researchers.** Measure factuality, accessibility, privacy, and validated trust separately, ideally in [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal]] or randomized field evaluations.

## Limitations
- Identification used only Scopus and Web of Science and journal publications, so conference-first work, preprints, and institutional reports may be missing.
- The review was not prospectively registered, and no prespecified public protocol is available.
- Original Web of Science session history and the 200-record export were not archived; a later rerun returned 21 and 202 records instead of the recorded 21 and 200.
- The ten-domain appraisal is review-specific rather than a validated bias instrument, and supporting-text fields in consensus records were blank.
- Heterogeneous designs and outcomes precluded a [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|meta-analytic]] effect estimate, and 2026 publication counts were incomplete at the search cutoff.
- Several primary studies had unresolved source-level problems, including conflicting generator descriptions, very low or negative composite alphas, and inconsistent platform subtotals.
