> **Synthesis:** Du and colleagues (2026) ask what sits between access to [[generative-ai]] and productive second-[[language-learning|language learning]], and their answer puts [[student-engagement]] at the center. Surveying 1,160 Chinese [[higher-ed|university]] students across seven institutions, they model the [[community-of-inquiry]] presences — social, cognitive and teaching — against GenAI use for language learning, then trace forward through speaking [[self-efficacy]] and engagement to [[metacognition|metacognitive regulation]]. Social and cognitive presence were associated with GenAI use; teaching presence was not, under the [[primary-education|primary]] estimator. GenAI use was strongly associated with engagement and moderately with speaking self-efficacy, and engagement carried by far the larger association with regulation. A configurational analysis pointed the same way from a different direction: every pathway to high metacognitive regulation included high engagement, whatever the level of GenAI use. The authors read this as a corrective to access-focused accounts, since more opportunity to interact with AI says little about whether learners regulate the interaction.

## Key Findings
- **Social and cognitive presence were associated with GenAI use; teaching presence was not.** The primary estimator found no significant path from teaching presence, though an alternative estimator did, and the authors report both.
- **GenAI use tracked engagement far more strongly than self-efficacy.** The path to engagement was roughly twice the path to speaking self-efficacy, and engagement in turn was the strongest predictor of regulation.
- **GenAI use had no direct association with metacognitive regulation.** Its association ran through engagement and self-efficacy rather than directly.
- **Every configuration for high regulation included high engagement.** The set-theoretic analysis found two pathways — GenAI use with engagement, and self-efficacy with engagement — both centered on the same condition.
- **The three presences moved together.** Correlations among social, cognitive and teaching presence were very high, and a second-order model fit the data nearly as well, so the presences are better read as one perceived learning climate than as independent inputs.
- **The model explained far more of GenAI use than of regulation.** Most of the variance in use was accounted for; regulation was explained largely through engagement.

## What the model connected

The measurement model put seven constructs into one structural model: the three CoI presences, GenAI use for L2 learning, L2 speaking self-efficacy, student engagement and metacognitive regulation tendency, all [[self-report-measures|self-reported]] on multi-item scales with reliabilities above 0.90. Fit was acceptable for the structural model and good for the measurement model, with one caveat the authors state plainly: the presences were so highly correlated (0.893 to 0.929) that discriminant validity failed for five construct pairs.

The structural estimates are large. Social presence predicted GenAI use at β = 0.519 and cognitive presence at 0.284, while teaching presence's estimate of 0.124 was not significant. GenAI use predicted speaking self-efficacy at 0.405 and engagement at 0.796. Self-efficacy and engagement then predicted regulation at 0.267 and 0.600 respectively, and the direct path from GenAI use to regulation was 0.050 and non-significant. The model accounted for 80.9% of variance in GenAI use, 63.3% in engagement and 59.6% in regulation.

## What the configurational analysis added

Fuzzy-set [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] comparative analysis asks a different question from SEM: not how much one variable moves another, but which combinations of conditions are consistently present when the outcome is high. For high GenAI use, the intermediate solution was a conjunction of all three presences, with cases clustered where all three were high or all three were low. For high regulation, two configurations appeared, both requiring high engagement.

The convergence is the useful part. Two methods with different assumptions both put engagement at the center, and the configurational result shows that high regulation occurred alongside different combinations of GenAI use and self-efficacy, so long as engagement was high. The authors caution that the two configurations overlap substantially and should be read as related patterns around engagement rather than as separate psychological pathways.

## Where the inference stops

The survey ran as four independent cross-sectional waves of different students, not a panel, so nothing here tracks change over time. Participant-level institution, class and wave identifiers were not retained, which prevented clustering analyses, tests of measurement invariance across institutions, and any check for repeated participation. Construct overlap and shared method variance are substantial, and the authors note that a single-factor model fit the data poorly while an equal-loading method factor improved fit slightly.

The practical consequence is that the study describes a plausible ordering of perceived conditions and reported tendencies, not a mechanism. A student who reports an engaged, socially connected GenAI-supported course also reports more regulation; that is consistent with the CoI account and also with a simpler explanation in which students who are generally more engaged answer both sets of items similarly.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Treat the AI-supported language task as a social and cognitive activity, not a private practice drill. The presences that tracked GenAI use were the ones about shared inquiry, not the ones about [[teacher-role|teacher]] direction.
- **Curriculum designers.** Build engagement into the task before adding the tool. In both analyses, engagement was the condition that accompanied regulation at every level of GenAI use.
- **Language program leaders.** Measure how students use the tool, not only whether they have access. Access was nearly universal in this sample and explained little about regulation.
- **Researchers.** Report discriminant validity when modeling multiple perceived presences. Here the constructs overlapped enough that a second-order specification fit nearly as well as the seven-factor model.

## Limitations

- Self-reported data from four independent cross-sectional survey waves; the findings concern perceived experiences and statistical associations, not observed interaction or change over time.
- Individual records of GenAI use — frequency, duration, platform, interaction mode — were not available.
- Participant-level institution and wave identifiers were not retained, so clustering, invariance across institutions and possible repeated participation could not be examined.
- Construct overlap was substantial: the three presences correlated 0.893 to 0.929, discriminant validity failed for five construct pairs, and a method factor improved fit.
- The teaching-presence path depended on the estimator, which the authors report rather than resolve.
