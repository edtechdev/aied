> **Synthesis:** Nuzzo and De Marco report a [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] single-case study of one [[secondary-education|secondary-school]] [[humanities-education|humanities]] teacher, "Elena", who spent three weeks designing a literary interpretation activity on Edoardo Sanguineti's Laborintus. Three semi-structured interviews, a [[reflection|reflective journal]], process observations and five successive lesson-plan versions were analyzed with reflexive thematic analysis. The AI's fluent reading of the poem generalized its distinctive features and invoked Surrealism, which prompted the teacher to notice that the explanation could have fitted Montale or Svevo; she reframed the output as an object of analysis and added a worksheet section asking students "What did the AI miss?". Three dimensions emerged: pedagogical dissonance and reflective reframing, situated [[tpack|TPACK]] reconfiguration, and asymmetrical human–AI co-agency in which the teacher retains epistemic and pedagogical responsibility. Student learning outcomes were not measured, and the authors present the themes as contextual analytical constructions rather than causal effects.

## Key Findings

- **Fluency masked disciplinary thinness.** The AI's accessible interpretation of Laborintus described identity crisis, fragmentation and alienation in terms the teacher recognized could apply to other authors.
- **Dissonance drove redesign.** The gap between rhetorical effectiveness and hermeneutic precision — the authors' Perfection Paradox — led the teacher to turn the output into a critical-evaluation task.
- **TPACK shifted from tool use to evaluation.** Initially the teacher used AI to generate explanations (technological knowledge); recognizing its limits required content knowledge, and turning the response into a comparison task activated pedagogical knowledge.
- **Co-agency stayed asymmetrical.** The system proposed alternatives while the teacher evaluated, adapted, accepted or rejected them and kept decision-making authority.
- **The worksheet made the shift visible.** Adding "What did the AI miss?" to the teaching materials turned interpretive limitation into a structured activity for students.
- **Change was documented, not measured.** Successive lesson plans show revisions, but the authors caution that a new activity does not demonstrate lasting conceptual change, and no student learning was assessed.

## The case and its evidence

An exploratory qualitative single-case study examined a secondary-school humanities teacher designing an activity for final-year students on Sanguineti's Laborintus, a work chosen for linguistic and intertextual complexity that makes the gap between a fluent response and disciplinary relevance observable. The corpus comprised three semi-structured interviews (initial, intermediate, final), a reflective journal, process observations, and five successive versions of the lesson plan. Analysis moved recursively between materials, research questions and interpretations, treating episodes where a generative output was linked to a teacher evaluation or plan revision as the analytical units. The participant is identified by a pseudonym and the evidence concerns the design process rather than student assessment.

## From dissonance to a new task

The teacher asked for an accessible analysis of a passage, highlighting its main themes, explaining why the language seems difficult, and whether the author criticizes contemporary society or expresses individual distress. The response invoked an identity crisis, fragmentation and alienation, attributed the difficulty to mixed registers and cultural references, and connected the poem to Surrealism. On rereading, the teacher found that many observations could have applied to Montale or Svevo; she judged that AI "correctly describes the text’s complexity, but it does not really seem to explain how that complexity is constructed linguistically". The contrast between rhetorical effectiveness and hermeneutic precision is called, for this case, the Perfection Paradox. Rather than correcting the output, she redesigned around it, adding the question "What did the AI miss?" and asking students to identify omissions, question generalizations and formulate alternative readings grounded in textual evidence.

## TPACK and the asymmetry of co-agency

The [[tpack|TPACK framework]] describes technology integration through technological, pedagogical and content knowledge; Celik's Intelligent-TPACK extends it to AI. The authors use TPACK restructuring to mean a situated reconfiguration of relationships among those domains, not a psychometric gain. Initially the teacher used AI to produce explanations (TK); recognizing generalization required content knowledge (CK) of neo-avant-garde poetry; deciding to make the response an object of comparison activated pedagogical knowledge (PK) and the "hybrid writing notebook" that organized reading, verification and revision. [[human-ai-collaboration|Human–AI co-agency]] is defined as asymmetrical: the system offers alternatives while the teacher assesses relevance and use. Three practices characterize the interaction: critically evaluating suggestions, adapting them to instructional objectives, and deciding whether to accept, modify or reject specific outputs.

## What the case can and cannot support

The authors integrate [[reflection|reflective practice]], conceptual change, TPACK and agency. They draw on Schön's reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action, Posner's conceptual change theory and Priestley's ecological account of agency, and they note that "reflection-with-action-partner" is an exploratory lens rather than a validated construct. They stress that a change in lesson design does not itself demonstrate lasting belief change, that alternative interpretations remain possible — the teacher may have used the response to confirm existing criteria rather than transform them — and that source triangulation supports a nuanced account rather than proof. The contribution is a perspective for further investigation across contexts and disciplines, not a causally validated model.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Ask the model for an accessible explanation, then ask what it missed: require students to compare the AI's reading with textual evidence and with other authors before accepting it, as the teacher's worksheet did.
- **Faculty developers.** Treat AI as a reflective partner rather than a content source in professional development. Evaluate disciplinary accuracy first, and use the mismatch to redesign the task.
- **Instructional designers.** Build the evaluative step into the artifact — a "what did the AI miss?" section, a verification column in a notebook — so the critical move is visible and repeatable rather than left to individual judgment.
- **Teacher educators.** Keep methodological decisions with the [[teacher-role|teacher]]. Define which parts of planning AI may inform (explanations, alternatives, language feedback) and which remain professional judgments about purpose and accuracy.
- **Researchers.** Test whether the dynamic recurs. Single-case, three-week evidence cannot show lasting change; longitudinal and comparative work across disciplines is needed.

## Limitations

- A single case in one disciplinary context over three weeks, so the themes are contextual analytical constructions and not generalizable stages or effects.
- Changes in lesson artifacts do not demonstrate stable changes in beliefs, and the design cannot distinguish conceptual change from a situational redesign.
- Student learning outcomes were not measured; the evidence concerns the teacher's design process.
- Reflexive thematic analysis is interpretive, and the authors acknowledge that the teacher may have used the AI response to confirm existing criteria rather than transform them.
- The framework integrates constructs (reflection, conceptual change, TPACK, agency) without validating measures, and reflection-with-action-partner is an exploratory lens rather than a tested instrument.
