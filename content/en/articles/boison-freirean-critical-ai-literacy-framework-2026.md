> **Synthesis:** Boison (2026) argues that [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] frameworks in [[higher-ed|higher education]] specify what learners should be able to do more sharply than they explain how critical consciousness develops. His Freirean Critical AI Literacy (FCAIL) framework answers with four dialectically related dimensions, each pairing a learner capacity with the educational conditions that enable or constrain it: AI World-Reading, Contradiction Naming, Critical Praxis, and Solidary Conscientization. A decolonial epistemic commitment runs through all four rather than sitting beside them as an [[ethics]] module, and the unit of analysis is the educational arrangement — learners, faculty, institutions, communities — not the isolated learner. The paper reports no sample, data, or empirical evaluation, offering four propositions and a construct-to-evidence matrix for later testing, and presents FCAIL as a complement to competency frameworks.

## Key Findings

1. **Each dimension pairs a learner capacity with enabling conditions.** The four are relational constructs naming what a learner develops and the arrangement that permits or forecloses it; the arrangement, not the isolated learner, is the unit of analysis.
2. **The central claim is architectural, not empirical.** Competency lists can specify ethical content without explaining how learners move from recognizing an AI problem to conscientization, a tendency Boison reads as banking education. The tendency belongs to the architecture, not to teachers' motives, since the same competencies can be taught dialogically.
3. **AI World-Reading reworks Freire's reading of the word and the world.** It treats an AI system as a designed, historically situated text and asks who built it, whose knowledge it carries, who is surveilled, and whose experience is legible — questions applied to [[learning-analytics|learning analytics]], [[ai-detection|AI detection]], advising, and [[ai-feedback-quality|automated feedback]].
4. **Contradiction Naming makes lived AI tensions the curricular entry point.** Generative themes must be locally situated — a falsely flagged essay, feedback that misses a student's purpose, analytics that assume the wrong study patterns — not drawn from a universal inventory of harms.
5. **Critical Praxis spans individual and institutional action and admits refusal.** Contesting a grade, redesigning an [[assessment|assessment]], rewriting policy, and holding a vendor accountable are praxis at different scales; for Indigenous communities, refusal and data authority under OCAP® or CARE are legitimate action, not opting out.
6. **Solidary Conscientization is collective, and governance access conditions its enactment.** A learner may hold the capacity for shared analysis while the institution offers nowhere to exercise it, and the incompleteness then attaches to the arrangement. The compound term is the author's.
7. **The decolonial commitment is constitutive, and the framework states what would disconfirm it.** Removing it changes each dimension's object of analysis rather than subtracting an ethical layer; the claim covers epistemic and governance questions, not decolonization as a whole. Four propositions are paired with disconfirming observations.

## Why competency architecture can domesticate

The critique targets a family of frameworks. Long and Magerko's (2020) synthesis identified 17 core competencies covering technical understanding and [[critical-thinking|critical evaluation]]. Ng et al. (2021) proposed four dimensions from knowing and applying AI to evaluating its ethical issues, and Laupichler et al.'s (2022) [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|scoping review]] covered 30 studies across ten research databases, finding a skills-centered field with inconsistent definitions and few validated assessments. UNESCO's (2024) framework specifies twelve competencies across four dimensions at three progression levels; Hackl et al.'s (2026) AI Literacy Heptagon organizes seven dimensions across four proficiency levels. Boison does not claim these frameworks lack critical content; his claim is that specifying a competency does not theorize how critical recognition becomes structural analysis and collective action.

The consequence is a distinction between two educational subjects. Competency frameworks tend to form the effective AI user, able to evaluate outputs, understand limits, reason ethically, and collaborate productively. Critical consciousness asks whether participation is justified, on what terms, and who decides. Freire's forms of consciousness supply the diagnostic: magical consciousness experiences AI as a natural feature of the landscape, naive consciousness reads bias as a technical deficiency better systems will fix, and critical consciousness reads AI as a sociotechnical artifact encoding values and open to contestation.

The stakes are uneven, and the clearest evidence comes from other studies. Liang et al. (2023) evaluated seven widely used GPT detectors and reported an average false-positive rate of 61.22% for TOEFL essays by non-native English speakers against 5.19% for essays by United States eighth-grade students; all seven detectors unanimously classified 19.78% of the TOEFL essays as AI-generated, a disparity those authors attribute to lower text perplexity. Boison uses it to show why skill in navigating such systems is not a basis for naming them as structural problems. A second, hypothetical illustration is an advising tool that reproduces historical enrollment patterns while presenting recommendations as neutral.

## Four dimensions of one arrangement

The dimensions form a dialectical, recursive structure with no required sequence. Any can serve as an entry point: a faculty member may enter through governance and reach world-reading later; a student through a contested grade. The dimensions are separable enough to study individually, but the propositions describe dependence, not a staged pathway. Table 1 maps each to a Freirean form of consciousness and the conditions it requires, as a heuristic rather than a diagnostic instrument.

The unit of analysis is the arrangement, so research can measure the learner while explaining through the conditions in which capacity was cultivated. Four conditions bound expected applicability: AI must be materially present in learners' experience, the setting must admit dialogue about institutional conditions, purpose must extend beyond operating within existing arrangements, and some pathway must exist for analysis to become action. Where these are absent, FCAIL identifies what is missing rather than a deficit in learners.

Boison distinguishes theoretical applicability from empirical transferability: the constructs are claimed to cohere wherever the four conditions hold, while whether findings travel is left open. That matters most in Indigenous-serving contexts, where themes, forms of praxis, and governance instruments must be settled through research conducted with those institutions under their own authority. The northern Canadian illustration is sector-level, not validation; it describes conditions that may obtain in small, isolated institutions, not documented practice anywhere.

## Decolonial ground and the research program

The decolonial thread conditions each dimension's analysis. Mohamed et al. (2020) apply postcolonial and decolonial theory to AI, proposing a critical technical practice, reverse tutelage, and renewed affective and political communities. Zembylas (2023) describes digital neocolonialism as Eurocentric knowledge systems imposed through apparently neutral infrastructure, and proposes problematizing algorithmic coloniality and centering non-Western epistemologies. The CARE Principles (Carroll et al., 2020) and the First Nations Principles of OCAP® give the governance dimension operational substance.

Boison accepts a limit many do not. Following Tuck and Yang (2012), he holds that decolonization in settler-colonial contexts concerns the repatriation of Indigenous land and life, and that metaphorical uses evacuate its force. FCAIL therefore claims only the epistemic and governance terrain relevant to AI literacy, and a theoretical framework cannot substitute for Indigenization, a place-based process conducted through accountable relationships with particular communities.

The empirical program is proposed, not reported. Four propositions hold that competency acquisition does not by itself specify the conditions for conscientization; that critical literacy develops as learners connect specific contradictions to the structures producing them; that understanding becomes praxis when means to contest conditions exist; and that collective analysis and claim-making are constitutive, with governance determining whether that capacity can be enacted. Table 3 pairs each proposition with manifestations, method families, and observations that would require revision. The proposed sequence runs from practitioner content validation through longitudinal comparison of problem-posing and competency-based courses to multi-site comparison of institutions differing in governance provision, including Indigenous-serving institutions from the start.

## What this means for practice

These are design proposals, not tested effects.

- **Instructors.** Begin with a system students already use and ask who built it, on which data, to optimize what, so technical description opens inquiry rather than closing it.
- **Curriculum designers.** Put a live contradiction inside disciplinary content: have students audit what an AI feedback rubric rewards and misses, whose English it treats as standard, and what that implies for professional judgment.
- **Assessment designers.** Ask what widespread AI substitutability reveals about what an assessment measured, then redesign toward process, [[metacognition]], contextual embedding, and [[oral-assessment|oral defense]] rather than detection alone.
- **Administrators.** Treat student participation in [[governance|AI governance]] as a literacy condition: seats with effect on vendor decisions, transparent procurement, and a real mechanism for contesting AI-mediated decisions about standing or support.
- **Faculty developers and researchers.** Prepare faculty as co-investigators who can facilitate critical dialogue, and measure conditions alongside capacity, preferring evidence about how dialogue changes students' reasoning rather than post-course satisfaction.

## Limitations

- The framework is conceptual and untested; the paper reports no sample, instruments, or data, and its dimensions are illustrated rather than validated.
- The four dimensions are reconstructed from one reading of Freire and offered as a principled reconstruction, not an exhaustive representation.
- Separability is asserted, not demonstrated: no evidence shows the four capacities are empirically distinguishable, and measuring learner behavior without institutional conditions captures only one term of each construct.
- The four boundary conditions are themselves an empirical question, and the framework claims theoretical applicability rather than empirical transferability; Freirean [[pedagogy]] emerged from acute oppression, so its adaptation to Global North settings remains unresolved.
- The decolonial claim is deliberately narrow and cannot substitute for Indigenization; the paper acknowledges the risk of metaphorical decolonization in Tuck and Yang's sense.
- No operational instruments are offered, and assessment risks reducing the constructs to scored behaviors; the northern illustration is sector-level rather than documented practice, and no cost, staffing, or scalability analysis accompanies the governance demands.
