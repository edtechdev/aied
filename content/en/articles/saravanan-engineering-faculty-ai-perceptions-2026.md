> **Synthesis:** Saravanan, Meissner and Glenn (2026) ran three focus groups with 16 [[engineering-education|engineering]] faculty at one research-intensive university in the United States. The dominant stance was reluctant engagement: faculty accommodated [[generative-ai|generative AI]] because they saw it as unavoidable, not because they endorsed it. They doubted the reliability of outputs, feared that students were offloading the very thinking that engineering coursework is meant to develop, and described homework losing its diagnostic value. They also reported conflict inside student teams and the emotional cost of managing unclear [[educational-policy-ai|institutional policy]]. Looking ahead, they expected curricular redesign and reasserted the value of in-person, experiential and mentorship-based learning. The paper calls for governance, [[educational-development|professional development]] and assessment redesign.

## Key Findings

1. **Faculty accommodated AI without conviction.** The authors name the prevailing stance reluctant engagement: acceptance driven by perceived inevitability rather than principled endorsement.
2. **Reliability doubts were widespread.** Participants described fabricated citations and plausible but wrong solutions, and worried that students with weak foundations could not recognize errors in authoritative-sounding output.
3. **[[cognitive-offloading|Cognitive offloading]] was the central teaching concern.** Faculty said students delegated problem setup, debugging and argument construction, bypassing the [[desirable-difficulties|productive struggle]] that engineering learning depends on.
4. **Homework lost its diagnostic value.** Some reported grades clustering near 100% while [[summative-assessment|closed-book exam]] performance varied widely, and shifted weight from assignments toward exams.
5. **[[group-work|Group work]] became a source of conflict.** Students accused teammates of inappropriate AI use, which faculty read as a threat to trust and cohesion when expectations were not shared.
6. **Policy gaps fell on individuals.** Instructors described redesigning assignments, monitoring use and communicating shifting rules largely without coordinated institutional support.
7. **Faculty expected redesign, not prohibition.** Participants anticipated inevitable integration and argued for in-person laboratories, mentorship and hands-on design as enduring sources of value.

## What the focus groups showed

Sixteen engineering professors at a large research-intensive university in the southern United States took part: 10 male and six female, with an average of 10 years of teaching experience (SD = 6.47 years). Three semi-structured focus groups were held in November 2025. The first two lasted approximately 70 minutes each and the third approximately 90 minutes; the first two included five participants each and the third six. Ethics approval was IRB-25-450. Two authors analyzed the transcripts with inductive thematic analysis, using open and then axial coding and maintaining reflective memos. Saturation was judged to have been reached after the second group, with the third mainly elaborating existing patterns. The authors repeatedly stress that statements about student behavior are faculty interpretations, not direct measures of student outcomes.

Three themes emerged, each with several subthemes. In the first, faculty combined skepticism about reliability with acknowledged benefits: several used AI for [[rubrics|rubric]] development and assignment design, and saw it as a scaffold for upper-level students synthesizing literature. Against that, one participant reported asking for a reading list and getting nine references, none of which could be found: "Those things are just fabricated by AI." Participants worried that polished work produced an illusion of competence, describing students who earned high homework scores but struggled on closed-book exams, and noting that troubleshooting conversations had become more surface-level.

The second theme covered student use and its effects. Common tasks included generating and debugging code, drafting lab reports, brainstorming designs and checking numerical solutions; one participant estimated that "half of the class is using AI to write their codes." Faculty described fewer office-hour visits, more superficial questions, and homework grades clustering near 100% alongside wide exam variability. One said simply, "I'm not going to grade Gemini and ChatGPT," and another reported decreasing assignment weight in favor of exams. They also described students turning on each other in teams when some members used ChatGPT and others did not, and spoke of the extra work and emotional strain of constant adaptation.

The paper situates these perceptions in wider figures it cites. The Digital Education Council Global AI Faculty Survey of 2025 reported that 80% of respondents perceived a lack of institutional clarity and that only 4% felt their institution's guidelines were both comprehensive and well-communicated. A global student survey found that 86% of students use AI in their studies, with over half doing so weekly. OECD 2026 findings indicated that students using [[conversational-ai|chatbots]] for mathematics practice did better during practice but scored 17% lower on subsequent closed-book examinations than peers who studied independently. These are cited context figures, not outcomes measured by the focus groups.

## What faculty expect next

The third theme looked forward. Faculty called for clear university and departmental policies that define acceptable use and protect instructors in disputes. They were uncertain about which knowledge remains fundamental and how employers will value engineering credentials, with some anticipating that [[higher-ed|higher education]] will need disruption before it can be rebuilt. At the same time, they treated AI integration as unavoidable and expected to redesign curricula explicitly: creating AI-specific assignments, rethinking exam formats, and asking students to critique or validate outputs. They repeatedly located engineering's distinctive value in mentorship, teamwork, laboratory work and physical design.

The implications are organized at three levels. Institutions are urged to provide [[discipline-specific-aied|discipline-specific]] governance with centrally stated rules and departmental flexibility. Programs are urged to redesign assessment around visible problem-solving, including in-class work, [[oral-assessment|oral examinations]], project logs and tasks that require students to challenge AI output. Instructors are urged to differentiate by level: limiting or structuring AI use in foundational courses where schema formation depends on productive struggle, and [[scaffolding]] [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] in advanced courses where students can evaluate outputs critically. The authors also argue that AI literacy should be taught as part of the [[curriculum-design|curriculum]] rather than left to policy statements.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Make student reasoning visible through in-class [[problem-solving|problem solving]], oral checks and tasks that require validating AI output rather than accepting polished answers.
- **Faculty developers.** Support staff with practical guidance on assessment redesign and on preserving productive struggle in foundational courses.
- **Institutions.** Provide clear acceptable-use policies, procedures for disputes and protection for instructors, so that adaptation is not left to individual effort.
- **Researchers.** Pair faculty accounts with student data; this study records interpretations of student behavior, not verified learning outcomes.

## Limitations

- **Single institution.** All participants came from one R1 university in the southern United States, limiting transferability to other institutional types and regions.
- **Small self-selected sample.** Sixteen faculty who responded to a recruitment email may overrepresent those already engaged with AI issues.
- **Shared context and positionality.** The researchers were colleagues in the same college of engineering, which may have shaped what participants disclosed in group settings.
- **Faculty perspectives only.** Claims about student use, motivation and learning are interpretations offered by instructors, not direct evidence about students.
- **Single moment.** Data were gathered in November 2025 in a rapidly changing landscape, and the authors call for [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal]] and multi-institution follow-up.
