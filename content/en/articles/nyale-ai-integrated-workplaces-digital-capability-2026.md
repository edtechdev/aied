> **Synthesis:** Nyale (2026) proposes a six-stage, program-level process for turning labor-market evidence into [[curriculum-design|curriculum change]] for AI-integrated workplaces. It is a conceptual framework, not an evaluation: the article carries Frontiers' Original Research label but reports no sample, no dataset, no empirical analysis and no results, and its own method section describes a purposive conceptual synthesis. The stages run from sensing through stakeholder validation, program diagnosis, curriculum redesign, enactment and outcome review, each with a human decision gate that AI may inform but cannot pass. Five cross-cutting conditions — data infrastructure, stakeholder participation, faculty capability, ethical [[governance|governance]] and academic authority — shape every stage, and four capability domains replace the idea of a single digital-skills specification. Kenya supplies the policy context and nothing more: no institution, consultation, readiness assessment or pilot is reported. The framework's value is a testable process logic and an explicit allocation of decision rights, not evidence that AI-assisted curriculum review improves [[career-development-and-readiness|graduate career readiness]].

## Key Findings

1. **Six stages, each with a non-delegable human decision.** Sensing, stakeholder validation, program diagnosis, curriculum redesign, enactment and capability, and outcome review are linked in a governed sequence; every transition carries a named decision right and an accountable output, so a labor-market signal cannot flow automatically into program change.
2. **Five conditions cut across the whole process.** Data infrastructure, stakeholder participation, faculty capability, ethical governance and academic authority are treated as interacting socio-technical conditions rather than as a single implementation step or a platform feature.
3. **Digital capability is split into four domains.** Universal digital foundations, [[discipline-specific-aied|discipline-specific]] digital applications, specialist technical capabilities and critical, ethical and adaptive capabilities replace a uniform skills list. The first three adapt the progression logic of Fraillon et al. (2020), ITU (2020) and OECD (2021); the fourth is the author's own curriculum addition, and specialist technical skill is explicitly not a universal endpoint.
4. **AI is confined to four bounded support functions:** pattern detection across heterogeneous labor-market information, competency mapping between validated priorities and program documents, generation of alternative curriculum options for human appraisal, and monitoring of implementation and outcome indicators. Academic prioritization, approval and student-level [[personalized-learning|personalization]] sit outside its remit.
5. **Four theories are integrated through mechanisms rather than mapped one-to-one onto stages.** Human capital theory (Becker, 1964) supplies the investment rationale and motivates sensing; stakeholder theory (Freeman and McVea, 2001) explains how signals are validated and competing interests balanced; constructive alignment (Biggs, 1996) explains translation into outcomes, activities and assessment; socio-technical systems theory (Trist and Bamforth, 1951) explains enactment.
6. **Four propositions and six outcome types define what an evaluation would test.** The propositions concern triangulated sensing, constructive-alignment routines plus authorisation, implementation conditions, and outcome differentiation; the outcomes are decision quality, curriculum alignment, implementation fidelity, competency attainment, career readiness and employment-related results, with employment treated as the most distal and context-sensitive.
7. **Three implementation configurations are offered for resource-constrained settings,** from a minimum viable human-led process through a digitally supported version to an advanced AI-enabled one, with scaling conditional on decision traceability, implementation fidelity, [[equity-in-ai-education|equitable]] participation and credible evidence of competency attainment rather than employment alone.

## How the six stages work

The process begins with sensing, which triangulates job advertisements, employer input, graduate tracer evidence, sector reports, professional standards and community signals. The gate is a data steward and stakeholder panel judging coverage, representativeness, provenance and uncertainty; the accountable output is a validated signal brief that records what the evidence cannot show. Stakeholder validation then clusters and compares competency signals, with employers, students, faculty, professional bodies and public actors confirming relevance and contestability. The output is a set of prioritized competency hypotheses, deliberately not curriculum requirements.

Program diagnosis maps those hypotheses onto existing outcomes, modules, assessments, resources and student opportunity structures, and an academic program committee determines gaps, redundancies, feasibility and trade-offs against disciplinary and public-purpose goals. Redesign generates alternative outcome, activity, assessment and sequencing options through constructive-alignment templates, but faculty and quality-assurance bodies select and authorize. Enactment covers [[educational-development|staff development]], authentic projects, work-integrated learning and monitoring of implementation fidelity, with faculty retaining pedagogic authority. Outcome review summarises student work, graduate feedback, internship and employer evidence, and equity indicators, and the committee decides whether to continue, revise, suspend or reverse the change. Iteration is therefore governed rather than automatic: a signal can be rejected or escalated, and a change can be withdrawn.

A useful discipline in the paper is terminological. It distinguishes digital capability (knowledge, skills, judgment and ethical dispositions for using digital systems) from career readiness (preparedness to enter and adapt within work), employability (capabilities and contextual resources for obtaining, retaining and progressing in work) and employment outcomes (observed labor-market results also shaped by economic conditions, geography, social capital, discrimination, institutional reputation and demand). The framework keeps the first three analytically separate and treats the fourth as distal.

## Capability domains and the specialist-skill question

The four domains are organized as a progression rather than a hierarchy of prestige. Universal foundations — information search, file and data management, digital communication, accessibility and security basics — are embedded across programs and assessed diagnostically. Discipline-specific applications connect general capability to sector practice through authentic projects. Specialist technical capabilities such as programming, data engineering, cybersecurity and model evaluation belong in electives, laboratories, capstones and work-integrated learning where the program or occupation warrants them. Critical, ethical and adaptive capabilities — evaluation, privacy, fairness, human–AI collaboration, communication, resilience and [[lifelong-learning|lifelong learning]] — are what allow graduates to keep working when tools, rules and processes change.

The paper's sector table, mapping finance, healthcare, education, logistics and public service roles to tiered requirements and indicative AI relevance, is explicitly a set of curriculum hypotheses. It is not a frequency estimate, a convergence analysis or a claim that all roles in a sector share one profile. That caveat matters, because the table is the part most likely to be lifted into a planning document as if it were labor-market data.

## Governance, evidence boundaries and the Kenyan illustration

Governance is treated as constitutive rather than as a compliance add-on. The paper pairs six risks with named decision rights: representativeness and bias, where online or employer-provided signals may underrepresent informal work, rural labor markets, self-employment, small enterprises and marginalized groups; privacy, security and data lifecycle; opacity and contestability, where a ranking must not be mistaken for an objective finding; academic authority, where AI-generated priorities may displace disciplinary judgment; vendor and model drift; and equity and access, where digital interventions may widen [[digital-divide|digital divide]] gaps in connectivity, language, disability or institutional capacity.

The evidence base is deliberately bounded. The paper classifies sources by role — foundational theory, capability frameworks, AI and [[learning-design]] studies, employability literature, and Kenya and LMIC policy material — and states what each can and cannot support. It concedes that task-level studies of student–[[student-ai-interaction|AI interaction]] do not demonstrate program-level labor-market sensing, and that AI has not been shown to be faster or more accurate than a curriculum committee. The defensible claim is narrower: AI might reduce search and comparison costs when data quality, provenance, representativeness and governance are adequate. The novelty claim is likewise integrative, not priority: the contribution is the explicit combination of program-level sensing, stakeholder validation, constructive alignment, implementation conditions and accountable review.

Kenya is used to illustrate why policy ambition outruns institutional capacity for continuous curriculum renewal, uneven faculty preparedness and weak mechanisms for translating labor-market intelligence into program design. The author states plainly that no Kenyan curriculum-process analysis, institutional-readiness assessment, stakeholder consultation or pilot data are presented, and that online job advertisements should not be treated as a representative labor market. The label on the article should not be read as a warrant: there is no empirical study here to replicate.

## What this means for practice

These are the author's design proposals, not tested effects.

- **Program leaders.** Adopt the six stages as a documented sequence with named gates, and keep a signal brief that records coverage, provenance and uncertainty rather than a dashboard score.
- **Curriculum designers.** Translate validated priorities through constructive-alignment templates, and preserve the record of alternatives considered and rejected so that a later review can contest the decision.
- **Faculty and academic governance.** Retain authority over outcomes, assessment and approval; treat AI recommendations as [[human-in-the-loop-ai|human-in-the-loop]] advisory inputs with a stated model, data source and confidence.
- **Administrators.** Assess readiness before scaling: data coverage, governance ownership, faculty capability, stakeholder participation, connectivity, and the practical ability to suspend or reverse a change.
- **Policymakers and quality bodies.** Require evidence of competency attainment and equitable participation, not employment counts alone, when approving AI-supported curriculum renewal.

## Limitations

- The article reports no primary empirical data, no sample, no instrument and no results; it is a conceptual synthesis despite its Original Research label.
- Source selection was purposive rather than systematic, with no preregistered search strategy, screening rules, complete corpus or inter-coder agreement, so coverage is a transparency boundary rather than a comprehensiveness claim.
- The framework has not been validated in a Kenyan university or any other institutional setting; no pilot, readiness assessment or stakeholder consultation is reported.
- Labor-market sensing may be biased toward formal, digitally visible work, and the sector and AI-relevance labels are illustrative author judgments rather than frequency estimates.
- The governance requirements — data stewardship, contestability routes, vendor audit rights, subgroup monitoring — may exceed the capacity of the institutions the framework is meant to help.
- The paper offers no cost, staffing or time estimate for the process, and its comparison with adjacent frameworks rests on a single author's reading rather than a [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|systematic review]].
