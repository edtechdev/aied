> **Synthesis:** Boyd-Swan and Reynolds (2026) run a survey experiment on ~1,750 U.S. hiring professionals to test how employers respond to college students' generative-AI use. Framing AI use as an *assistive* tool raised concern about credential evaluation only modestly and did not penalize applicants; framing it as a *substitute for student effort and ability* raised concern sharply and **decreased interview likelihood and, more starkly, salary-negotiation willingness — concentrated among high-GPA applicants**, suggesting employers discount grade signals they believe AI may have produced. Across both framings, the only remedy both groups selected as reassuring was **in-person testing** — an early signal that hiring markets may push assessment back toward presence-required formats, complementing the ghost-student literature's argument that institutions must "design for human presence."

## Summary

The first college cohort to have generative AI throughout their degrees entered the labor market in 2026, and how hiring professionals respond to their AI use is largely unknown. This experiment manipulates the one thing employers most plausibly vary on — their *belief* about what student AI use means — and traces it through to hiring outcomes.

Using Prolific, the authors surveyed ~1,750 U.S. professionals with hiring decision-making responsibility (full-time, for-profit sector, ≥1 year at current employer). After baseline measures of concern about identifying high-quality applicants, respondents were randomized into a control group or one of two framings before evaluating two otherwise similar resumes differing in GPA and applicant race/sex: a **mild framing** (AI as an assistive technology that can be misused) and a **strong framing** (AI as a technology capable of doing students' academic work for them). Respondents rated interview likelihood and willingness to negotiate starting salary for each applicant, then identified what information would ease concerns about applicants' academic ability.

## Key Findings

- **Baseline concern was moderate, and beliefs proved malleable.** As of mid-2026 most respondents were only moderately concerned that student AI use makes identifying high-quality applicants harder (a meaningful minority very concerned); both framings raised concern, but the strong framing raised it significantly more — evidence that complete information about student AI use is not yet widespread among employers and that concern is likely to grow as press coverage and direct experience accumulate.
- **The assistive framing did not penalize applicants.** Respondents shown the mild framing were no less willing to interview than controls, and in some cases *more* willing to negotiate higher starting salaries — though those salary differences were not statistically significant.
- **The substitution framing penalized applicants — especially high-GPA ones.** The strong framing was associated with decreased interview-likelihood scores and, more starkly, decreased willingness to negotiate salary; high-GPA applicants took significantly larger penalties on both margins regardless of race/sex — direct evidence that employers discount grade signals they believe AI may have produced, complementing prior findings that AI weakens cover-letter signal value.
- **Post-interview margins matter.** The stronger salary effect suggests some of AI's cost to job seekers is borne *after* the interview stage — a margin resume-audit studies cannot observe by design.
- **In-person testing was the only universally reassuring remedy.** While various factors allayed concerns for the mild-framing group, only in-person tests were selected by both treatment groups — respondents gravitated toward verifying the individual directly and relying less on the academic record.
- **Randomization balance held.** Chi-square tests across education, organization size, industry, hiring frequency, degree requirements, GPA importance, and AI-use measures showed no detectable imbalance (p = 0.50–0.96), supporting clean treatment assignment.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** The narrative around AI use in student work has market consequences: framing student AI use as legitimate assistance carries no hiring penalty in this experiment, while the substitution frame does — how programs talk about (and design) AI use may matter for graduates' outcomes as much as the use itself.
- **Administrators / program leaders.** If employers discount GPAs they suspect AI produced, credential inflation risks devaluing the degree signal for all students; assessment redesign that verifies capability directly (portfolios, live demonstration, oral defense) protects signal value rather than fighting technology.
- **Career services.** Advising graduates to demonstrate AI-assisted work *transparently* (showing process, judgment, and verification) aligns with the mild framing employers did not penalize; concealment invites the substitution assumption.
- **Researchers.** The study isolates belief manipulation from actual applicant quality — a design that separates "what AI use signals" from "what AI use does," showing the signaling channel alone moves hiring outcomes. The salary-margin finding argues for expanding audit-study designs beyond interview callbacks.
- **Policy / accreditation.** The finding that both framing groups converged on in-person testing as the credible verification method is early evidence that AI-era labor markets may reward — and eventually demand — presence-verifiable assessment, reinforcing the direction already urged in the AIEd assessment literature.

## Limitations

- Hypothetical vignette experiment with Prolific respondents, not real hiring decisions: respondents rated willingness to interview/negotiate on structured scales, which may diverge from behavior in actual hiring, especially for salary negotiation.
- Respondents' AI beliefs were experimentally induced rather than naturally formed; the manipulation shows how framings *can* move judgments, not how employer beliefs will actually evolve — though the authors argue the direction (growing concern) is plausible as experience accumulates.
- Resume design trade-offs: institution and major were redacted (sample-size constraints, unknown industry mix), so the experiment isolates GPA and race/sex signals while excluding other realistic resume variation.
- Subgroup analyses by applicant GPA/race/sex are within-experiment comparisons, and the paper is framed as *early* evidence — the labor-market equilibrium under widespread AI use remains unobserved.
- U.S.-only, for-profit-sector respondents; effects for public-sector, non-profit, or non-U.S. hiring are untested.
