> **Synthesis:** A [[mixed-methods-research|mixed methods]] study at one Swedish university asked what [[ethics|ethical]] concerns actually shaped how students and teachers used, or declined to use, [[generative-ai|generative AI]]. Surveys reached a census of roughly 10,000 students and 800 teachers in a social science faculty and returned 541 student and 128 teacher responses, followed by five focus groups with 13 students and two teachers. Ethics came up unprompted for 31% of participants (209 of 669), and those concerns ran well past [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]]: participants worried about [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]], [[creativity]] and [[agency|autonomy]], about moral character, and about a tendency to treat model output as objective. Students were more positive about the technology than teachers, though both groups agreed that submitting an entirely AI-generated text as one's own is wrong. The authors read these findings through environmental ethicist Carolyn Merchant's *partnership ethics*  (mutual interdependence, respect for diversity, inclusiveness, accountability and a shared commitment to long-term [[sustainability]]) and argue it offers a better frame for institutional decisions than a compliance-and-detection stance.

## Key Findings

- **Ethics surfaced without being asked.** 31% of participants (209 of 669) raised ethics in survey comments or interviews, even though no survey or interview question explicitly asked about it. The authors read that as evidence that ethical (non)use is a live concern rather than a topic imposed by the research design.
- **Concerns reached past cheating.** Most participants who raised ethics went beyond [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]], the topic that dominates the genAI-in-education literature. A dominant preoccupation was how use affected individual cognitive and personal development: loss of [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]] skills, [[creativity]] and [[agency|autonomy]]. Many also described a propensity to assume model responses were unbiased or objective.
- **Moral character entered the conversation.** Participants frequently spoke about genAI in relation to moral character, a result the authors connect to the "moral judgements" reported in an Australian focus group study. Many worried that using genAI made people "lazy"; a small number instead saw it as a way for the individual to be "effective".
- **Students were more positive than teachers, with one shared line.** Overall students took a more favorable view of genAI than teachers did, but the two groups agreed that submitting an entirely AI-generated text as one's own work is wrong. Among respondents who expressed a view of the technology, 94% (n = 196) were ambivalent or negative.
- **Developmental harm was the sharpest concern.** 51% (n = 106) expressed concern that genAI use could hamper individual development, making cognitive and personal growth rather than rule-breaking the most vivid risk in participants' own accounts.
- **Environmental impact was absent from policy.** GenAI's environmental consequences were not discussed in the university's policy documents, a gap the authors note is mirrored internationally. Their practitioner notes argue that any discussion of genAI ethics that omits environmental effects fails to address the full range of concerns teachers and students hold.
- **Participants wanted guidance.** Students and teachers alike wanted the university to offer more guidance on genAI use, a pattern the authors report as consistent with studies in other national settings.

## Study Design

The study ran during the 2024-2025 academic year at the University of Gothenburg and was supported by internal grant funding awarded after institutional review. It combined an exploratory online survey, administered in English, with focus group interviews conducted in Swedish and English. Work followed the Swedish Ethics Review Act (2003:460) and the Swedish Research Council's guidelines, and all participants were informed of the study's purposes and consented.

The survey was sent to the entire undergraduate and postgraduate student body and all teachers in the social science faculty through faculty and departmental email lists, making it a census rather than a sample: approximately 10,000 students and 800 teachers were invited, and 541 students and 128 teachers completed it. Respondents who were willing to take part in follow-up interviews provided names and contact information; 70 students and 13 teachers volunteered, and five focus groups were ultimately held with 13 students and two teachers. The authors are explicit that this self-selection is a limitation.

Respondents broadly reflected the faculty's composition. 69% of student and 55% of teacher respondents identified as female; 72% of students were at the undergraduate level, which normally takes three years in Sweden; and 80% of teacher respondents reported more than five years of teaching experience. Familiarity with genAI was high: 46% of students and 50% of teachers described themselves as moderately familiar and about 30% as very familiar, while students were more than twice as likely as teachers to call themselves extremely familiar (7.2% versus 3.1%).

## What this means for practice

- **Treat silence as the problem, not the absence of a problem.** The authors' third practitioner note is that silence around teachers' and students' genAI use creates uncertainty and suspicion, reducing opportunities for agency and collaboration. A policy that says nothing while use is widespread leaves both groups guessing.
- **Move beyond the cheating frame.** Their second practitioner note states that cheating and plagiarism is too narrow a frame to support ethical (non)use. The study's participants raised development, character and environmental questions that an integrity-only policy cannot address.
- **Build dialogue into the decision, not just the rule.** Partnership ethics asks all members of an academic community to navigate uncertainty together through ongoing dialogue. Concretely, the authors suggest open discussions among and between teachers and students about genAI's effects on individual development, society and the environment.
- **Put environmental consequences on the agenda.** The fourth practitioner note argues that discussions omitting the environmental costs of developing and deploying these technologies fail to address the concerns teachers and students actually hold.
- **Use partnership ethics as a lens on existing [[educational-policy-ai|policy]].** Merchant's five principles (mutual interdependence, respect for diversity, inclusiveness, accountability and shared commitment to long-term sustainability) give institutions a way to audit current guidance against the range of concerns the study documents.

## Limitations

- **A single case, at a single moment.** The study is a case study of one social science faculty at one Swedish public university during the 2024-2025 academic year, and the authors are careful that findings reflect that particular time and place.
- **One discipline only.** The research focused exclusively on a social science faculty, so the views recorded cannot be assumed to generalize across a university's other faculties.
- **Low and self-selected participation.** Survey response rates were comparatively low and [[teacher-role|teacher]] participation in the focus group interviews was low, with only two teachers across five groups. Participants self-selected, and the authors cannot know whether their views were held by non-participants.
- **A moving target.** The research concerned a rapidly evolving technology, so the specific uses and concerns recorded describe a technology that had already changed by publication.
- **Generalisation is unresolved.** The authors find significant resonance between their context and international scholarship on genAI, but state that more research is needed to determine how generalizable the findings may be.
