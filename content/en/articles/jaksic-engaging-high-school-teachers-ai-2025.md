> **Synthesis:** Jaksic, Ansaf and Massey (2025) describe a one-day, hands-on [[professional-training|professional development]] workshop for nine high school teachers from two local school districts, built to change what teachers believe about artificial intelligence by showing them what it already does in their daily lives. The workshop ran from 9:00 to 12:00 and covered four modules: intelligent behaviors and systems, [[ethics|ethics]], six families of AI application (classification, recognition, memory, decision-making, optimization, prediction), and AI tools that support teaching. A pre-workshop survey found the group split on whether AI is a threat — 40 percent thought so, 20 percent did not, 40 percent were undecided — and unsure about what their students knew. The exit survey, whose percentages imply eight respondents, was uniformly positive: every teacher reported learning something new, all rated the workshop excellent or very good, and 12.5 percent declined to join a follow-on project, which the authors traced to that teacher having just taken an administrative post. One participant integrated AI content into a coding course, and one author went on to build a three-credit graduate elective, ED 537 AI in K-12 Education. The authors present this as a proof of concept for university-school partnership, and it is best read as one: nine self-selected teachers, same-day self-report, no comparison group.

## Key Findings

- **Teachers arrived with mixed knowledge and real fear.** The initial survey found that prior knowledge "varied substantially," that 60 percent were aware of AI in everyday use and 40 percent were not, that 40 percent believed AI could be a threat while 20 percent did not and 40 percent were undecided, and that only 20 percent believed their students had some knowledge of AI, against 20 percent who believed they had none and 60 percent who were unsure.
- **Every participant reported learning something new.** On the exit survey all teachers agreed they had learned something new about AI; the authors note this implies no teacher already knew all the material, and treat it as evidence that the content was pitched above the group's entering level rather than at it.
- **The quantitative ratings clustered at the top of the scale.** Question 2 drew 62.5 percent strongly agree and 37.5 percent agree, Question 3 drew 75 percent strongly agree and 25 percent agree, Question 4 drew 62.5 percent strongly agree and 37.5 percent agree, and Question 6 rated the workshop 62.5 percent excellent and 37.5 percent very good.
- **One refusal is explained rather than averaged away.** Question 5 on willingness to join a new AI project drew 12.5 percent unwilling; on follow-up the authors found that teacher had just accepted an administrative position and would not have time. With eight respondents, that single teacher is the whole of the negative case.
- **The most valued outcome was knowing where to look and whom to ask.** Responses to the second open question named two benefits: knowing how to search for specific resources, and knowing whom to contact to collaborate. Teachers had assumed AI tools would be expensive and had not considered that free ones existed, and the workshop's websites, tutorials and applications were all free.
- **Awareness of AI's presence in daily work was the qualitative theme.** Teachers reported enjoying "seeing the AI aspects of daily life," and the authors describe a shift in realization: that many AI applications surround them, that ethical issues concern them, that some teaching and assessment tools they already use are AI applications, and that students can reach these tools outside class.
- **One teacher implemented, and one course was created.** A participating teacher integrated AI content into a coding course he was teaching. Separately, an author developed and implemented ED 537 AI in K-12 Education, a three-credit, one-semester graduate elective covering problem spaces and search, classification and clustering, image classification, natural language, robotics and fairness in AI.
- **The workshop content was organized around use, not around algorithms.** The six application families each carried everyday examples — spam filtering, face recognition, shopping personalization, flight autopilot, ridesharing optimization, credit scoring — and the teaching-tools module distinguished analytic AI (individualized plans for English language learners, IEP and 504 plans), interactive AI (Siri, Alexa), functional AI for students with special needs, and visual AI that narrates content for visually impaired students.
- **The format was short, face-to-face and deliberately broad.** The agenda paired two content parts with a hands-on object-recognition example, an AI4K12 presentation, two discussion blocks and free time, ending with the exit survey and a conversation about future collaboration. The authors chose breadth over depth because of the time limit.

## How the study was done

This is a descriptive case report of one workshop, not a controlled study. Nine high school teachers from two local districts attended a single morning session at the university, organized jointly by the School of Engineering and the School of Education. Both districts serve schools that average more than 44 percent Hispanic and marginalized students, which the authors connect to the university's mission and to the goal of reaching minoritized students.

Before the workshop, participants completed an initial survey on prior AI knowledge, current use, fears and perceptions of student knowledge; the authors say the results shaped the modules and the applications demonstrated. At the end they completed an eight-question survey: six quantitative items on five-point or three-point Likert scales and two open questions, one designed to prompt positive self-reflection and one to reinforce learning through summarization. The authors state that loss of fear was assessed through the initial survey and then through individual interviews after the workshop, though the interview procedure and analysis are not described. Results are reported as percentages, with no inferential tests and no pre-post matching of individual responses.

The paper also functions as a curriculum artifact: it lists the module topics, the agenda, and the teaching-tools taxonomy, so the workshop can be reused. It is an updated and improved version of an earlier ASEE conference paper titled "Engaging High School Teachers in Artificial Intelligence Concepts and Applications," submitted in November 2021 and published on 31 March 2025.

## What this means for practice

- **For faculty developers and outreach coordinators.** A three-hour session can move teachers from "AI is a threat" to "AI is a set of tools," and the cheapest lever is concrete everyday examples rather than technical instruction. The finding that teachers assumed AI tools were expensive is a recruitment argument: lead with free resources.
- **For workshop designers.** Pair every concept with an application the teacher already uses, and reserve time for the question "what can I do Monday." The two items teachers valued most were resource discovery and a named person to contact, which are logistics as much as content.
- **For K-12 curriculum leads.** The teaching-tools taxonomy is directly reusable: analytic AI for individualized plans, interactive assistants, functional support for students with special needs, and visual AI for accessibility. It gives teachers a way to see that some tools in their building already count as AI.
- **For anyone planning to evaluate this kind of workshop.** Follow the teachers into their classrooms. This study can show that participants liked the workshop and reported learning; it cannot show that the one reported implementation, or any other, changed student learning.

## Limitations

- Nine teachers attended and eight answered the exit survey, so each percentage moves by 12.5 points per respondent and the "all positive" result rests on a very small base.
- There is no comparison group, no delayed post-test and no classroom observation; the evidence is same-day self-report plus two open questions, and the interviews used to assess fear are not described or quoted.
- Participants were volunteers from two districts in one region who chose to attend an AI workshop, so the shift in attitude may reflect selection as much as the intervention.
- The workshop content dates from a 2021 submission, before the current generative AI period; the paper's applications are classical machine learning and commercial assistants rather than large language models, which limits how far the enthusiasm transfers to today's tools.
- The one documented classroom implementation and the graduate course are reported as consequences of the workshop, not as outcomes measured by the study; no student-level data are given.
- The paper is a practitioner report with light methods reporting: sampling, interview protocol, survey item wording and response rates beyond the eight exit surveys are not specified.
