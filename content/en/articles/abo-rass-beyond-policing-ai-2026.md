> **Synthesis:** Abo-Rass (2026) argues that the debate over AI in higher education has focused on policing submission authenticity while avoiding a prior question: what teaching is for. In social science education, the author contends, the aims are skills formed through dialogue, collaboration and reflective practice, and these cannot be replaced by a tool that produces text. The proposed response is a shift toward very small, often interdisciplinary learning groups in which students lead discussion, analyze cases, teach peers and revise their thinking, with assessment built around participation, scaffolded writing and long-term development rather than final products alone. AI-generated text can enter that process as material to critique and rebuild. The article reports no empirical data of its own: it is a reflexive argument from a faculty member's experience and cited literature, with no sample, instrument, measured outcome or evaluation of the proposal.

## Key Findings
1. **The central question is purpose, not detection.** The author argues that anxiety about authentic submissions obscures whether courses are meant to transmit content or develop competencies.
2. **AI cannot replace socially formed skills.** Critical thinking, reflexivity and leadership are described as capacities developed through human interaction and collective learning spaces.
3. **Scale undermines the stated aims.** Teaching large introductory and methods groups is said to make meaningful interaction, discussion and analytical practice difficult regardless of AI.
4. **Very small groups are the proposed response.** Small groups would allow dialogue, collaborative teamwork, case analysis, peer teaching and both individual and collective reflection.
5. **Interdisciplinary teaching should be the norm.** Groups drawn from different social science departments and co-taught by instructors from different disciplines model how fields overlap and disagree.
6. **Assessment should follow the learning process.** Longitudinal evaluation, participation, scaffolded writing, close mentoring and attention to effort are proposed in place of product-only judgment.
7. **AI can be used as a departure point, but feasibility depends on institutions.** Students can bring generated text into class to critique and rebuild it; large classes arose from massification and funding pressures, so pedagogical change alone cannot undo those conditions.

## The question behind the AI anxiety
The Opinion begins from the author's own position as a new faculty member receiving guidance on acceptable AI use, syllabus requirements and integrity best practice. Many documents are described as unclear, hard to implement and frequently revised, while colleagues report uncertainty about assessing student work. Some have moved from written assignments to closed-book exams to protect integrity; others fear that AI will erode analytical and writing skills.

The author's response is to reject the premise that AI is the primary threat. In the social sciences, the argument runs, AI cannot replace skills grounded in human interaction: critical thinking, reflexivity and leadership. What the debate exposes is a longer neglect of curricular purpose. If higher education aims to develop skills rather than transmit content, the important question is not how to police tool use but whether teaching creates conditions in which those skills can be practiced. Engagement tools such as Wooclap are described from the author's experience as a brief departure from an otherwise unidirectional lecture: they do not replace authentic interaction and can make the instructor's role look like entertainment rather than pedagogy.

## The proposal in concrete terms
The proposal is a systematic move to very small learning groups, including in introductory and research methods courses. In such groups, teaching can be organized around open dialogical space, collaborative teamwork, analysis of complex social situations, case-based learning, peer teaching, and individual and collective reflection. Learning becomes co-construction of meaning rather than passive consumption of information, and instructors can see students who would remain invisible in a mass lecture. The author draws on a cited meta-analysis reporting better outcomes for small-group learning in higher education, and on high-impact practice literature, without presenting new data.

Interdisciplinary structure is treated as part of the design rather than an enrichment. The example is a course such as Developmental Psychology for Social Work taught jointly to students of social work, education, psychology and sociology, with two instructors modeling disagreement and complementarity. The author notes that such models exist as exceptions requiring special approvals and resources, and argues for reversing that logic so interdisciplinary small-group teaching becomes part of core training.

Assessment changes with the group structure. The author proposes longitudinal evaluation, assessment of participation, scaffolded writing processes, close mentoring and attention to learning processes rather than final products. These formats also reduce the force of plagiarism and AI concerns, because students can be known through their contributions over time. AI is not banned: generated text can be treated as an object of study, rewritten and examined for how it handles evidence, power and knowledge production in social systems. The paper closes by naming the structural conditions — massification, financial and political pressures and higher student–staff ratios — and insisting that institutional investment and organizational culture change are required.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Ask what a course is meant to develop before choosing an AI rule, and design activities in which students must speak, question and revise in response to peers.
- **Instructors.** Bring AI-generated text into class as a text to critique and rebuild, rather than treating every instance of use as an integrity case.
- **Assessment designers.** Replace some final-product marks with participation, scaffolded drafts and longitudinal evidence of learning that a submitted essay cannot carry.
- **Curriculum designers.** Build interdisciplinary small-group courses into core training, with co-teaching that makes disciplinary disagreement visible to students.
- **Administrators.** Treat small-group teaching as a structural commitment requiring staffing and resources, not as an optional innovation left to individual instructors.

## Limitations
- The article is an Opinion and reports no empirical data; it offers no sample, instrument, outcome or analysis of its own.
- The argument rests substantially on the author's experience of emails, colleagues' concerns and teaching large groups, which are reported as reflections rather than systematically collected evidence.
- The cited small-group and high-impact-practice literature is used to support a claim about social science education without testing the proposal in that setting.
- No cost, staffing model or implementation evaluation is provided; the paper acknowledges that structural conditions make small-group teaching difficult but does not show how to overcome them.
- The claim that AI cannot replace skills formed through dialogue is argued from the author's perspective and is not compared against evidence of AI-supported collaborative learning.
