> **Synthesis:** A systematic review of 44 empirical studies from 2020 through 2025 finds that gamification can support motivation, self-regulated learning and achievement in digital educational environments, but only conditionally. The most frequently reported outcomes were motivation and engagement, self-regulated learning development, and knowledge retention and achievement. Results varied with implementation context, personalization, feedback timing and whether mechanics were aligned with learning goals. The review reports an enabling configuration of adaptive challenge, meaningful learner choice, immediate goal-referenced feedback and teacher facilitation, alongside an undermining configuration of sustained competition, reward salience and limited personalization. Because the evidence was heterogeneous, the authors synthesized it narratively and pooled no effect sizes. They offer a mid-range explanatory model of four supportive pathways and a dark-side pathway, two design configurations, six boundary conditions and eight propositions, while stressing that the field's strongest claims rest on its weakest research designs.

## Key Findings
1. **The corpus is small but appraised.** The review retained 44 peer-reviewed studies from five databases, all published between 2020 and 2025, and assessed every one with the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool.
2. **Motivation and engagement lead the outcome counts.** Motivation and engagement appeared in 11 studies, more than any other outcome category, followed by self-regulated learning in 10 and academic performance and knowledge retention in 9.
3. **The field's central claim is its least well-warranted.** Controlled designs underwrite most achievement claims but only a small share of self-regulated learning claims, which rely heavily on self-report.
4. **Configurations matter more than individual mechanics.** The studies with the best-supported outcomes combined adaptive challenge, learner choice, immediate feedback and teacher facilitation; documented harms clustered around persistent ranking and salient rewards.
5. **Benefits are contingent, not automatic.** Reported effects varied across designs, samples, instruments and contexts, and primary-study estimates were not comparable with one another.
6. **Equity is invoked but rarely measured.** Equity and readiness gaps motivated many adoptions, yet equity-relevant outcomes were hardly ever measured, so the corpus cannot show whether gamification narrows the gaps cited to justify it.
7. **The proposed model is a research agenda, not a result.** Eight propositions are offered as falsifiable claims to be tested in longitudinal, cross-cultural and multi-method studies.

## What the review measured and how

Searches were run in Scopus, Web of Science, ERIC, IEEE Xplore and the ACM Digital Library on 31 December 2025, covering records from 1 January 2020 to 31 December 2025. They returned 399 records; 52 duplicates were removed, leaving 347 for title and abstract screening. A total of 218 records were excluded at that stage, 129 reports were sought and retrieved in full, and 85 were excluded after full-text assessment, yielding the 44 included studies. Two reviewers screened independently; agreement at full text was Cohen's κ = 0.87, and a second researcher re-coded a 20% random subsample at the framework stage with 92% agreement.

The authors explicitly declined to pool results. Given heterogeneity in designs, interventions and measures, they followed Synthesis Without Meta-analysis guidance; all quantitative values are counts of studies or effect estimates as reported by primary authors. Counting used a denominator of 44 for implementation and outcome categories, and categories were not mutually exclusive, so percentages do not sum to 100.

Quality appraisal found 18 studies (40.9%) meeting all five Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool criteria, 16 (36.4%) meeting four, 8 (18.2%) meeting three, and 2 (4.5%) meeting two or fewer. Non-random sampling, uncontrolled confounding and incomplete outcome reporting were the commonest limitations. No study was rejected for quality; appraisal informed the weighting of findings.

## Where the evidence is strong and where it is thin

Outcome counts were led by motivation and engagement (n = 11; 25.0%), then [[self-regulated-learning]] development (n = 10; 22.7%), academic performance and knowledge retention (n = 9; 20.5%), personalized learning effectiveness (n = 7; 15.9%), critical thinking and problem solving (n = 5; 11.4%), social and emotional development (n = 3; 6.8%) and teacher development (n = 2; 4.5%). Primary-study estimates ranged from d = 0.42 to d = 0.78 for motivation and from d = 0.54 to d = 0.67 for achievement, and a cited meta-analysis put gamification's motivational effect at g = 0.36. The review warns that these figures come from different designs and instruments and must not be read comparatively.

The sharpest diagnostic is a cross-tabulation of outcome against design. Controlled designs underwrite 6 of the 9 studies claiming achievement gains (66.7%) but only 2 of the 10 claiming self-regulated learning gains (20.0%). The review turns this asymmetry into proposition P7: estimated effects on self-regulated learning vary systematically by measurement modality, with self-report instruments yielding larger estimates than behavioral-trace or performance-based measures. It calls for a multitrait–multimethod test or a meta-regression with measurement modality as a moderator.

The strongest positive pattern is configurational. Engagement-focused elements appeared in 15 studies (34.1%), personalized implementations in 12 (27.3%), virtual learning environment integration in 10 (22.7%) and problem-based or collaborative designs in 7 (15.9%). Higher education was the largest stratum (n = 17; 38.6%), followed by K-12 (n = 14; 31.8%), teacher preparation (n = 6; 13.6%) and adult education (n = 5; 11.4%). Mixed-methods designs predominated (n = 21; 47.7%); experimental or quasi-experimental designs appeared in 12 studies (27.3%).

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Begin with a needs assessment that identifies a specific engagement or self-regulation problem before choosing game mechanics.
- **Instructors.** Calibrate challenge to learner competence and offer meaningful choice; adaptivity without choice is reported as producing engagement without forethought-phase gains.
- **Instructors.** Make feedback immediate, actionable and tied to learning objectives; delayed or absent feedback undermines self-monitoring.
- **Instructors.** Use leaderboards sparingly and watch for demotivation and anxiety, especially where ranking is sustained and high-stakes.
- **Institutions.** Pilot, measure and iterate rather than adopting a template, and invest in teacher preparation before classroom deployment.

## Limitations
- Nine limitations are named, including a 2020–2025 window that excludes foundational earlier work, an English-language restriction, and coverage limited to peer-reviewed journal articles.
- Heterogeneity precluded meta-analysis; the synthesis is vote counting, and juxtaposed effect estimates are not comparable.
- The Engagement Dynamics–Systems Integration lens was developed and applied by the same team on the same corpus, so its appraisal is internal rather than independent.
- The protocol was specified in advance but not prospectively registered, so deviations cannot be independently verified.
- Geographic, level and discipline cell sizes are too small for formal moderator testing; the cross-cultural proposition P8 is a theoretical extrapolation.
- Coding was of published reports rather than of interventions as delivered, so implementation fidelity could not be appraised.
