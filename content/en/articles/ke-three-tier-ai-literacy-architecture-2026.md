> **Synthesis:** [[professional-training|Reskilling]] scholarship assumes that the technology delivering training is causally independent of the skill shift it answers. Ke argues that AI breaks the assumption, because the same absorption of cognitive work that makes a skill obsolete is what delivers its replacement. Five failure modes follow: velocity without capability, a deskilling loop in which the instrument reproduces the condition it was funded to remedy, personalization that fragments collective capability, stratified access, and the erosion of entry-level roles where capability used to form. The remedy is [[governance|governance]], organized through the author's HUMAN framework—[[human-in-the-loop-ai|human oversight]], upskilling for [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]], meaningful augmentation, accountability and audit, networked learning—and extended into a three-tier literacy architecture: operational fluency, evaluative literacy, and complementary capability. The tiers depreciate in inverse order to the investment they attract, so the cheapest and most measurable layer is the most perishable. Five propositions specify how each claim would be tested; none has been.

## Key Findings

- AI occupies both causal positions at once, generating the reskilling requirement and supplying the instrument that meets it, so the two roles form one governance problem rather than two literatures.
- Velocity without capability: courses delivered, completion, and cost per learner all improve while workforce capability holds still, because throughput is what learning functions were already measured on.
- The deskilling loop conceals itself, since a single system can design the pathway, deliver the content, and score the assessment, so a learner skilled at eliciting answers registers as competent.
- Personalization raises every individual measure while quietly dissolving the social conversion—cohorts, quality circles, shared problem solving—through which individual learning becomes knowledge the organization actually holds.
- Stratified access inverts the equity case for AI-enabled reskilling: workplace AI develops workers whose roles already carry discretion and merely accelerates those whose roles do not.
- The vanishing rung is structural, not pedagogical: automating entry-level tasks removes the experiential setting where early-career capability forms, and no volume of formal training restores it.

## The assumption that expired

Reskilling research has a quiet premise: the training technology is independent of the shift it responds to. The power loom did not teach loom operation. Ke distinguishes two ways AI could relate to worker skill. A valence problem asks whether a given use helps or harms capability, and better design or management can settle it. A position problem asks where AI sits in the causal chain, which design cannot settle, because the technology is what it is. Morandini and colleagues describe AI's dual nature in the first sense; this paper means the second. That distinction does the work: an organization can perfect its [[adaptive-learning|adaptive]] tutoring platform and still find the platform belongs to the family eliminating the roles the tutoring prepares people for.

## Three tiers, one dependency chain

The architecture classifies competence by its object. Tier 1, operational fluency, acts on the tool: prompt construction, tool selection, workflow integration. Tier 2, evaluative literacy, acts on the tool's output: hallucination, bias, drift, confidence calibration, provenance, knowing when to distrust. Tier 3, complementary capability, does not depend on the tool at all: critical and creative thinking, self-management, social and communicative competence, ethical judgment. The order is a dependency, not a list. Tier 1 is a precondition for Tier 2, since a learner who cannot operate the tool has nothing to interrogate; Tier 2 is a precondition for productive Tier 3 development, since judging which human contributions endure requires knowing what the system cannot be trusted to do. Tier 1 alone yields fluent, uncritical users, which is exactly the condition the deskilling loop needs.

## The investment-durability inversion

Tier 1 content is bound to interfaces and model behavior that change with every vendor release. Tier 2 principles persist while their failure signatures evolve. Tier 3 competencies survive technology generations. Investment runs the other way: Tier 1 is cheapest to procure, fastest to deliver, and easiest to measure, while Tier 2's benefit is avoided loss and Tier 3's is diffuse capability. No individual budget decision is irrational; the aggregate is a workforce repeatedly retrained in the most perishable layer. The market signal compounds it, since short-run demand for social skills in roles adopting [[generative-ai|generative AI]] has slipped while cognitive-skill demand held, so an organization reading [[career-development-and-readiness|demand signals]] faithfully under-invests in the tier with the longest half-life. That is where governance earns its place: the case for Tier 3 cannot be derived from current demand.

## Governance a program can be audited against

Each failure mode gets a mechanism rather than a principle. Human oversight becomes sign-off on whether a pathway builds capability, not whether it covers content. [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] becomes a required tier, with evaluative literacy mandatory instead of advanced, since an organization deploying AI without it has no functioning oversight, whatever its policy says. Meaningful augmentation becomes an explicit list of which difficulties are load-bearing and must survive the efficiency argument. Accountability becomes named ownership of reskilling equity plus audit of participation, completion, and advancement disaggregated by role level, function, age, and gender. Networked [[learning-design|learning design]] becomes cohort and community structures layered over individualized pathways. Where a platform designs, delivers, and assesses, at least one function should be separated, and [[assessment|assessment]] is the one whose separation tells you most.

## What this means for practice

- **Administrators.** Fund evaluative literacy as a required component of every AI training offering, and treat the training portfolio as a durability question rather than an annual spend.
- **Learning designers.** Build one assessment per program that is delayed, unaided, and scored independently of the AI system that delivered the training.
- **Program directors.** Give someone named ownership of who the reskilling system develops, and audit outcomes by role level, function, age, and gender rather than in aggregate.
- **Researchers.** Treat the five propositions as a test agenda: instruments for epistemic dependency and delivery-independent assessment do not yet exist.

## Limitations

- Conceptual throughout: the five failure modes are derived analytically from evidence produced for other purposes and were not observed in a reskilling program.
- The deskilling loop is inferred from skill-decay and [[cognitive-offloading|cognitive-offloading]] findings rather than demonstrated in a workforce training context.
- The half-life claim is argued from the nature of the content, and the tier boundaries are analytical rather than empirically derived.
- The HUMAN framework is the author's own manuscript under review and unpublished, so the crosswalk may need revision.
- The review is purposive, roughly forty sources weighted toward North American and European settings; the stratification argument leans on one Danish dataset.
