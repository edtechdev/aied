> **Synthesis:** Jiang (2026) tests whether institutional [[governance|AI governance]] reaches students as anything more than a policy on a website. Two-wave survey data from 644 undergraduate and [[graduate-education|postgraduate]] students across research-intensive, teaching-oriented, and vocational-applied universities in eastern, central, and western China were combined with objective coding of each institution's [[educational-policy-ai|AI policy]], and the model was estimated with institution-clustered standard errors. Perceived governance quality predicted [[authentic-assessment|authentic assessment]] — the [[assessment|assessment]] students actually experience — well beyond mere policy awareness (β = 0.36 against 0.12) and raised explained variance from 7.7% to 19.1%; authentic assessment then fully mediated the path to [[student-engagement|engagement]] (indirect 0.150) and partially mediated the path to perceived [[equity-in-ai-education|educational equity]] (0.076). [[digital-divide|Digital access]] remained an independent predictor of equity, which governance could not substitute for.

## Key Findings
1. **Quality of governance, not awareness of it, did the work.** Perceived governance quality predicted perceived authentic assessment well beyond policy awareness, and adding it more than doubled the explained variance.
2. **Authentic assessment fully mediated the engagement path.** Governance quality had no significant direct path to engagement once authentic assessment was in the model.
3. **Equity was only partly mediated.** Authentic assessment carried part of the governance–equity association, but a significant direct path remained, so governance shapes equity through more than assessment design.
4. **Digital access stayed an independent predictor of equity.** Its correlation with perceived equity matched authentic assessment's, and it was not significant in the assessment model.
5. **[[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] did not moderate the assessment–engagement link.** The interaction was non-significant, though conditional slopes rose from low to high AI literacy.

## Why policy awareness is the wrong outcome measure

Prior work found that simply knowing an AI policy exists predicts little about what students do, and this study shows why: awareness and governance quality behave as different constructs. Policy awareness correlated with governance quality at only *r* = 0.31, correlated weakly with engagement, and had a negligible correlation with equity (*r* = 0.09). Once governance quality entered the model, awareness's coefficient dropped to 0.12 while quality held at 0.36 (cluster *SE* = 0.048, *p* < 0.001), and explained variance rose from 7.7% to 19.1%. For an institution asking whether its [[educational-policy-ai|AI policy]] is working, the answer is that measuring publication and dissemination tells you almost nothing; what predicts student outcomes is whether students experience the policy as fair, consistent, and connected to how they are actually assessed.

## Assessment as the transmission belt

The mediation structure is the paper's substantive contribution. Governance quality had no significant direct path to engagement — its entire association ran through authentic assessment, which fits the [[assessment|assessment]] literature's claim that students orient their effort toward what counts. If AI governance changes task design, it changes engagement; if it only changes rules, it does not. The partial mediation for equity is equally informative: authentic assessment carried 0.076 of the governance–equity association, but a direct path remained, which means governance also operates through procedural fairness that students perceive independently of the tasks they are set. The author's framing draws on procedural justice and [[self-determination-theory|self-determination theory]] for exactly this reason: fair process is a need, not a courtesy.

## Equity has a material floor

The most policy-relevant finding is the negative one. [[digital-divide|Digital access]] predicted perceived equity as strongly as authentic assessment did (*r* = 0.31), and it was not significant in the assessment model, meaning it operates on a separate pathway. No amount of governance quality compensated for it. This separates two agendas that institutions often merge: redesigning assessment so AI use is defensible, and ensuring students have the connectivity and devices to participate at all. A university can score well on the first and still leave equity untouched, which is why the author argues that equity requires attention to procedural *and* resource conditions rather than to policy alone.

## What this means for practice
- **Administrators.** Judge an AI policy by what students experience in assessment, not by whether they have read it; the awareness coefficient collapsed once governance quality entered the model.
- **Assessment designers.** Redesign tasks so AI use is visible and defensible within them — authentic assessment carried the entire governance-to-engagement path.
- **Policymakers.** Pair governance reform with access provision; digital access predicted equity independently of anything the institution's policy did.

## Limitations
- Both governance quality and the mediators are student perceptions collected in the same two waves, so causal ordering rests on theory and temporal separation rather than on manipulation.
- Policy maturity was researcher-coded from institutional documents rather than taken from an external register, so it carries the coders' judgment.
- Chinese [[higher-ed|universities]] only, across three institution types; governance regimes elsewhere may transmit through different mechanisms.
- Engagement and equity are [[self-report-measures|self-reported]], with no behavioral or outcome data linked to the survey.
