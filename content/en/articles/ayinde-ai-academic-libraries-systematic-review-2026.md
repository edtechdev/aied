> **Synthesis:** Ayinde, Ebiefung and Oladokun review 29 studies published between 2020 and 2024 to ask what academic libraries actually do with [[generative-ai|AI]], what stops them, and what should be studied next. The practices sort into six dimensions — technical services, reference and information services, administrative and circulation services, collection development, [[information-and-media-literacy|information literacy and user education]], and professional development — and reference is the center of gravity: AI for directional and ready-reference questions appears in 34.48% of the reviewed studies. The barriers are organizational more than technical. Funding is named in 48.28% of studies and technical skill and data-quality gaps in 58.62%, while fear of job loss appears in only 13.79%. The authors read the pattern as a field automating transactional work first and leaving judgment work human, and they call for user-driven research rather than more pilot descriptions.

## Key Findings

1. The review follows PRISMA across Web of Science, Scopus and LISA, moving from 319 records to 221 screened, 80 full texts and 29 included studies; 28 are journal articles and one is a conference paper, and none used a mixed-method design.
2. Reference and information services are the most prominent application: AI for answering directional and ready-reference questions appears in 34.48% (9) of the 29 studies.
3. Cataloguing and chat-transcribed or expedited reference tie for second at 27.58% (8) each; classification appears in 24.13%, indexing in 10.34%, and intelligent content extraction, acquisition assistance, metadata generation, automation and rare-resource curation in a single study each (3.45%).
4. Funding is the most cited institutional barrier at 48.28% (14 studies).
5. Technical challenges — gaps in technological skill, hallucinated or confusing bot responses, insufficient datasets, deficient bibliographic data — appear in 17 of 29 studies (58.62%).
6. Infrastructure problems are cited in 12 studies (41.38%), including inadequate infrastructure (20.69%), limited power supply (10.34%), poor networking and outdated technology.
7. Ethical concerns — academic integrity, bias and false information, intellectual property, plagiarism, copyright, privacy, cybersecurity — appear in 31.03% of studies.
8. Fear of job loss is the most cited individual challenge, in 4 of 29 studies (13.79%), with resistance to change in 6.89%.
9. Environmental challenges appear in four articles (13.8%): public misunderstanding of AI capability, global governance disparities, and exposure to international standards.
10. Only one study used a quality checklist (Liu and Liu, 2023) with an inclusion threshold of 5 or higher; 15 of the 29 (51.72%) were published in 2024, and the *Journal of Academic Librarianship* and *Library Philosophy and Practice* each supplied three (10.3%).

## What the adoption pattern says

The distribution is the finding. The library functions that AI has entered most often are the ones with a bounded request and a checkable output: a directional question, a catalogue record, a transcribed chat. The functions that require deciding what a source means, or what a collection should be, remain human in this literature — and the reviews of instruction elsewhere in the knowledge base find the same boundary, with librarians consulted when a recommender is least confident.

That makes the funding result less decisive than it looks. If the binding constraint were only money, the applications would cluster where a purchase buys the most capacity. Instead the studies show a field buying reference automation while reporting skill gaps, data-quality problems and policy absence in the same breath. The authors' framing — six practice dimensions crossed with a technology-organization-environment reading of barriers — is a reasonable way to hold those together, but it also shows that the organizational variables are not a residual: they are where most of the reported difficulty sits.

## A literature that is mostly description

Twenty-nine studies is a thin base for a professional field, and the composition is thinner than the count. None used a mixed-method design, the quality appraisal is applied once, and the modal contribution is a survey of one institution's use or intentions. The authors say so directly, calling for more user-driven research on applications and for retraining that covers chatbot integration and [[prompt-engineering|prompt engineering]] rather than generic AI awareness.

For a reader trying to act, that changes the status of every percentage above. The figures describe what has been *published*, not what libraries do; a practice that no one has surveyed is invisible here. The review is best used as a map of where the literature has looked, and as an argument that the next study should measure a service rather than ask about attitudes.

## What this means for practice

- **Choose one service and instrument it.** The literature is full of adoption surveys and short of service evaluations; a library that measures a reference chatbot against the questions it was meant to answer contributes more than another attitude survey.
- **Budget for staff skill, not only licenses.** Funding is the headline barrier, but skill and data quality appear in more studies; a license without a training plan reproduces the gap the review documents.
- **Write the policy before the pilot.** Ethical concerns appear in nearly a third of studies, and policy absence is a recurring organizational barrier; the pilot's data-handling and integrity rules should exist before the first patron uses it.
- **Keep the human escalation path.** The applications that dominate are transactional, which is also where automation is safest; the judgment questions should keep a route to a librarian.

## Limitations

- The review covers 29 English-language studies from 2020–2024 across three databases; work in other languages, other databases and books or chapters is excluded, and the authors note that book chapters may hold foundational theory.
- No study in the sample used a mixed-method design, so the reviewed evidence is dominated by single-method surveys and descriptions rather than triangulated service evaluations.
- Quality appraisal against a five-area checklist with a threshold of 5/10 filters weak studies but cannot create outcome data the original studies did not collect.
- The authors describe their own method as conceptual and note that no primary data were collected; the six dimensions and the barrier categories are analytical groupings, not validated constructs.
- Rapid change in the technology means the 2020–2024 window under-represents current tools, particularly agentic and retrieval-augmented systems.
