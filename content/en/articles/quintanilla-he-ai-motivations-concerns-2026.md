> **Synthesis:** Quintanilla Villegas and Pineda Rivas (2026) surveyed 268 university students in El Salvador about motivations, consequences and concerns surrounding AI use, especially ChatGPT, and analyzed the responses with exploratory CHAID decision trees. The leading patterns begin with academic cycle and perceived workload: students who reported overload and difficulty retaining information also tended to report more frequent ChatGPT use. High-performing students often combined routine use with worry about memory loss; younger students facing overload more often used ChatGPT to learn concepts; students who rarely used it for exam preparation more often reported difficulty meeting deadlines. The authors treat these as associations within a convenience sample, not causal effects, and call for explicit institutional guidelines and pedagogical guidance.

## Key Findings
1. **Workload and progression lead the patterns.** The strongest tree begins with academic cycle and splits on perceived academic workload; students who reported memory difficulties alongside heavier demands also reported more frequent ChatGPT use.
2. **High achievement and cognitive worry coexist.** In the performance tree, most students had high grade point averages, and a substantial subgroup combining frequent use with self-reported dependence also expressed concern about losing memory.
3. **Age shapes use for conceptual learning.** Among students who did not report feeling overwhelmed by their studies, younger students were more likely to report using ChatGPT to learn course concepts.
4. **Deadline trouble was not simply about employment.** Difficulty submitting work on time appeared more often among students who reported little use of ChatGPT for exam preparation, whether or not they were employed.
5. **The trees describe associations, not causes.** The authors repeatedly caution that cross-sectional CHAID segmentation cannot establish causal relationships or produce generalizable predictive models.
6. **Regulation did not emerge as a strong predictor.** Ethical and regulatory concerns did not yield a robust model; the authors read this as weak institutional guidance rather than evidence that ethics is irrelevant to students.

## What was measured and how
The study used a quantitative, cross-sectional, exploratory design. Exhaustive CHAID (Chi-square Automatic Interaction Detection) split the sample by comparing categories with adjusted chi-square tests and selecting the predictor with the greatest discriminative power. Workload, time pressure, reward sensitivity and quality sensitivity were measured on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. ChatGPT use used a 6-point frequency scale from 1 = never to 6 = always; procrastination and perceived memory loss used 5-point Likert scales. Scales adapted from prior work were translated into Spanish and validated by expert review. Sociodemographic predictors included sex, age range, marital status, type of high school attended, academic cycle, grade point average expressed as the Coefficient of Units of Merit (CUM), employment status and having children.

A total of 268 university students from seven private institutions in El Salvador participated through convenience sampling. The survey was administered via QuestionPro and processed in SPSS v.23, with internal consistency reported as "Adjusted Cronbach's α = 0.824". The sample was 54.1% female, 84.3% single, 51.9% from public secondary schools and 47.8% from private ones, 42.9% employed and 88.1% without children. Bachelor's degree students made up 54.9% and engineering students 35.8%, with participation concentrated in the second (34.3%), fourth (17.2%), eighth (14.2%) and tenth (17.9%) academic cycles.

## What the models found
The first model is labeled 47.8% and reported chi-square statistics of 2683.119 (df = 9), 1058.311 (df = 9) and 340.385 (df = 8), all p < 0.001. Its first split used agreement with "My academic workload is too heavy": 73.1% of the sample (n = 196) disagreed or gave intermediate answers, while 26.9% (n = 72) reported being somewhat or strongly in agreement. Within the lower-workload branch, the next discriminator was "Lately, I cannot retain much in my mind", with students reporting memory difficulties making up 48.9% (n = 131) of that node. ChatGPT use then separated the groups, and the authors summarize the pattern as students reporting both workload and memory difficulties also reporting more frequent use.

The second model reported "χ 2 = 162.025; 83.474; 25.171; all p < 0.001". Its root separated students with a CUM below 7 (22.0%, n = 54) from those with a CUM above 8 (78.0%, n = 213). Use of ChatGPT for academic activities and self-reported dependence produced further splits. Students who reported never, almost never or almost always depending on the tool made up 59.9% of the sample; within that branch, those giving agreement, strong disagreement or intermediate answers to "I am losing my memory" showed high rates of elevated CUM, with 84.4% at CUM ≥ 8. The authors stress that performance and cognitive concern can coexist rather than treating this as a paradox to be resolved.

The third model split first by age: 70.1% (n = 188) were 17–25 years old and 29.9% (n = 80) were 25 years or older. Among students who disagreed that they felt overwhelmed by their studies, using ChatGPT "to learn concepts" (42.5% of the total sample) separated the groups: the frequent-use group was 66.7% aged 17–25, while the low-use group included 40.4% of students 25 or older. The fourth model began with employment: 42.9% were employed and 57.1% were not. Use of ChatGPT to prepare for exams and agreement with "I find it difficult to submit my assignments and projects within the deadlines" produced the later splits. Overall, the authors conclude that use is driven more by situational pressure and curiosity than by planned institutional training, and that ethical and regulatory variables did not form a robust predictive model.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Treat student AI use as a response to workload and unclear concepts, not simply as misconduct; ask which tasks triggered use and what the tool was used for.
- **Instructors.** Offer non-AI routes for clarifying difficult concepts and organizing study, since concern about memory and dependence appeared alongside high performance.
- **Assessment designers.** Make deadline pressure visible in task design with staged checkpoints, because difficulty submitting work clustered among students who rarely used ChatGPT for exam preparation.
- **Institutions.** Adopt and communicate explicit AI guidelines and build training around them; ethical and regulatory variables did not discriminate student behavior in these models.
- **Researchers.** Use longitudinal, multi-institution designs to test whether these associations hold and whether clear guidelines change patterns of use.

## Limitations
- The design is cross-sectional and exploratory, and the convenience sample came from seven private institutions in El Salvador; the trees describe sample-specific associations, not causal or generalizable predictions.
- Measures were self-reported, including perceived memory loss; no objective memory measure was collected, and academic performance relied on students' reported CUM.
- The abstract refers to nine decision trees, while the methods and results present four models; the discrepancy is not reconciled in the article.
- Repeated splits can leave small terminal nodes, and the authors note that the algorithm was configured to preserve sufficiently large nodes for interpretation.
- Ethical and regulatory variables did not yield a robust model, but the study did not test an intervention or measure awareness of institutional policy directly.
