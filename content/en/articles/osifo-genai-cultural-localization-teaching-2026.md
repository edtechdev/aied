> **Synthesis:** Osifo, Aydogan and Izmir (2026) interviewed 20 faculty at five universities in the United Arab Emirates about how they use generative AI for culturally responsive teaching. Faculty described real gains in localizing examples, language and materials, but also said that faster localization does not by itself produce deeper responsiveness. Whether it does depends on examining bias, representation and whose knowledge is treated as authoritative. Four themes emerged: pedagogical strategies, faculty sensemaking, cultural and relational benefits, and institutional, ethical and cultural risks. The authors extend Gay's framework with cultural-technological judgment and call for locally grounded guidance, faculty development and vetted case resources.

## Key Findings

1. **Localization is a common use.** Faculty used generative AI to generate UAE-based cases, adapt names and settings, translate materials and polish tone for local students.
2. **Surface change is not the same as responsiveness.** Participants themselves distinguished swapping a company name from substantive integration of culturally relevant content.
3. **Judgment decides cultural fit.** Faculty described filtering, editing and verifying outputs, and said responsiveness depended on prompts and human review rather than on the tool alone.
4. **Relational teaching remained human.** Mentoring, facilitation, empathy and emotional attunement were treated as work the technology could extend but not replace.
5. **Benefits went beyond efficiency.** Subtitles, translation, interactive activities and rapid contextualization were linked to access, engagement and belonging when paired with deliberate design.
6. **Risks were cultural as well as technical.** Participants raised stereotyped representations, sensitive topics, privacy, authorship, trust and uneven access to institutional guidance and tools.
7. **The framework is being extended.** Cultural competence and academic success were more visible in the accounts than critical consciousness, prompting a proposed dimension of cultural-technological judgment.

## What the interviews showed

The study used a qualitative interpretivist design and a semi-structured interview protocol grounded in Gay's culturally responsive teaching framework. Participants were recruited through purposive and snowballing sampling from five universities: UAE University, Zayed University, Khalifa University, Abu Dhabi University and the University of Sharjah. To be eligible, faculty needed a higher education degree, active use of generative AI in teaching and at least one year of teaching in the UAE. Interviews were conducted online via Zoom during the Fall 2025 semester with 20 volunteer academics representing 10 nationalities. The protocol was reviewed by two experts and refined after two pilot interviews. Ethical approval was ZU25_036_F. Thematic analysis followed an inductive, collaborative coding process with memos and team discussion; member checking was not conducted because participants requested only one point of contact.

The first theme covered culturally responsive strategies. A participant described asking for an Emirati company example so students could connect global and local perspectives. Another called changing a company name from Amazon to Noon or Kareem "very superficial." Language work included "English-to-English translation" to remove idioms, and tone-polishing of emails and announcements to meet local expectations of politeness. Faculty also generated multimedia and gamified activities, while cautioning that visuals still needed checking for audience fit.

The second theme captured sensemaking. Faculty repeatedly framed the tool as a capacity-extender, not a replacement for the educator's relational work. Fit was described as something produced through inputs and human-in-the-loop revision: one participant summarized the rule as "garbage in, garbage out," and another warned that even a navigation device could direct a driver into a river if not questioned. Some faculty noticed stereotyped patterns in outputs, including associations of poverty with Arab characters and defaults that one participant described as "the camel, the desert and Ramadan." Adoption stances ranged from professional growth to resistance.

The third theme concerned benefits beyond speed: subtitles and hearing support, translation into several languages, interactive activities and faster production of locally recognizable material. The fourth concerned constraints. Institutional policies ranged from permissive to discouraging; professional development often omitted cultural dimensions; subscriptions were not always funded. Faculty described "too perfect" submissions, limits of detection tools, privacy boundaries on real data, and avoidance of politics and religion as sensitive topics. The paper notes that the country's population is predominantly expatriate, at 88%, which frames the internationalized setting.

## What the framework adds

The discussion reads the findings through Gay's three dimensions: cultural competence, critical consciousness and academic success. Participants showed clear efforts toward cultural competence and support for academic success, while evidence of critical consciousness was more uneven. Localizing names and examples can create surface relevance without changing assumptions, a pattern the authors describe, citing earlier work, as cultural substitution without epistemic transformation.

The proposed adaptation adds cultural-technological judgment to cultural competence: the capacity to evaluate how the system selects, prioritizes and represents cultural knowledge, not merely whether its output looks locally familiar. In practice this means checking accuracy alongside representation, bias, sensitive phrasing and whose knowledge is included or excluded. Critical consciousness is correspondingly widened to cover the training data and cultural defaults through which material is generated. The authors frame culturally responsive use as sociotechnical and institutionally mediated, requiring collaborative teaching cultures, locally grounded Arabic-English cases, bias-audited systems and recognition of the work involved in building them.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Treat a locally named example as a starting point; check whose perspective is centered and which assumptions survive translation before using generated material.
- **Faculty developers.** Combine AI competence with local cultural knowledge, critical reflection and guided practice in adapting, filtering or rejecting outputs.
- **Institutions.** Develop context-specific guidance on verification, data protection and sensitive topics such as religion, gender, family, nationality and politics.
- **Researchers.** Include student perspectives and learning evidence; this study records faculty accounts and offers no measured outcomes for learners.

## Limitations

- **Selection bias.** Eligibility required active generative AI use, so the sample is skewed toward faculty already invested in both the technology and culturally responsive teaching.
- **Disciplinary skew.** Education, humanities and social sciences were better represented than STEM, health sciences or technical fields.
- **Self-report and context.** Accounts are vulnerable to social desirability effects, and the first two authors' positions at a public university in the UAE may have shaped responses.
- **Language and checking.** Interviews were conducted in English, and participant requests ruled out member checking; trustworthiness relied on memoing, peer debriefing and repeated returns to transcripts.
- **No learner evidence.** The paper reports limited evidence of measurable learning outcomes, changes in belonging or student voice, so cultural responsiveness is assessed from the instructor side.
