> **Synthesis:** Javadinejad and Davari synthesized 221 unique peer-reviewed publications on AI in English as a foreign or second [[language-learning|language education]], using a PRISMA-guided search of four databases. They coded each study for [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] role, mediating process, reported outcome and contextual constraint, then read the patterns through a three-layer framework of efficiency, pedagogy and ideology. AI systems appeared less as isolated tools than as multifunctional actors—tutors, assessors, conversational partners and content generators—that compress the interval between production, feedback and revision. Benefits such as expanded practice and faster feedback were widely reported, but the same body of studies recorded reliability, privacy, over-reliance and integrity concerns. Because the studies were heterogeneous, the review did not pool effects.

## Key Findings
1. **AI systems perform several roles at once.** The most frequent roles were [[teacher-role|teacher]] orchestration or support, content generation, assessment or diagnosis, coaching, tutoring and conversational partnership, and single systems often combined several.
2. **Writing and speaking dominate.** AI-mediated learning concentrated in writing and speaking or communication, using text, voice, [[multimodal]], immersive and [[adaptive-learning|adaptive systems]] for feedback, revision, rehearsal and learner–system interaction.
3. **Reported benefits cluster around three functions.** Studies described expanded practice opportunities, accelerated feedback cycles and a redistribution of instructional labor, alongside skill gains, autonomy and affective support.
4. **Constraints recur as often as benefits.** Technical reliability and [[ai-feedback-quality|feedback quality]], teacher readiness, privacy, over-reliance, infrastructure, bias, authorship and weak study designs appeared across the studies.
5. **The ecology is layered.** The authors read the results through efficiency, pedagogy and ideology: one system can speed feedback, reshape interaction and embed assumptions about correctness and authorship at the same time.
6. **No pooled effect was calculated.** Heterogeneity in designs, contexts, tools, language domains and measures led the authors to treat [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|meta-analysis]] as inappropriate.

## How the studies were selected and coded

The review followed PRISMA 2020. ScienceDirect, Scopus, ProQuest and JSTOR were searched from January to March 2025 with the string combining ESL, EFL or second language learning with AI or artificial intelligence; no publication date restriction was applied. Searches identified 1,302 records. After 116 duplicates were removed, 1,186 were screened by title and abstract; 357 were excluded, leaving 829 reports sought for retrieval. Of those, 293 could not be retrieved and 536 were assessed for eligibility. Full-text screening and bibliographic verification excluded 315, leaving 221 studies in the final set. Searches and screening were conducted by two trained research assistants under the lead author, with disagreements resolved by discussion.

Each publication was summarized in a structured record covering methods, sample, AI tools, instructional uses, feedback, outcomes, benefits, challenges and author limitations, then coded into a matrix. Coding was hybrid deductive–inductive, and two reviewers coded every record independently; initial agreement exceeded 80% across the main categorical fields, with differences resolved to consensus. Coding was multi-label, so a study could be counted under several tools, roles, skills, outcomes and challenges. Percentages use 221 as the denominator, and totals within a dimension can exceed 100%.

By design, 104 studies (47.1%) were survey or correlational, 85 (38.5%) [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] or case-study, 65 (29.4%) empirical interventions and 51 (23.1%) [[mixed-methods-research|mixed-methods]]. [[higher-ed|Higher education]] (99; 44.8%) and [[teacher-education|teacher education]] (98; 44.3%) were the most common settings, followed by mixed or unspecified contexts (48; 21.7%), [[secondary-education|secondary education]] (16; 7.2%) and [[primary-education|primary education]] (13; 5.9%). The review treats these as configurations of practice rather than as isolated tool effects.

## Roles, outcomes and constraints in the coded studies

Teacher orchestration or support was the most frequently coded role (117; 52.9%), followed by content or materials generation (111; 50.2%), assessment or diagnosis (97; 43.9%), coaching or practice companionship (88; 39.8%), tutoring or scaffolding (65; 29.4%) and conversational partnership (48; 21.7%). [[generative-ai|Generative AI]] and [[llm|large language models]] were the most frequent tool category (115; 52.0%), close to adaptive, [[intelligent-tutoring|intelligent tutoring]] and ICALL systems (111; 50.2%). [[automated-essay-scoring|Automated writing evaluation]] and writing assistants appeared in 43 studies (19.5%), [[conversational-ai|chatbots]] in 38 (17.2%), speech and voice tools in 28 (12.7%), translation tools in 28 (12.7%) and immersive or simulation environments in 21 (9.5%).

The most coded language domains were writing (101; 45.7%) and speaking or communication (76; 34.4%), then vocabulary and grammar (56; 25.3%), integrated or general learning (47; 21.3%), reading and listening (29; 13.1%) and pronunciation (24; 10.9%). Affective or [[motivation|motivational]] feedback appeared in 139 publications (62.9%), [[automated-assessment|automated scoring]] or diagnostic feedback in 85 (38.5%), corrective feedback in 67 (30.3%), [[metacognition|metacognitive]] or strategic feedback in 36 (16.3%) and elaborative feedback in 21 (9.5%). The authors describe a compression of the instructional cycle: production, evaluation, remediation and revision increasingly occur in one interface and often in one learning episode.

On outcomes, language performance gain was the most frequent category (177; 80.1%), followed by teacher support or efficiency (140; 63.3%), motivation and engagement (121; 54.8%), affective support or [[anxiety-and-stress|anxiety]] reduction (108; 48.9%) and autonomy or [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulated learning]] (49; 22.2%). These are coding frequencies, not pooled effects. The review illustrates them with primary-study claims: Duolingo was associated with greater willingness to communicate and engagement; AI-assisted teacher instruction supported online IELTS writing; metacognitive strategies and AI-based writing [[self-efficacy]] were associated with lower writing anxiety; and speech tools such as EAP Talk gave feedback on pronunciation, accuracy, organization and presentation. Avatar-based chatbots improved willingness to communicate and self-perceived competence while reducing speaking anxiety, although measured speaking performance improved less. Conversational systems created low-stakes rehearsal, and writing tools supported iterative revision. The authors repeatedly qualify these as conditional on task design, teacher mediation and institutional support.

Challenges were coded just as frequently. Technical reliability and feedback quality appeared in 177 studies (80.1%), teacher readiness and pedagogical integration in 150 (67.9%), privacy and data security in 137 (62.0%), over-reliance or agency loss in 104 (47.1%), infrastructure and access in 95 (43.0%), bias, fairness or cultural mismatch in 78 (35.3%), and authorship or [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]] in 61 (27.6%). Methodological warnings included [[self-report-measures|self-report]] or perception reliance in 131 publications (59.3%), small or context-specific samples in 65 (29.4%), limited generalizability in 59 (26.7%), short duration or no [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal]] follow-up in 41 (18.6%) and no control or comparison group in 10 (4.5%).

The synthesis interprets these patterns as a layered ecology. Efficiency includes automation of feedback, assessment, content generation and monitoring; pedagogy includes restructuring of interaction, practice, [[feedback]] cycles and [[scaffolding]]; ideology includes authorship, linguistic norms, bias and algorithmic authority. The authors conclude that AI is neither inherently effective nor ineffective: it is most useful when embedded in sequenced designs with goal-setting, guided practice, feedback, revision, reflection and teacher mediation, and least useful as an answer generator. Governance, teacher preparation and infrastructure are conditions of effectiveness, not background variables.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Design AI-supported tasks around goal-setting, guided practice, feedback, revision and reflection rather than using the system as an answer generator or shortcut.
- **Instructors.** Treat AI feedback as one source among several: ask learners to compare it with teacher feedback and other evidence, then decide how to act on it.
- **Institutions.** Put policies on privacy, authorship, acceptable use and [[human-in-the-loop-ai|human oversight]] in place alongside infrastructure and teacher preparation, since the benefits reported in the studies depend on those conditions.
- **Teacher educators.** Train teachers to evaluate AI outputs, design bounded tasks, manage feedback and discuss ethical issues, not simply to operate the tools.
- **Researchers.** Favor longitudinal and classroom-based designs that record interaction data, revisions and feedback uptake instead of relying mainly on perceptions.

## Limitations

- The studies were heterogeneous in design, context, AI system, language domain, outcome measure and reporting quality, which the authors took to make meta-analysis inappropriate.
- Many studies were short, small, context-specific or dependent on perception data with limited follow-up, so claims about long-term learning, transfer and [[equity-in-ai-education|equity]] remain weak.
- Coding was multi-label and percentages use the full set of studies as the denominator with overlapping categories, so totals exceed 100% and categories are not mutually exclusive.
- Searches were confined to four databases and conducted early in the review year, so a rapidly developing field may have moved past the systems and risks represented here.
- The benefits described are claims made by the individual studies, not effects estimated by this review; the synthesis maps patterns rather than testing them.
