> **Synthesis:** Crisol Moya, Romero López and Gámiz Sánchez (2026) surveyed 546 undergraduates at the University of Granada on five dimensions of AI in their studies: [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]], the skills students believe they need, what they use AI for, its benefits and its problems. Integrity ranked first and perceived problems last, with every dimension packed into a narrow band. Only reported use and perceived skill needs varied by degree program, and only slightly. The distinction worth keeping is between what students do with [[generative-ai|generative AI]] and how much they say integrity matters: the second is uniformly high across programs, so it cannot justify discipline-specific ethics training. The digital-citizenship framework the paper proposes is mapped onto the survey after the fact rather than tested by it.

## Key Findings

1. **Students put integrity first and problems last.** The order runs integrity, required skills, actual use, perceived benefits, perceived problems. That ordering is the paper's main result, and it says more about relative salience than about how strongly students hold any one view.
2. **AI is treated as an ordinary academic resource.** The authors read the use rating as evidence that students no longer see AI as an emerging technology but as a settled part of how they study.
3. **Degree program barely matters.** Only reported use and required skills differed across programs at all; integrity, benefits and problems did not, and the differences that did appear were small in magnitude.
4. **A high integrity score is climate, not conduct.** Students say integrity matters. The survey cannot show that they act on it, and the authors state twice that this is reported attitude rather than observed behavior.
5. **The instrument is sound in summary and thin in detail.** The five-factor structure fitted well and overall reliability was high, but no per-dimension reliability, item count or scale anchors are printed, so a reader cannot judge how precise any single dimension score is.
6. **The framework is a proposal, not a finding.** The paper's digital-citizenship model maps the five measured dimensions onto four conceptual ones, and the authors describe that correspondence as interpretive rather than psychometric.

## What a five-dimension ranking can and cannot show

The headline is an ordering, and the order is compressed. Integrity sits at 3.79, required skills at 3.64, AI uses at 3.61, benefits at 3.49 and problems at 3.05 — less than a point of separation across the whole set, on a scale whose anchors, item count and per-dimension reliability the article never prints. The table still assigns "Very High" to the top score and "Moderate" to the bottom one without stating the rule behind the labels, so a reader cannot tell whether the gap between integrity and perceived problems is substantively large or an artifact of item wording.

What the ranking does license is narrower and more useful. Students place the competence demand of AI above its perceived usefulness: required skills at 3.64 beats benefits at 3.49, and both beat problems at 3.05. That is a claim about relative salience inside one instrument, consistent with the authors' reading of AI as a resource requiring judgment rather than a shortcut. It is not evidence that students behave ethically, and the authors say so twice — the high integrity mean indicates reported ethical attitudes, not direct evidence of actual academic behavior. Read against the [[self-report-measures|self-report]] literature, this is a clean case of an instrument measuring the construct it names while leaving the behavior it implies unobserved.

## Why the discipline difference is smaller than the p-values suggest

Two of five dimensions differ significantly by degree program, the kind of result usually summarized as "AI use varies by discipline." The effect sizes undercut that summary: 0.030 for AI uses and 0.014 for required skills, small enough that degree program explains little of the variation in either. The authors themselves warn that the significant differences should not be read as evidence of substantial differences in students' overall perceptions. With several hundred participants a rank-based test will detect small, orderly differences, so significance here is about detectability, not the size of a disciplinary gap.

The null result on integrity is the more interesting one. Integrity did not differ by program while reported use did, which means the dimension students rate highest is also the one that is most uniform across degree programs. That sits beside, rather than against, the wiki's existing evidence that what counts as cheating varies by discipline: [[student-experience|student experience]] research comparing STEM and humanities students finds markedly different judgments about whether AI use is cheating at all. The two findings are compatible because they measure different things — the importance students attach to integrity versus the boundary they draw around it — and keeping them apart matters for practice. A program with higher reported AI use is not a program with weaker ethical commitment, and an integrity intervention aimed at the heaviest users would be aimed at the wrong variable.

What the design cannot supply is the identity of the groups. The paper reports no post-hoc comparisons, no program-level means and no group sizes, so the significant differences establish that some programs differ without saying which. The comparison is a screening result, not a map.

## A framework mapped onto the data, not tested against it

The paper's conceptual contribution is an AI-mediated university digital citizenship framework, which aligns AI uses with responsible participation, required skills with [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]], benefits and problems with critical awareness, and integrity with ethical responsibility. The mapping is tidy, and the authors are unusually direct that it is a reinterpretation: the correspondence is interpretive rather than psychometric, the study did not directly measure the four conceptual dimensions, and validation of the framework is left to future research. Nothing in the survey tests whether the four dimensions relate to one another as the framework claims, so the figure is a lens for reading the descriptive results, not a model of them.

The assessment section inherits the same status. Its recommendations for [[assessment|assessment]] — [[authentic-assessment|authentic tasks]] that cannot be reduced to a generic generated response, AI-supported formative feedback under human oversight, [[process-oriented-assessment|process evidence]] such as drafts and reflective commentary, explicit disclosure of AI use, and consistent institutional guidance — are reasoned from the dimension means and from the literature the authors cite. None of them is tested here: there is no comparison of assessment formats, no outcome measure and no student work. The recommendations converge with what the wiki's assessment pages already argue from studies that did test designs, which makes this paper a student-side warrant for redesign rather than new evidence that redesign works. Its distinctive support is that the population who would have to comply already ranks skill demand above convenience, a more favorable starting point for a process-oriented policy than the compliance literature would predict.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors:** Target support at use and skills, not at integrity. Integrity rated uniformly high across programs, so the case for discipline-specific ethics instruction is weak here; discipline-specific AI literacy support follows from the data.
- **Administrators:** Do not read small disciplinary differences as a divide. A program-level AI policy justified by "our students use it differently" rests on effect sizes of 0.030 and 0.014, and the paper cannot even say which programs differ.
- **Designers:** Survey your own cohort before importing the ranking. The five-dimension structure is a usable local diagnostic, but the published means come from 546 students at one Spanish university, with no per-dimension reliability or scale anchors available for comparison.
- **Assessment leads:** Pilot the redesign instead of citing this paper as proof. Authentic tasks, process evidence and disclosure are design arguments here, so adopt them as hypotheses with local evaluation rather than as validated fixes.
- **Researchers:** Treat a high integrity mean as climate, then verify conduct through work and process evidence. This study measured perceptions and nothing about assessment formats, and the proposed framework remains untested.

## Limitations

- The sample is 546 undergraduates at a single institution, the University of Granada, recruited by convenience sampling; the authors name this as a limit on representativeness, and no response rate or program-level breakdown is reported.
- The design is cross-sectional and descriptive, so no causal claim about AI use, skills or integrity is possible; the authors note that longitudinal and experimental designs would be needed.
- All measures are self-reported perceptions; the integrity score cannot stand as evidence of conduct, and the authors call for behavioral indicators, qualitative evidence and assessment artifacts.
- Only rank-based group comparisons and effect sizes are reported: no post-hoc pair comparisons, no adjusted alpha and no group sizes, so the significant results cannot be attributed to named programs.
- The instrument's reporting is thin in the article: no item count, no response scale or anchors, no per-dimension reliability, and no derivation for the "Level of Assessment" labels in the descriptive table.
- The proposed framework was not measured, so its four dimensions and their relationships remain untested; the correspondence to the survey is the authors' interpretation.
