> **Synthesis:** Teachers are increasingly expected to act as practitioner-researchers, which makes [[teacher-ai-competency]] part of the research dimension of professional expertise. This mixed-methods study traced 105 chemistry teachers through a six-week, AI-supported [[professional-training|professional development]] program built around [[generative-ai|generative AI]] tools. Research competence correlated positively with [[social-emotional-learning|social-emotional monitoring]] practices (r = .664, p < .01) and readiness to use AI (r = .622, p < .01), and all measured dimensions improved significantly from pre-test to post-test (p < .001), with Cohen's dz values ranging from 0.47 to 0.63. Qualitatively, teachers shifted from seeing AI as a tool for information retrieval and writing to viewing it as support for research planning and data-informed decision-making in [[ai-assisted-educational-research|AI-assisted educational research]]. The study proposes an empirically informed conceptual model integrating teacher inquiry, social-emotional monitoring, and AI-supported research practices for [[chemistry-education|chemistry education]], grounding AI-supported research practices in teachers' everyday inquiry and positioning AI as an analytic partner for evidence-informed [[pedagogy]].

## Key Findings
1. Data came from 105 chemistry teachers, using closed-ended questionnaire scales and two open-ended questions administered before and after a six-week AI-supported professional development program.
2. Research competence correlated positively with social-emotional monitoring practices (r = .664, p < .01) and with readiness to use AI (r = .622, p < .01).
3. Statistically significant pre-post improvements appeared across all measured dimensions (p < .001), with Cohen's dz values ranging from 0.47 to 0.63.
4. The largest standardized change was in teachers' research competence (dz = 0.63), whose mean rose from 3.50 (SD = 0.88) to 4.16 (SD = 0.52).
5. Qualitatively, teachers' perception of AI shifted from a tool for information retrieval and writing to one supporting research planning and data-informed decision-making.
6. The study proposes an empirically informed conceptual model integrating teacher inquiry, social-emotional monitoring, and AI-supported research practices.

## Research competence and AI in teacher expertise

Research competence has become a core dimension of teachers' expertise, covering the ability to identify educational problems, formulate research questions, and interpret empirical evidence. The demands are acute in [[chemistry-education|chemistry]], a subject that integrates abstract theory, macroscopic observations, submicroscopic explanations, and symbolic representations and frequently produces persistent learning difficulties. In this framing, teachers act as [[teacher-education|reflective practitioner-researchers]] rather than transmitters of disciplinary knowledge. The authors argue that [[generative-ai|AI]] can support nearly every stage of the research process, from identifying and synthesizing literature to analyzing data and interpreting findings; this study supplies the missing empirical account of how AI-supported [[professional-training|professional development]] changes chemistry teachers' research competence.

## A six-week program for classroom-based inquiry

The program ran in stages over six weeks, one per research stage, each combining three parts: review of theoretical material with demonstrations of relevant AI tools, authentic research activity inside teachers' own schools and chemistry classrooms, and collaborative reflection with professional dialogue. Teachers worked from a self-identified pedagogical problem through literature review, instrument design, data collection, statistical analysis, and a mini research report, with tools such as ChatGPT, Perplexity, Scite, and Julius AI. AI functioned as a [[scaffolding|cognitive and methodological scaffold]] rather than a substitute for professional expertise. The evaluation used a single-group pre-test/post-test design with an embedded [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] component — closed-ended scales plus two open-ended questions — analyzed with Pearson correlations, paired-samples t-tests, and qualitative content analysis under a [[mixed-methods-research|mixed-methods]] logic.

## Results: correlations and pre-post gains

Correlation analysis revealed positive relationships among all five questionnaire constructs (p < 0.01), with coefficients ranging from 0.443 to 0.671. Research competence correlated most strongly with [[social-emotional-learning|social-emotional monitoring]] practices (r = .664, p < .01) and with readiness to use AI (r = .622, p < .01). After the program, mean scores rose on every scale and paired-samples t-tests showed statistically significant improvements on all dimensions (p < .001), with Cohen's dz values ranging from 0.47 to 0.63 — approximately medium effects. The largest change was in research competence (dz = 0.63; from 3.50, SD = 0.88, to 4.16, SD = 0.52), followed by readiness to use AI (dz = 0.54), and post-test standard deviations were consistently lower, indicating greater consistency after the intervention.

## A conceptual model for developing research competence

The qualitative analysis documented a shift in how teachers characterized AI: before the intervention they emphasized preparing lessons, developing materials, and searching for information; afterward they increasingly described AI as a methodological partner supporting research planning, data collection, analysis, and reporting. The share of responses applying AI across teaching and research activities rose from 12 (11.4%) before the program to 38 (36.9%) after it. Drawing on CASEL's social and emotional competence framework, the study proposes an empirically informed conceptual model integrating teacher inquiry, social-emotional monitoring, and AI-supported research practices, with AI as analytic support for evidence-informed pedagogical decisions rather than a replacement for judgment.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Use AI as a cognitive and methodological support in teachers' research, not a substitute for expertise; the gains were associated with a program that also included research-methods training, classroom inquiry, and collaborative reflection.
- **Faculty developers.** Structure professional development around authentic classroom inquiry — one research stage per week, with AI tools matched to each stage from literature review through data analysis to a mini research report.
- **Administrators.** Expect the largest shifts in teachers' research competence and readiness to use AI (dz = 0.63 and 0.54), but treat single-group pre-post results as changes associated with participation rather than causal proof.

## Limitations

- The sample comprised 105 in-service chemistry teachers from the South Kazakhstan region, ages 24 to 59, recruited by purposive sampling, limiting generalization to other subjects, contexts, or national systems.
- The single-group pre-test/post-test design had no comparison or control group.
- No direct student-level social-emotional measures were collected and no AI models analyzed students' social-emotional states; conclusions rest on teachers' perceptions and reported practices.
- The multicomponent program prevents isolating AI's independent contribution from research-methods instruction, classroom inquiry, and collaborative reflection.
- Two participants did not respond to Question 35 after the intervention, so post-program percentages were calculated from 103 valid responses.
