> **Synthesis:** Dehghan (2026) reviews and analytically synthesizes research and frameworks on [[ai-education|artificial intelligence in education]], with particular attention to what AI can do for decision-making among [[administrator|educational administrators]]. Writing that the article is review-analytical rather than a systematic review, the author organizes the material into five themes and proposes a six-stage implementation framework running from problem identification through [[assessment|readiness assessment]], tool selection, training and piloting, impact and [[equity-in-ai-education|equity]] assessment, and a scale-up or discontinue decision. He argues that predictive analytics and [[generative-ai|generative AI]] solve different problems, that AI alone does not guarantee improvement, and that value depends on instructional design, data quality, user competence and human oversight. The central conclusion is that AI should act as a supportive partner rather than a replacement for teachers and administrators. The article collects no data of its own.

*Review-analytical synthesis: the author collects no data and runs no registered screening protocol, so no sample, instrument or measured outcome is reported. Figures cited in the body are counts of the proposed framework or attributed claims about referenced studies, never measured findings.*

## Key Findings

- **AI alone does not guarantee improvement.** The review's central analytical claim is that value depends on how applications are designed and implemented, their alignment with educational objectives, the quality of underlying data and the ability of teachers and administrators to interpret results critically.
- **Two capabilities, one coupled system.** AI offers direct support for teaching and learning — personalization, timely feedback, material generation, error analysis — alongside enhanced administrative analytics; in practice, managerial decisions about resources, training and program design shape students' learning experiences.
- **Predictive analytics and generative AI address different problems.** Estimating the likelihood of academic underachievement is not the same task as generating a simplified explanation, and the article keeps the distinction explicit throughout.
- **Implementation is a six-stage sequence.** Problem identification and objective setting; readiness and risk assessment; tool selection and guideline development; training and pilot implementation; impact and [[equity-in-ai-education|equity]] assessment; and a decision to scale up or discontinue.
- **Outcome and risk indicators must be read together.** Faster grading matters only if accuracy and fairness hold; early-warning models help only when they lead to support rather than disproportionate labeling.
- **Context decides feasibility.** For Iran, the article names the Persian language, geographic and socioeconomic diversity, school infrastructure, legal frameworks and uneven internet access as conditions that organizational strategy must address.
- **Human judgment stays final.** The recommended posture is human-AI collaboration, with human review and appeal built into consequential decisions.

## Predictive analytics and generative AI are different problems

The article opens by distinguishing a system that estimates the likelihood of future events from historical data — predictive analytics — from one that generates new content or responses. That distinction matters administratively: an early-warning model produces a probability that demands interpretation, whereas a generative tool produces text, questions, lesson plans or feedback that demands verification. The review treats both as part of a broad field spanning [[intelligent-tutoring|intelligent tutoring systems]], [[educational-nlp|natural language processing]], [[learning-analytics|learning analytics]] and generative tools, and it warns that intelligent analysis should serve as a starting point for further inquiry rather than a substitute for dialogue with students, teachers and families. Absenteeism or declining performance may reflect family circumstances, health, transportation or social factors, so warnings require human review.

## Teaching, learning and the administrator's decision environment

On the teaching side, the article maps applications onto [[learning-design|instructional design]], individualized learning, [[feedback|feedback]] provision and [[assessment|assessment]]. AI can generate preliminary lesson plans and differentiated exercises, recommend exercises matched to performance, offer preliminary explanations of errors, and analyze responses — each with a stated caveat about verifying accuracy, age appropriateness, restricted pathways and the teacher's continuing role. On the management side, four administrative uses recur: [[learning-analytics|data-driven decision-making]] over resource allocation, curriculum planning, attendance and [[educational-development|professional development]]; early identification of underachievement and dropout risk; resource planning and operational management such as scheduling and staffing forecasts; and evaluation of programs and policies. Each carries a matching warning: optimization on cost alone can overlook educational quality and teacher workloads, and correlation alone does not establish causation. This is the terrain of [[governance|AI governance]], [[institutional-ai-infrastructure|institutional infrastructure]] and [[change-management|change management]], and the paper reads as a bridge between those policy concerns and classroom practice.

## A six-stage implementation framework and success indicators

The proposed framework begins with a clearly defined problem and measurable objectives, then assesses infrastructure, data quality, staff competencies, costs and information sensitivity. Only then are tools selected against evidence of performance, security, [[explainable-ai|explainability]], language support and total cost of ownership, followed by training and a bounded pilot using predetermined evaluation criteria. Stage five measures learning quality, time expenditure, user satisfaction, error rates, accessibility and differences across groups; stage six scales up, modifies or discontinues. Success indicators are organized at three levels — learning, instructional and management — and explicitly paired with risk indicators: the number of identified errors, frequency of output corrections, privacy incidents and performance differences across groups belong in periodic monitoring alongside satisfaction and speed. The framework resembles other staged guidance in the [[educational-policy-ai|educational AI policy]] literature, including calls for human review and appeal in sensitive decisions.

## Context, risk and the human role

The article closes with context-specific recommendations. In Iran's system, the application of AI should account for the Persian language, geographic and socioeconomic diversity, school infrastructure, legal frameworks, teachers' needs and [[curriculum-design|curriculum]] objectives; limited access to high-quality data, disparities in internet availability and insufficient specialized training may hinder benefits, so strategy should extend beyond tool selection to infrastructure, data protection standards, locally relevant resources, independent research and teacher participation. Practical recommendations follow for administrators, teachers, and policymakers and researchers: define the problem before procuring, establish a working group with data protection officers, require human review and appeal, pilot across diverse schools, and commission longitudinal studies of actual effects on learning outcomes and workloads. The conclusion restates that technology leads to improvement only when the underlying educational problem has been properly defined, and that [[human-ai-collaboration|human-AI collaboration]] — not substitution — is the realistic pathway.

## What this means for practice

- **Administrators.** Define a specific problem and measurable success criteria before adopting any tool, form a working group that includes teachers and a [[privacy|data protection]] officer, require [[human-in-the-loop-ai|human review]] with an appeal route for consequential decisions, and pilot across diverse schools before scaling.
- **Teachers.** Use AI for preliminary content development, brainstorming and varied exercises while reviewing every output before sharing it, design assignments that make student reasoning visible, and avoid uploading personally identifiable information to unapproved platforms.
- **Policymakers.** Build and regularly update data-protection, transparency and accountability frameworks, fund resources that support local languages, and commission [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal research]] on actual learning effects and teacher workload.
- **Researchers.** Examine under-resourced schools, [[bias-mitigation|algorithmic fairness]] and organizational cost-effectiveness, and clarify which tasks suit AI assistance and which require direct human involvement.
- **Institutions.** Treat infrastructure, connectivity and training as prerequisites rather than afterthoughts; the review's context analysis shows that tool procurement without them reproduces existing inequities.

## Limitations

- This is a review-analytical article, not a systematic review with a registered protocol and quantitative screening, so its coverage of the evidence is selective.
- It cannot establish an effect size for any tool or intervention, because it reports no data of its own.
- Much of the cited literature focuses on specific contexts or [[higher-ed|higher education]], so generalizing the conclusions to all educational levels and cultural settings requires caution.
- [[ai-technologies|AI technologies]], data policies and output quality evolve rapidly, so the analysis may date quickly.
- The argument is a single-author synthesis of selected sources rather than a meta-analysis, and it offers a framework for implementation without testing it.
