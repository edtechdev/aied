> **Synthesis:** Magwa and Zongozzi (2026) asked seven lecturers, fourteen students and a quality assurance coordinator at one campus of Zimbabwe Open [[higher-ed|University]] which assessment practices resist inappropriate AI use in Open Distance eLearning. Using a [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] instrumental case study, they identified four families of practice: performance-based and experiential tasks, reflective and [[metacognition|metacognitive]] work, oral and interactive formats, and observation-based [[formative-assessment|formative assessment]]. Participants judged these formats harder to fake because they require personal evidence, real-time reasoning and activity in a specific context. The authors read the four families as an interdependent framework aligned with [[constructivist|constructivist learning]], and recommend staff development, clear guidelines and attention to workload. No outcomes were measured against a comparison group.

## Key Findings

1. **Real-world tasks were seen as hardest to fake.** Fieldwork, experiments, community engagement and context-specific [[problem-solving|problem solving]] were reported as difficult for AI to complete on a student's behalf.
2. **Reflection centers personal evidence.** Journals, learning logs, [[eportfolio|portfolios]] and reflective essays were judged resistant because they draw on an individual's own learning history.
3. **Oral formats shift the demand to real-time reasoning.** Vivas, oral exams, presentations, debates and panel discussions require students to respond spontaneously and defend their positions.
4. **Observation and formative checks help.** In-class tests, supervision and continuous tracking let lecturers see performance as it happens and give timely feedback.
5. **The four families reinforce each other.** The authors present them as one coherent framework that shifts emphasis from finished products to observable learning processes.
6. **Implementation has real costs.** Lecturer workload, large enrollments, limited infrastructure and the need for staff development are named as constraints.
7. **The evidence is perceptual.** The study records what participants believed effective; it did not measure [[learning-gains|learning gains]] or actual reductions in AI use.

## How the study was done

The setting was the Masvingo Regional Campus of Zimbabwe Open University, chosen as a typical Open Distance eLearning environment with accessible participants who had faced assessment challenges associated with AI. The authors used a qualitative, instrumental single-case design and purposive sampling. Fourteen students were selected, described as two representatives from each faculty and assumed to have the maturity to reflect on [[ai-misuse-learning-harm|AI misuse]]. Seven lecturers were selected for longer service at the university, with more than five years of teaching experience and two from each faculty. One quality assurance coordinator was included for knowledge of assessment practices.

Semi-structured interviews lasting between 45 and 60 minutes were held with the lecturers and the coordinator; focus group discussions with the students lasted 70 to 90 minutes. Collection continued until no new themes emerged. Transcripts were analyzed using the six phases of thematic analysis set out by Braun and Clarke, with independent coding followed by consensus discussion. Member checking, peer debriefing and continuous comparison were used to support trustworthiness. Ethics approval came from the University of South Africa's College of Education Research Ethics Committee, reference 7673, and permission was sought from Zimbabwe's Ministry of Higher and Tertiary Education, Innovation, Science and Technology Development. Constructivist [[learning-theories|Learning Theory]], associated with Piaget and [[sociocultural-learning|Vygotsky]], supplied the interpretive lens.

## The four assessment families

Performance-based and experiential assessment was the first theme. Participants proposed projects involving interviews, surveys, environmental audits and small community initiatives, with photo or video evidence of engagement. A student described a lecturer shifting to hands-on, context-specific problem solving after noticing reliance on AI for assignment writing. Lecturers emphasized practical application; students emphasized the reduced opening for AI-generated responses.

Reflective and metacognitive assessment formed the second theme: personal reflection, [[reflection|learning journals]], logs, reflection essays and portfolios. Participants argued that an account of what was learned, how it was learned and how it might be applied is too individualized to be generated convincingly. One lecturer proposed weekly entries on key takeaways, personal application and feedback received. Students spoke of ownership of learning; lecturers spoke of deeper cognitive development.

The third theme covered oral and interactive assessment: [[oral-assessment|oral examinations]], viva voce, structured oral assessments, discussion forums, [[peer-assessment|peer assessment]] and presentations. One participant suggested 10 to 15 minute viva sessions through Zoom or MS Teams in which students explain their process and answer scenario-based questions. Another described students debating in camps on a topic set by the tutor. A quality assurance coordinator noted that presentations delivered without reading a written document deprive students of the chance to use AI.

The fourth theme was observation and structured formative assessment: direct observation, continuous performance tracking, in-class activities, scaffolded tasks and ongoing feedback. A lecturer described in-class tests with invigilators moving around, so that students attempting questions at a set time and place "have no way" of getting responses from AI. Across groups, participants agreed that supervised assessment reduces opportunities for AI-assisted submission, though lecturers raised workload concerns and students raised fairness and transparency.

The discussion treats these approaches as interdependent rather than interchangeable. Performance tasks provide experiential grounding, reflection deepens internal processing, oral work strengthens social negotiation of meaning, and observation enables continuous feedback. The authors also describe a heritage-based [[curriculum-design|curriculum]] in which students produce teaching materials, models, artifacts and prototypes with a local cultural character, and argue that assessment should move beyond rote recall toward [[critical-thinking|higher-order thinking]] and problem solving.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Replace some take-home writing with tasks that require firsthand evidence, oral explanation or personal reflection over time.
- **Assessment designers.** Combine the four families rather than relying on a single format; each covers a different weakness of product-only assessment.
- **Institutions.** Provide technological resources, [[educational-development|professional development]] and assessment support, and plan for scalability, workload and class size before mandating redesign.
- **Researchers.** Test whether these perceived safeguards actually reduce misuse or improve learning; the present design captures opinion, not effect.

## Limitations

- **Single campus case.** Findings come from one regional campus and a restricted range of students and lecturers, so they may not reflect other campuses or institutions.
- **Purposive sample.** Participants were selected for strategic importance, which limits breadth and does not support generalization.
- **Perceived effectiveness.** The study reports what participants believed resistant to AI use; no comparison, baseline or outcome measure tested that belief.
- **Implementation burden.** The authors acknowledge increased lecturer workload, large enrollments, limited infrastructure and a need for staff development, all of which affect transfer.
- **One institutional context.** All accounts come from a Zimbabwean Open Distance eLearning setting, which the authors note while arguing for rich description to support replication.
