> **Synthesis:** Wang argues that [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]], however critical, does not make judgment consequential: a student can evaluate an output and still lack notice that AI was used, access to reasons, rights to human review, authority to override, or a voice in the rules. The article develops an ecological framework of [[agency|Consequential Human Agency]] that joins human capabilities to institutional conditions, authority and contestability, and accountability, with human flourishing as the normative horizon. Ethical, civic, professional, and leadership agency are treated as contexts of enactment rather than stages of development. Universities appear not as benign guarantors but as actors that can enable or constrain agency through procurement, assessment, surveillance, and analytics. The article closes with minimum institutional conditions — notice, intelligible explanation, human review and appeal, documented responsibility, participation in procurement, due process, and enforceable limits on data collection and secondary use.

*Conceptual paper: the author describes it as "a conceptual, theory-building article" whose purpose is "not to report an empirical intervention or estimate causal effects." No data were collected or analyzed. Every figure below is a component of the proposed framework or an attributed claim about another study, not a finding of this one.*

## Key Findings

- **Agency is relational, not a trait.** The framework defines consequential human agency as "situated capacity-in-action": people may hold relevant knowledge and judgment, but agency exists only when institutional conditions allow that judgment to matter.
- **The unit of analysis shifts.** The question moves from what learners should know and do with AI to whether their judgment can actually affect the conditions, decisions, and consequences of AI use.
- **Four contexts, not a ladder.** Ethical, civic, professional, and leadership agency are contexts in which answerable judgment is enacted — justification of choices, participation in public rules, professional discretion, and organizational governance — and none is a stage learners pass through.
- **Universities can constrain what they claim to protect.** As purchasers of platforms, assessment authorities, and users of surveillance and analytics, institutions can enable or limit the agency of students and faculty.
- **Contestability needs procedure.** The article specifies notice, intelligible explanation, accessible human review and appeal, documented responsibility, participation in procurement and deployment, due-process protections for AI-supported sanctions, and limits on data collection, retention, and secondary use.
- **Readiness is not agency.** The author distinguishes workforce readiness (succeeding within AI-enabled economies) from human readiness (exercising responsible judgment in shaping them), and treats the second as a capability condition that still requires institutional opportunity.

## Why competence is not enough

Wang begins from a split in current responses to AI in higher education. Restriction, detection, and competence-building are necessary, he argues, but they often treat AI as a compliance problem or a skills deficit, leaving unanswered what kinds of persons, professionals, and citizens the university should cultivate when intelligent systems mediate consequential decisions. A learner may know how to verify an output yet remain unable to challenge a platform, an assessment rule, or an automated recommendation; agency cannot be reduced to confidence, choice, or technical skill.

The article locates its contribution inside [[ai-literacy|AI-literacy]] scholarship rather than against it. UNESCO's student framework already carries a human-centered mindset and an explicit human-agency dimension, and Wang's addition is institutional: those frameworks specify capabilities students should develop, whereas Consequential Human Agency asks whether universities allocate the authority, procedures, and material opportunities through which those capabilities can change AI-mediated decisions. The author is explicit that the proposal is not "Human Agency Literacy" as another competency construct, but an ecological educational concern.

## An ecology of capability, authority, and recourse

The framework places Consequential Human Agency at the intersection of four elements: human capabilities; institutional conditions; authority and contestability; and accountability, with human flourishing and the public purpose of the university providing the normative horizon. Capability remains indispensable — AI literacy, [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]], ethical reasoning, reflective judgment, disciplinary knowledge — yet a learner may recognize that an automated recommendation is wrong and still lack access to its rationale, a right to challenge it, or an authorized human who can reverse it.

Institutional conditions determine whether capability becomes consequential action. The article lists specifying rights to explanation, appeal, and human review; identifying who may override automated recommendations; involving students and faculty in procurement and deployment; establishing due-process protections for AI-supported assessment, proctoring, detection, and analytics; limiting secondary uses of student data; and documenting lines of responsibility when systems fail. These conditions make contestability operational rather than rhetorical. They also acknowledge conflict: institutional authority and student autonomy diverge, collective governance can produce outcomes particular individuals reject, and a right to appeal does not guarantee that an appeal will prevail. The analytic demand is therefore to make authority visible, provide procedurally meaningful opportunities to contest consequential decisions, identify who can revise them, and specify who remains answerable.

## Institutions carry agency, too

A less benign view of the university runs through the argument. Universities are simultaneously educators, purchasers of platforms, credentialing authorities, employers, data controllers, and disciplinary institutions, and they can protect agency or constrain it through mandatory platforms, surveillance, automated suspicion, opaque [[learning-analytics|analytics]], and procurement arrangements that leave students and faculty without meaningful alternatives. Wang draws on critical educational technology — Feenberg's social shaping of technology, Winner's claim that artifacts embed politics, Tyack and Cuban's institutional caution — to argue that designs distribute power and make some forms of action easier than others. [[human-in-the-loop-ai|Human-in-the-loop]] arrangements are not sufficient on their own if the person left holding responsibility has neither information nor discretion to alter the outcome.

For curriculum and pedagogy, the implication is to move beyond teaching how AI works toward helping students understand why humans remain responsible for its use: compare AI-generated responses with scholarly evidence, analyze ethical dilemmas, identify [[automation-bias|algorithmic bias]], defend independent conclusions, and reflect on societal consequences.

## Framework in action: an AI-detection dispute

The illustrative case is a student whose essay is flagged by an [[ai-detection|AI-detection]] system and referred for an [[academic-integrity|academic-integrity]] sanction. The student has the capability: she can explain her drafting process, identify sources, provide version history, and question the detector's reliability. Capability alone does not make the judgment consequential. Institutional conditions determine whether she receives notice that an automated tool materially influenced the allegation and whether the detector's limitations are intelligible; authority and contestability determine whether she can request human review, submit contrary evidence, challenge the tool, and appeal to someone empowered to reverse the decision; accountability determines who owns the final judgment rather than displacing it onto the vendor or algorithm. If the institution offers only a detector score with no review capable of changing the sanction, an AI-literate student can still lack consequential agency.

## What this means for practice

- **Administrators.** Put the minimum conditions in writing for any AI-mediated decision that can sanction a student: notice of material AI involvement, an intelligible account of basis and limits, routes to human review and override, named responsibility for the final call, and enforceable limits on data collection and secondary use.
- **Instructors.** Design tasks where students must contest, modify, or refuse an AI recommendation and justify the choice, not merely consume one, so that evaluating outputs becomes practicing judgment.
- **Faculty developers.** Help teachers name the boundary between supported use and consequential judgment: which decisions may be delegated to a system, which must stay human, and what evidence a student can demand either way.
- **Policymakers.** Pair AI-literacy investment with institutional rights and procedures, and evaluate adoption by whether it expands substantive freedom and public participation rather than accuracy and efficiency alone.

## Limitations

- There are no data: the framework's warrant is conceptual, and the author says it should be judged by the transparency of its premises, its explanatory relationships, and its usefulness for generating propositions to test empirically.
- Sources were selected purposively rather than through a preregistered database search and screening protocol, so the article cannot establish the prevalence of positions or claim exhaustive coverage of these fields.
- Contestation is costly to administer and may be exercised unevenly by differently resourced students and faculty; participatory processes can reproduce hierarchy and can conflict with duties involving safety, integrity, privacy, accessibility, or collective governance.
- Vendor contracts and proprietary systems may restrict the information needed for meaningful review, which limits how far transparency requirements can be met in practice.
- The examples, including the AI-detection dispute, are illustrative rather than empirical tests of the framework.
