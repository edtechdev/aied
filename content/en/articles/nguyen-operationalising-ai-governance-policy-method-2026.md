> **Synthesis:** Nguyen (2026) argues that [[generative-ai|generative AI]] is now embedded across learning support, [[assessment|assessment design]], teaching preparation, research assistance, [[student-support-and-success|student services]] and administrative automation, yet most [[higher-ed|higher education]] institutions still lack a method that turns use-specific risks into decisions, safeguards and review routines. Existing frameworks state principles — transparency, accountability, human oversight, [[privacy]], fairness — but stop short of showing a cross-functional team how to move from use-case evidence to enforceable policy. The proposed response is the Higher Education Artificial Intelligence Policy Development Method (HE-AIPD), an eight-step procedure that converts AI-use scenarios into a reusable [[governance|governance]] package: a scope sheet, a use-case inventory, a decision matrix, a risk–safeguard map, a clause bank, a role responsibility table, an implementation toolkit and a review cycle. Six decision categories replace a crude allow-or-prohibit split, and a traceability design links each use case to the decision, clause, role and tool that justify it. The method was formatively validated through an expert walkthrough judging its relevance, clarity, feasibility, completeness and transferability.

*This is a conceptual method proposal: it presents a stepwise policy-development procedure and validates it formatively with an expert panel, and it reports no field trial, implementation outcome or measured institutional impact.*

## Key Findings

- **A gap between principle and procedure.** Normative frameworks specify what institutions should value but not how a cross-functional team converts AI-use evidence into policy decisions, safeguards and review routines.
- **Eight iterative steps.** HE-AIPD moves from defining policy scope through mapping AI use cases, classifying decisions, mapping risks to safeguards, drafting clauses, assigning roles and building implementation tools, to establishing a review mechanism.
- **Six decision categories.** Encouraged, permitted, permitted with disclosure, restricted, prohibited and under review specify the permission, disclosure, oversight and safeguarding an institution commits to, rather than ranking uses as good or bad.
- **Every risk needs a safeguard and an owner.** A risk without a safeguard is only a warning, and a safeguard without an owner is unlikely to be implemented; high-risk items cannot be classed as permitted or restricted unless an owner, safeguard and review trigger are specified.
- **Traceability is the audit trail.** A worked example follows a student-support [[conversational-ai|chatbot]] from a restricted classification through over-reliance and privacy risks to human-review requirements, clauses, IT and student-services roles and a disclosure template.
- **Three layers share the work.** The institutional layer sets common categories, prohibited uses and data rules; faculties, programs and service units adapt them locally; courses implement task-level guidance and disclosure requirements, with review running across all three.
- **Formative validation found the method broadly acceptable.** Expert ratings supported scale-level content validity, with most components retained or needing minor revision, while implementation-tool design was the weakest component and required major revision.

## The implementation gap in institutional AI governance

The pressure on institutions is specific. Generative AI is already embedded in learning support, assessment design, teaching preparation, research assistance, student services and administrative automation, and institutions are expected to enable responsible use while managing risks to [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]], data protection, fairness, accountability and tool reliability. Guidance documents and AI-ethics frameworks supply principles — transparency, accountability, human oversight, privacy, fairness, [[explainable-ai|explainability]] — but they do not specify how a cross-functional team moves from use-case evidence to implementable decisions, safeguards and review routines. That is the implementation gap HE-AIPD targets. The method is intended for teams creating an [[educational-policy-ai|AI-use policy]] for the first time, revising one in response to changed conditions, or extending an existing policy to new tools, user groups or risk areas; it requires no specialist technical background. Minimum inputs are existing integrity and assessment regulations, data-protection rules, research ethics guidance, current or anticipated use cases, [[stakeholders|stakeholder concerns]], approved, restricted or prohibited tool lists where available, and relevant national or international frameworks. Typical users include academic leaders, quality-assurance staff, integrity officers, teaching-and-learning developers, information technology and data-protection staff, legal or compliance advisors and student representatives; external consultants may help, but the method assumes internal knowledge of current governance structures, assessment regulations and stakeholder concerns. Framed as a contribution to the operationalization of [[governance|AI governance]], HE-AIPD offers a reproducible, traceable and formatively validated procedure rather than a set of aspirational principles.

## Eight iterative steps, each with a practical output

The workflow runs through eight iterative steps, each specifying required inputs, a governance action and a practical output, and later steps may send the team back to earlier ones when edge cases or new evidence appear. Step one fixes the boundary: institutional, faculty, program or course level, covered user groups, included and excluded activities, related policies and the escalation route, which prevents policy drift and duplication. Step two builds the use-case inventory in practical terms — user group, activity area, tool type, data type, whether the output affects assessment or decision-making, and current or anticipated frequency — covering observed, planned and plausible future uses. Step three classifies each case using purpose, assessment stakes, data sensitivity, potential harm, equity implications, accountability requirements and tool maturity, flagging borderline cases for deliberation. Step four pairs every identified risk, from integrity and [[assessment-validity|assessment validity]] to [[privacy|privacy]], [[bias-mitigation|bias]], [[hallucination-risk|hallucination]], [[equity-in-ai-education|equity]], accessibility, accountability, data leakage, dependency on proprietary tools and regulatory compliance, with at least one [[guardrails|safeguard]] and one responsible role; risks are prioritized as high, medium or low. Step five drafts clauses that state who is covered, what is permitted or required, under what conditions, and with what consequences for non-compliance, each linked back to the decision and safeguard that justified it. Step six assigns responsibilities across students, lecturers, program leaders, quality-assurance and integrity offices, IT and data-protection staff and academic leadership, distinguishing decision ownership, implementation, monitoring, escalation and communication. Step seven builds the implementation tools: central materials such as an approved and restricted tool list, data-use guidance, disclosure policy, training plan and reporting form, plus course-level task sheets, disclosure templates, course-outline inserts and student-facing summaries, which also support [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]]. Step eight sets the policy owner, scheduled review, triggered review events, evidence sources, decision authority and communication routes. Outputs are cumulative: a use-case inventory without decision categories does not yet support policy writing, a decision matrix without safeguards does not yet manage risk, and a clause bank without role assignments is unlikely to be implemented.

## Decision categories, traceability and the worked example

Classification is deliberately finer-grained than an allow-versus-prohibit switch. The six categories — encouraged, permitted, permitted with disclosure, restricted, prohibited and under review — specify the permission, disclosure, oversight and safeguarding the institution commits to for each case; they are not a ranking from good to bad. Encouraged covers use that actively supports learning, accessibility, feedback, productivity or responsible experimentation. Permitted covers use that does not materially affect assessed output, data protection or decision integrity. Permitted with disclosure adds a duty to state the type, purpose and extent of assistance, which makes [[ai-use-disclosure|disclosure]] a load-bearing requirement rather than a formality. Restricted permits use only under specified conditions, safeguards and oversight, as with AI-supported formative feedback, student chatbot support or research data coding under human review and data controls. Prohibited bars use that undermines assessment validity, privacy, fairness, safety or accountability, such as AI in a closed-book examination or uploading identifiable student data to a public tool. Under review holds cases where evidence, legal status, risk or institutional capacity is insufficient. The translation logic runs from evidence to package: classification converts scenarios into decision categories, safeguard mapping converts categories into risk controls, and clause drafting converts controls into enforceable text. Traceability is the audit trail. In the worked example, an AI chatbot for student support is classified as restricted use, linked to over-reliance and privacy risks, safeguarded by [[human-in-the-loop-ai|human-review]] requirements and data-handling rules, converted into clauses, assigned to IT and student-services roles, supported by a disclosure template, and made subject to triggered review when the tool changes. The point of the example is that the chain is inspectable: a reader can see which risks justified the classification, which safeguards answered those risks, which clauses operationalized the safeguards and which tools support compliance.

## Governance architecture and formative validation

HE-AIPD separates stable institutional boundaries from local implementation. The institutional layer defines common decision categories, prohibited uses, data rules, tool-approval principles and escalation routes; faculties, programs or service units adapt these within defined limits; courses implement both through task-level guidance and disclosure requirements. The review cycle runs across all three layers and is triggered by incidents, tool changes and [[regulation|regulatory updates]], which keeps shared [[institutional-ai-infrastructure|infrastructure]] and local practice aligned rather than drifting apart. A typical implementation is organized as three to five facilitated working sessions — scoping and use-case mapping; decision classification and risk–safeguard mapping; clause and role drafting; implementation-tool design; and review-cycle approval — with each decision documented through a short rationale, a responsible role and a review trigger. Validation was formative. An expert walkthrough converted the method into component-by-criterion items rated by twenty experts drawn from five functional groups — integrity, examination and [[ethics|ethics]]; educational technology and [[ai-education|AI in education]]; governance and quality assurance; IT, data protection and cybersecurity; and AI tools and [[learning-analytics|learning analytics]] — using a four-point scale in which ratings of three or four counted as positive. Across twelve components and five criteria, the overall scale-level average content validity index was 0.882 and 1058 of 1200 rating cells were positive. Component ratings varied: the highest were the early scoping and use-case mapping steps, while the lowest were building implementation tools (average item index 0.77) and the scenario-based demonstration with reusable outputs (0.81). Three items required major revision and none required redesign. The revision log therefore strengthened implementation tools, clarified central–local adaptation, added triggered-review events, expanded decision-category examples and made clause guidance more operational; those revisions shaped the published version. The authors are explicit that content validity indices are not a psychometric scale and produce no institutional readiness score.

## What this means for practice

- **Administrators.** Convene a cross-functional team spanning academic leadership, quality assurance, integrity, teaching and learning, IT and data protection, legal and student representation before drafting anything, because the method depends on internal knowledge of assessment regulations and governance structures that consultants cannot supply.
- **Policymakers.** Adopt the six decision categories as the vocabulary of the policy rather than an allow-or-prohibit switch, and require borderline cases to remain under review until the evidence supports a firmer ruling.
- **Institutional leaders.** Treat implementation-tool design as the hardest step and resource it accordingly, covering approved and restricted tool lists, disclosure templates, training plans, reporting forms and course-level task sheets, because this component drew the weakest expert ratings.
- **Faculty developers.** Build the course-level layer deliberately — task sheets with AI-use guidance, disclosure templates, course-outline inserts and student-facing summaries — so institutional rules reach the task the student actually sees.
- **Assessment professionals.** Require each identified risk to carry at least one safeguard and one named owner, and refuse permitted or restricted classifications for high-risk use cases that lack both.

## Limitations

- The paper is a conceptual method proposal validated formatively; it reports no field trial, implementation outcome or measured institutional impact, so the method's effect on policy quality or compliance remains untested.
- Content validity rests on twenty experts from five functional groups rating a pre-revision version of the method; the revision log was produced after the ratings and comments were reviewed, and no independent replication is reported.
- HE-AIPD is a governance support method, not a legal compliance instrument; institutions must adapt it to applicable legal, regulatory and institutional requirements and obtain appropriate legal or data-protection advice before finalizing policies.
- The worked example is illustrative, and actual policy decisions and implementation tools may vary with local governance structures, assessment regulations, student-support models, procurement processes, data-protection rules and stakeholder input.
- The content validity indices record expert judgment of relevance, clarity, feasibility, completeness and transferability; they are not a psychometric scale and yield no institutional readiness score.
- Because AI tools, regulation and institutional practice change quickly, the method is designed for scheduled and triggered review rather than one-off adoption, and a policy built with it can become outdated within months without a review mechanism.
