> **Synthesis:** Zou, Wang, Liu, and Guo (2026) follow three first-year [[undergraduate-education|undergraduates]] through four [[generative-ai|generative-AI]]-assisted essay revisions across one 16-week semester, using the APSE model of [[ai-literacy|critical AI literacy]] — awareness, positionality, interaction strategies, and evaluation. All three students showed critical AI literacy across the four dimensions, but the evaluative and positional strands dominated: students rejected AI suggestions that they judged too advanced, stylistically misaligned, or factually unreliable, while accepting ones that served their argument. Over the semester, two students became more selective and context-sensitive, and the third was strategic from the outset but fluctuated sharply with each task's demands. [[feedback|Feedback]] uptake moved in different directions for different students, so the authors conclude that critical AI literacy is situated and task-dependent rather than a uniform progression. The pedagogical claim is that instruction should build [[evaluative-judgment|evaluative judgment]] and authorial voice alongside [[prompt-engineering|prompt design]].

*[[longitudinal-research|Longitudinal]] case study of three focal students, four CET-4-based writing tasks in weeks 3, 7, 11, and 15 of a 16-week semester, using DeepSeek; data from student–AI interaction logs, think-aloud protocols, stimulated recall interviews, and initial and revised drafts.*

## Key Findings

- **All four APSE dimensions appeared, unevenly.** Every student demonstrated critical AI awareness, positionality, interaction strategies, and evaluation, but critical evaluation of AI affordances and critical positionality were the most salient.
- **Students filtered on three grounds.** Feedback was accepted, adapted, or rejected against rhetorical fit, linguistic appropriateness, and factual accuracy — including refusals of vocabulary the writer felt was beyond their own level.
- **They recognized the tool's limits.** Inconsistency and black-box behavior, patterned and predictive responses, and information overload were all named by students across the four tasks.
- **Prompting strategies differed by student and shifted over time.** One student moved from broad exploration to proactive guidance, another from minimally specified requests to deliberate adaptation, and the third used directive prompts throughout.
- **Uptake did not converge.** Feedback uptake fell steadily for two students across the four tasks and rose and fell sharply for the third, reaching its highest point in the final task.
- **Trajectories were task-dependent, not linear.** The authors read the pattern as evidence against uniform or stage-based models of AI literacy development.

## The APSE framework

Critical AI literacy is positioned here as a critical extension of AI literacy. Where AI literacy covers understanding, using, and evaluating AI systems, the critical version adds evaluative judgment, reflexive awareness, sociotechnical awareness, and [[agency|agency]] in [[student-ai-interaction|human–AI interaction]] ([[critical-thinking]]). The study operationalizes it through the APSE model developed by Wang and Wang: Critical AI Awareness, Critical AI Positionality, Critical Strategies for Human–AI Interaction, and Critical Evaluation of AI Affordances. Those four dimensions gave the coding scheme for tracing how students' critical engagement appeared, interacted, and changed across repeated revision tasks.

The setting matters to the design. Participants came from a selective first-year English Writing course taught by the first author at a research-oriented university in China. Their initial GenAI literacy was measured with a 20-item questionnaire adapted from Ma et al.'s ChatGPT literacy instrument for language education (the original had 26 items across six dimensions; the teacher-oriented assessment dimension was removed and two others streamlined). It used a 5-point Likert scale and produced totals from 20 to 100, with good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.83, N = 30). Writing proficiency was rated from first essays on a four-dimension scale — Linguistic Range and Accuracy, Content and Ideas, Discourse Organization, and Linguistic Appropriacy — each scored 0 to 5, summed to 20, then multiplied by five for a 0–100 score; the two raters' scores correlated strongly (r = 0.905).

Three focal students were chosen to represent different combinations. Michael, a Bioinformatics major, scored 92 on GenAI literacy and 72 on writing. Daisy, a Clinical Medicine major, scored 64 and 90. Nora, a Bioengineering major, scored 60 and 70. National Matriculation English Test scores served as supplementary evidence of general proficiency. Before data collection, the first author ran a workshop on responsible use of DeepSeek, covering prompting, follow-up questions, critical evaluation of feedback, and AI-related guidelines and university policy.

## Profiles: what critical AI literacy looked like

Across the four tasks, critical evaluation was the dimension students reached for most often. They checked factual accuracy — Daisy declined AI-generated statistics sourced to the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention and the WHO because she could not verify them; Michael recognized cited data and information as fabricated or unverifiable and left those revisions out of his draft. They weighed linguistic and rhetorical fit closely, as when Daisy rejected "develop emotional disorders" in place of "suffer" because it shifted her intended meaning, or refused a suggested domain-specific term that was too general. And they judged feasibility against the learning context: in a timed exam, data not previously verified cannot be used, and in a 300-word essay an example consuming 40–50 words displaces too much of the count.

Critical positionality showed up as protection of originality and authorial voice. Students rejected tone adjustments that went beyond their proficiency ("a bit too academic"), filtered lexical suggestions against their own vocabulary range, and in Nora's case re-read the whole essay after every change so that AI contributions blended with the original. Interaction strategies ranged from prompt engineering and proactive human guidance to autonomous adaptation and AI-assisted self-learning — Daisy, for instance, saved an AI paragraph containing questionable statistics because it demonstrated how to cite data and structure content, without adopting it ([[learner-identity]]).

Awareness was thinner but present: students named inconsistent responses that changed parts of an essay they had not asked to revise, outputs that repeated earlier advice almost verbatim, and explanations so lengthy that reading them all became its own burden. The authors treat that awareness as shaping later filtering and uptake decisions ([[metacognition]]).

## Trajectories: heterogeneous and task-dependent

Daisy entered with low GenAI literacy and high writing proficiency. Her uptake of actionable feedback fell from 52.8% in Task 1 to 25.7% in Task 4, while her prompts moved from exploratory requests toward proactive guidance — asking AI for its opinion rather than corrections, and insisting on consistency with her style and level. Nora, starting with low literacy and intermediate proficiency, adopted 54.5% in Task 1 and 23.5% in Task 4, moving from short directives such as "Can you comment on my essay?" toward elaborated prompts and the rejection of suggestions she judged overstated. Michael began with the highest literacy score and moderate uptake of 40.9%; his uptake rose to 80.0% in Task 2, fell to about 19% in Task 3, and peaked at 86.7% in Task 4.

Michael's case is the study's cautionary thread. He retained a fabricated story about a student named Maria and invented COVID-19 statistics in Task 2 because, as he later put it, there was nothing better available at the time — a pragmatic trade of source integrity for persuasive effect. His high final uptake came largely from stylistic enhancements, including a metaphorical conclusion he said added something he could not have come up with himself. The authors read this as a tension between rhetorical effectiveness and critical control, not as a failure of literacy ([[academic-integrity]], [[hallucination-risk]]).

Coding followed a deductive [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] approach. The first task was independently coded by two authors with 91.7% agreement, discrepancies resolved by discussion; the remaining data were coded by one author with periodic review to guard against drift. For prompting, each student-entered prompt was the unit of analysis. For revisions, "actionable feedback" meant AI feedback offering concrete, revisable suggestions, and uptake ratios were computed per task. The authors are explicit that those ratios are descriptive, because uptake varies with the quantity, quality, and relevance of the feedback generated ([[self-regulated-learning]]).

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Design activities where students compare AI output with their own draft, identify inaccurate or unsuitable suggestions, and explain the decision to accept, adapt, or reject — the evaluation that the study found dominant is best built through repeated public reasoning, not through a single tutorial.
- **Assessment designers.** Treat word limits and timed conditions as evidence about feedback rather than as noise. Students in this study rejected usable suggestions because they could not fit them into a 300-word or exam-bound task.
- **Learners.** Interrogate what a suggestion claims about your intended meaning, not only whether it is correct — several of the strongest judgments in the study were about nuance, register, and voice rather than about facts.
- **Researchers.** Pair interaction logs with process-tracing measures such as [[sensor-and-gaze-analytics|eye-tracking]] or keystroke logging, and compare CAIL enactment across different generative systems rather than treating one model's affordances as the general case.

## Limitations

- Three participants in one selective course doing CET-4 essay revision limit both generalizability and transferability to other genres, disciplines, and longer-term learning contexts; the authors call for larger and more diverse cohorts.
- DeepSeek was the focal tool, and the observed manifestations of critical AI literacy may reflect that particular model's affordances and limitations at the time, including its handling of terminology, contextual sensitivity, and instruction alignment.
- Uptake ratios are descriptive measures rather than independent indicators of development, since they depend on the quantity, quality, and relevance of the feedback generated in each task.
- The first author was also the course instructor. Steps were taken to reduce positionality bias — grades independent of AI use and participation, voluntary involvement, a non-evaluative atmosphere — but the dual role remains part of the design.
- The evidence base is qualitative and process-oriented: interaction logs, think-aloud protocols, and stimulated recall interviews, with no eye-tracking or keystroke logging to illuminate the cognitive processes underneath.
