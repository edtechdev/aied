> **Synthesis:** Chen and colleagues developed the Artificial Intelligence Literacy Scale for Medical Students (AIL-MS) through stakeholder interviews, item development with expert review and pilot feedback, and a single-center survey. The self-report instrument covers five domains: technical understanding and application, [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]], information and [[statistics-and-data-science-education|data literacy]], [[human-ai-collaboration|human-AI collaboration]], and [[ethics]] and morality. Its scores describe perceived [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] and readiness, not what students can demonstrate in clinical practice. Internal-structure analyses in two random halves of one institutional sample supported the five-dimension representation provisionally under both maximum-likelihood and ordinal estimators, while a single-factor account fit poorly. Three response profiles and grade-related differences in a self-reported performance composite described cross-sectional variation, not within-person change. The paper is an Article in Press; the authors call for external, performance-based, [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal]] and multi-institutional evaluation before consequential use.

## Key Findings
1. **The domains came from stakeholders.** Interviews with physicians, medical educators and students generated the content, which expert review and pilot feedback then refined.
2. **Five related but distinct domains were retained.** A single undifferentiated factor did not represent the response structure, but the authors still describe the five dimensions as provisional.
3. **Scores measure perception, not competence.** Students judge what they believe they can do; the authors warn that a high score does not establish safe or effective clinical performance.
4. **The internal structure was consistent across estimators.** Exploratory and confirmatory analyses in random halves of one sample, plus ordinal analyses, supported the proposed representation.
5. **Subscale consistency was acceptable in this sample.** The authors report no test-retest reliability or external-criterion evidence.
6. **Three response patterns emerged.** Latent profiles described lower, information/data-oriented and higher self-reported literacy, labeled descriptively rather than diagnostically.
7. **Associations differed by grade.** Links between the self-reported performance composite and dimension scores varied across grades after multiplicity adjustment, but the design cannot show change within students.

## What the study measured

The exploratory sequential [[mixed-methods-research|mixed-methods]] study had three linked phases. Fifteen stakeholders were purposively recruited from Wenzhou Medical [[higher-ed|University]] and affiliated teaching hospitals: five physicians, five medical educators and five senior medical students. Framework Analysis of the semi-structured interviews produced 146 initial codes, consolidated into 29 concepts and 19 categories, which supported five higher-order dimensions. Two coders independently coded three randomly selected transcripts; Cohen's κ was 0.78, and after revision and a further independently coded transcript it was 0.89. Recruitment stopped at the fifteenth interview because no new major concepts emerged, a local sufficiency decision rather than evidence that all perspectives were exhausted.

The 19 categories were mapped one-to-one to 19 self-report items. Three experts in medical informatics, educational technology and clinical AI reviewed every item in two rounds, rating relevance from 1 to 4; all final items received ratings of 3 or 4 from all three, yielding I-CVI = 1.00 and S-CVI/Ave = 1.00. The authors describe this as descriptive preliminary content evidence, not a stable population-level estimate. Thirty medical students excluded from the main sample completed pilot testing; feedback led to minor wording changes to three Chinese items. Response options ran from "1 = Cannot do" to "5 = Can do consistently."

The cross-sectional survey was completed by 350 students at one university. Of 360 students with recorded logins, 350 submitted complete [[self-report-measures|questionnaires]]; the authors state that 97.2% is the submission proportion among logged-in students, not an overall response rate. The sample included 194 men (55.4%) and 156 women (44.6%), with a mean age of 22.32 years (SD = 2.66; range 18–28). "GPA" does not denote a transcript record: it is the equal-weight mean of a self-reported end-of-semester academic grade-point score and a self-rated clinical-skills score, each on a 1–5 scale, calculated as GPA = (academic grade-point score + clinical-skills score)/2. The authors center it at the sample mean of 3.403 for interaction models. Dimension scores were summed, and the paper does not establish an overall total as a decision score.

## What the psychometric results show

In the EFA half (n = 175), KMO was 0.903 and Bartlett's test was significant (χ2 = 1749.811, df = 171, p < .001). Principal-axis factoring with Promax rotation extracted five factors accounting for 71.24% of the variance, with [[primary-education|primary]] loadings from 0.515 to 0.878 and inter-factor correlations from 0.449 to 0.590. Common-factor parallel analysis retained five factors, but the principal-component sensitivity analysis retained four components, so dimensionality was not uniform. Subscale Cronbach's α values were 0.804, 0.778, 0.871, 0.844 and 0.894, and corrected item-total correlations ranged from 0.596 to 0.775. A limited single-factor diagnostic found the first unrotated factor accounted for 41.07% of variance; the authors state it cannot exclude common-method bias.

Maximum-likelihood CFA in the confirmatory half (n = 175) fit the five-factor model (χ² = 162.911, df = 142, χ²/df = 1.147, CFI = 0.986, TLI = 0.983, RMSEA = 0.029); the single-factor model fit poorly (χ² = 739.576, df = 152, CFI = 0.608, TLI = 0.559, RMSEA = 0.149). Because items were ordinal, WLSMV was used as an estimator-sensitivity check: the five-factor model showed close fit (CFI = 1.000, TLI = 1.000, RMSEA = 0.000, 90% CI 0.000–0.036; SRMR = 0.038), as did the whole-sample robustness analysis (CFI = 1.000, TLI = 1.002, RMSEA = 0.000, 90% CI 0.000–0.019; SRMR = 0.024). The authors note that CFI = 1 and RMSEA = 0 are index boundaries, not error-free measurement, and that the two halves came from the same institution rather than an external sample.

Ordinal invariance evidence was mixed for gender: although the scalar model had CFI = 0.999, RMSEA = 0.009 and SRMR = 0.041, the scalar-versus-metric difference test was significant (Δχ² = 80.160, df = 52, p=.0073). Internship status showed preliminary support (scalar CFI = 1.000, RMSEA = 0.000, SRMR = 0.037; Δχ² = 54.190, df = 52, p=.3909). Grade invariance was not tested, and measurement moderation across continuous GPA was not evaluated.

[[latent-profile-analysis|Latent profile analysis]] retained three classes on parsimony grounds, with most-likely membership of 115/350, 132/350 and 103/350 (32.9%, 37.7% and 29.4%) and entropy = 0.821; the bootstrap likelihood-ratio test favored four classes (p = .0128) while the adjusted Lo-Mendell-Rubin test did not (p = .1642 and 0.1752). All five dimension-level grade × GPA interactions remained significant after Holm adjustment (all adjusted p<.001), with partial η² ranging from 0.072 to 0.103 and the largest effect for Human-AI Collaboration. Positive adjusted slopes appeared mainly in later grades. These are cross-sectional associations, not within-person trajectories.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Use the five subscales for formative needs assessment and learner reflection, not to label individual students as deficient or competent.
- **Medical educators.** Pair the self-report with scenario work — critique an AI answer with missing context, verify a claim against a guideline, explain uncertainty to a simulated patient — because no performance evidence was collected.
- **Curriculum designers.** Address verification, applicability judgments, documentation, escalation of human-AI disagreement and patient-[[privacy|data protection]] together; these came directly from stakeholder accounts.
- **Assessment designers.** Interpret scores at the dimension level; the study does not validate a raw total, a cutoff or a proficiency claim.
- **Researchers.** Treat the split-half analyses as preliminary internal evidence and seek independent, multi-institutional and longitudinal samples before any benchmarking.

## Limitations

- **Article in Press.** The paper is citable with a permanent DOI but is not the final edited version.
- **Single-center convenience sample.** Data came from one Chinese medical university; the 97.2% figure covers only students with recorded logins, so an overall response rate and nonresponse bias could not be assessed.
- **Perceived, not demonstrated, capability.** The scale measures AI literacy/readiness by self-report; there was no knowledge test, observed performance, external criterion or test-retest reliability assessment.
- **Preliminary dimensionality.** Common-factor parallel analysis retained five factors while PCA retained four; the two 175-person halves shared one institution and recruitment process, and no model-based a priori sample-size calculation was documented.
- **Unresolved comparability.** Gender ordinal scalar evidence was mixed, grade invariance was untested, and "GPA" was a self-reported equal-weight composite whose underlying assessments may not be equivalent across training levels.
- **Exploratory profiles and associations.** The three-class solution had entropy = 0.821 and conflicting fit indices, and the grade × GPA findings cannot establish developmental change or widening disparities.
