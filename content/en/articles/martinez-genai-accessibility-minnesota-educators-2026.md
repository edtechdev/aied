> **Synthesis:** Martinez, Baule, Kappers and Martinez ask whether the [[accessibility|access]] promise of [[generative-ai|generative AI]] matches what educators actually do with it. In an exploratory secondary analysis of a survey of 293 Minnesota PK-12 and higher education educators, they compared endorsement of improved accessibility for diverse learners as a benefit with reported use of AI to enhance accessibility for students with disabilities. Among the 160 AI-using educators who answered both items, 133 (83.1%) endorsed the benefit and 51 (31.9%) reported the use; 87 endorsers did not report it, while five respondents reported the use without endorsing the benefit. Self-rated [[teacher-ai-competency|competence]] was associated with reported use among all AI users (44.0% versus 27.9%; about twice the odds after adjustment), but the estimate among endorsers was imprecise, and no subgroup differences were detected despite limited power. Keyword-selected open-ended responses more often described general differentiation and language support than disability-specific accommodation, suggesting the narrow item undercounts broader inclusion work. Because the two items differ in scope and target population, and the survey did not establish whether respondents taught students with disabilities, the comparison describes response proportions rather than a failure to act on a belief.

## Key Findings

- **Endorsement far outpaced reported use.** Among the 160 AI-using educators who answered both items, 133 (83.1%) endorsed improved accessibility for diverse learners as a benefit, while 51 (31.9%) reported using AI to enhance accessibility for students with disabilities.
- **Most endorsers did not report the use.** Of the 133 endorsers, 87 (65.4%) did not select the reported-use option, whereas five respondents reported the use without endorsing the benefit.
- **Accessibility was still the third most common activity.** After developing course content (64.2%) and creating assessment materials (60.1%), enhancing accessibility for students with disabilities was the next most frequently selected use (32.4% of the 173 AI users who answered).
- **Competence tracked with use, imprecisely.** Among all AI users, 44.0% of those rating themselves competent reported accessibility use against 27.9% of others; among endorsers the direction was the same but the interval was wide enough to include no association.
- **Subgroup tests found no detectable differences.** Reported use among endorsers did not differ detectably by sector, setting, gender or highest degree, though the authors caution the tests were underpowered.
- **The write-in extension changed little.** Four inclusion-related write-ins from paired-sample respondents moved reported use from 51 to at most 55 of 160; the category that mattered instructionally, direct adaptation for learners, added one respondent.
- **Educators described broader inclusion than the item captured.** Of 29 respondents describing realized or anticipated inclusion-adjacent use, 13 (44.8%) named no specific population, and only 8 of 26 learner-directed descriptions concerned students with disabilities.

## Design and measures

This is a secondary, cross-sectional analysis of an online survey of Minnesota educators, framed by the [[digital-divide|digital-divide]] literature's move from access to skilled use; the primary findings of the broader study have been published elsewhere. Licensed educators were drawn at random from the state licensing board database and invited by email, with additional respondents recruited through professional networks and education deans. Data were collected between 15 October 2024 and 2 February 2025. After consent screening and exclusion of respondents whose primary setting was neither PK-12 nor higher education, the analytic sample was 293 educators (200 PK-12, 93 higher education). Most were PK-12 educators (68.3%) and women (69.3%); roughly a third worked in rural settings (33.8%) and half held a master's degree (49.1%). Overall, 63.4% of the 287 who answered reported using generative AI in their teaching, and 19.2% rated themselves competent in its use.

Two select-all items anchor the comparison. All respondents were asked which potential benefits of generative AI in education they saw, and endorsement means selecting improved accessibility for diverse learners. Only respondents who reported using generative AI in teaching were routed to the instructional-activities item, where reported use means selecting enhancing accessibility for students with disabilities. The paired sample comprises the 160 AI users who selected at least one option on both items. Self-rated familiarity was measured on a four-point scale and contrasted as competent versus all other responses. Analyses used exact McNemar tests for paired proportions, Fisher's exact or chi-square tests for subgroup differences, and logistic regression adjusting for sector and gender. The survey did not ask whether respondents taught students with disabilities or had occasion to adapt instruction for them.

## Perceived benefit versus reported use

Among the 251 respondents who answered the benefits item, 194 (77.3%) endorsed improved accessibility for diverse learners, the most frequently selected of five benefits, ahead of increased efficiency (71.7%), workload reduction (64.1%), personalized learning (59.8%) and enhanced creativity (52.6%). Within the paired sample, 133 of 160 (83.1%) endorsed the benefit and 51 (31.9%) reported the use; among endorsers, 46 of 133 (34.6%) reported it. The paired difference is statistically detectable (McNemar exact p < 0.001), but the authors are explicit that the two items are not aligned measures of one construct: one asks about a broad population in general terms, the other about a narrower group and a specific behavior. The five respondents who reported the use without endorsing the benefit underline that the mismatch runs in both directions.

The reported-use item was not ignored. Among the 173 AI users who answered it, developing course content (64.2%) and creating assessment materials (60.1%) led, and enhancing accessibility ranked third at 32.4%. The write-in extension tested whether inclusion work had been reported elsewhere. Among paired-sample respondents who had not selected the accessibility option, four write-ins described inclusion-related work, all from endorsers: one direct instructional adaptation, one instance of training colleagues to differentiate, and two instances of [[special-education|special-education]] documentation. Adding each category in turn raised reported use only slightly, from 51 to 52, 53 or 55 of 160, and only the first category is a direct instructional adaptation for students.

## Competence, subgroups, and the open-ended record

Reported use among endorsers did not differ detectably by sector (PK-12 37.9% versus higher education 28.3%), setting (rural 37.8%, urban 36.1%, suburban 30.8%), gender (women 33.3%, men 35.9%) or highest degree. The authors do not read these null results as evidence of similarity: the tests had limited power and the intervals remain compatible with differences of 10 percentage points or more. Among all AI users, competence showed a clearer pattern. Of the 172 who answered the relevant items, 44.0% of those rating themselves competent reported accessibility use, against 27.9% of others; adjusting for sector and gender, competence was associated with higher odds of reported use (OR = 2.31, 95% CI [1.13, 4.73]), although the model as a whole was of borderline significance. Among endorsers the direction was the same but the estimate was imprecise, compatible both with no association and with a substantial one. Competent educators were also somewhat more likely to endorse the benefit (87.8% versus 74.6%).

The keyword-screened open-ended responses add texture but cannot estimate prevalence. Thirty-eight respondents contributed 45 matching comments. Thirteen described a use they had already carried out, typically leveled or translated texts, modified assignments and notes; eighteen described anticipated or conditional use; four described drafting special-education documentation; four named enabling conditions such as a tool purchased only for [[multilingual-learning|English-learner]] teachers; and eleven raised cautions, including stereotyped names and family structures in generated [[rubrics|exemplars]], absent disability and income range, and the need to review outputs. When the 29 respondents describing realized or anticipated use were coded by population, seven named students with disabilities specifically, five named English learners or multilingual students, and the largest group, 13 of 29, described general differentiation without naming a population. The authors read this as a measurement warning: many educators' working sense of inclusive AI use sits closer to the broad benefit item than to the disability-specific use item.

## What this means for practice

- **Teachers.** Treat endorsement of AI's accessibility potential as a belief, not a record of practice — in this sample, most endorsers did not report the corresponding use.
- **Instructional designers.** Attach an accessible-variant step to tasks educators already run, in the spirit of [[universal-design-for-learning|Universal Design for Learning]]: after generating a lesson, reading or quiz, request leveled, translated or alternate-format versions and have the teacher verify fit.
- **Faculty developers.** Make output review part of training. Respondents flagged bias, accuracy and overreliance, and stressed that teachers must read, edit and own generated materials.
- **Administrators.** Treat uneven tool access and professional development as first-order barriers. Some educators described tools purchased only for English-learner teachers and predicted use would be "very patchy" without training.
- **Researchers.** Align perceived-benefit and use items in scope and population before comparing them, and ask whether respondents teach the population in question.

## Limitations

- A self-selected Minnesota sample that skews female (69.3%) and experienced, with respondents outside PK-12 and higher education excluded, so generalization is limited.
- All measures are [[self-report-measures|self-reported]]: stated perceptions could be inflated by social desirability and checkbox use understated; self-rated competence reflects confidence, not demonstrated skill.
- The benefit and use items differ in scope and target population, so the comparison is exploratory and cannot establish that perceived benefits outpace use.
- The survey did not establish whether respondents taught students with disabilities or had occasion to adapt instruction for them.
- Select-all items cannot distinguish non-selection from non-response, and the rule used to address this may introduce selection bias of unknown direction.
- Subgroup tests are underpowered — only 11 endorsers held a bachelor's degree — and non-significant results should not be read as evidence of similarity; the design is cross-sectional and cannot show that competence causes use.
