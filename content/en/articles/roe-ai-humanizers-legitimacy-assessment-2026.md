> **Synthesis:** Roe, Perkins, Bannister, Furze and Wood present the first exploratory, systematic investigation of [[ai-detection|AI humanizer]] websites — commercial services that alter AI-generated text to evade human and technological detection. Cataloguing 55 sites through a four-dimension dramaturgical rubric and running a multimodal critical discourse analysis (MCDA) on the three highest-scoring, they show how these sites perform legitimacy: recontextualizing evasion as rational self-protection against false positives, mystifying the mechanism behind proprietary "advanced algorithms" and implausible scale claims, and borrowing university and corporate logos as unexplained authority props. The argument is political-economic rather than technological. Humanizers sit in a [[feedback]] loop where [[assessment]] demands a performance of [[academic-integrity|independent authorship]], that demand creates a market for commercial tools, the tools prompt institutional [[ai-detection|surveillance]], and surveillance creates a further market for evasion — and because each layer of the loop is inhabited by commercial interests with no structural incentive to interrupt it, new tools alone cannot break it. Students appear in the loop simultaneously as users of AI production tools, subjects of detection, customers for evasion services, and sources of behavioral data.

## Study Design

The study treats a humanizer site as a bounded performance: the audience-accessible pages are the front stage, and the technology and owners are the backstage. Goffman's (1959) dramaturgy is operationalized through a scoring rubric with four dimensions — **visibility and reach** (how likely a student's search surfaces the site), **persona** (the student-user identity the site constructs alongside its own), **trust and authority artifacts** (the props that build credibility), and **mystification** (technical language and complexity that block scrutiny of the backstage) — each scored 1–5. van Leeuwen's (2008) recontextualization supplies the discursive mechanism, and Kress and van Leeuwen's (2006) visual grammar supplies the reading of logo placement, salience and framing. The authors are explicit that this is a study-specific operationalization rather than a validated scale, and that a higher score means a more conspicuous performance, not a better or more ethical service.

Data collection ran in December 2025 and January 2026 across four geographies (Australia, England, Spain, Vietnam), with Google as the sole engine on the reasoning that it is the most visited site in the world and therefore approximates typical student search behavior. Nine keyword families were divided among the researchers — including "AI humanizer," "bypass AI detection," "make AI undetectable," "trick AI detector" and "pass Turnitin/Copyleaks/GPTZero AI detector" — filtered by functionality, access, relevance, language and duplication criteria to **55 unique sites**, each archived and independently scored. Three sites were then purposively chosen for MCDA: the highest scorers, taken as information-rich cases rather than as a representative sample. They are reported as Website 1–3 with names and URLs withheld so the paper does not advertise them.

The most candid methodological detail is the reliability check. Three sites were independently scored by the researchers before the main round, yielding Krippendorff's α = 0.48 — limited agreement, reflecting differences in interpretation. The rubric was **not** revised or rescored afterwards; the authors retain it as an exploratory framework for directing analytical attention while stating that the limited agreement constrains the precision of any comparison between sites.

The version of record (submitted June 2026, accepted September 2026) supersedes the 2026 preprint, which circulated under a different title and framed the study around "dramaturgies of deception." The retitling tracks the paper's shift from a discourse analysis of deception toward a political economy of integrity: the sites matter less as a product category than as the most legible surface of a market that converts uncertainty about authorship into commercial value.

## Key Findings

1. **Humanizers are readily available and cheap to find.** The systematic search surfaced 55 humanizer sites, and all three analyzed sites appeared on the first page of Google across multiple keywords, so the corpus reflects what a student searching for one would actually encounter rather than an obscure fringe.
2. **The commercial structure is uniform.** Every site offered a usable humanizing service on a freemium, subscription, trial or demo basis, with pricing tiers and — on the largest — an affiliate-marketing program. The corpus documents availability, not use: the paper cannot identify how many students use these services.
3. **Scale claims are unverifiable and not comparable.** Website 1 claims close to 3 million humanizer users and more than 800,000 users of its detector; Website 2 claims more than 10,000 students; Website 3 claims 1.5 million users and support for over 400 companies. These are vendor claims covering different populations and periods, and the authors state they cannot be summed or converted into a student-use estimate.
4. **Trust and authority artifacts are the dominant front-stage material.** University and corporate logos appear as unexplained props placed directly beneath the text-entry box — Yale and Oxford, Adobe and Atlassian on Website 1; Johns Hopkins, Princeton, Netflix, Airbnb and eBay on Website 3 — with no wording anywhere claiming endorsement, partnership or use. Spatial proximity supplies an association that no sentence asserts. Detector logos and detector-result screenshots (Website 2 displayed seven detectors being "passed"), satisfaction ratings (4.9/5), user-statistics counters and testimonials complete the props inventory; Website 1 deployed every trust artifact except testimonials.
5. **Mystification is systematic and quantified.** Sites reference "NLP and deep learning," "advanced algorithms," "1.6 trillion parameters," "state-of-the-art" technology and training on "trillions of pieces of content"; Website 1 claims to humanize over 1 billion words a month. Every site frames humanization as one click while concealing the mechanism, and every site offers opaque modes — 1.0/2.0, tuned for Turnitin/ZeroGPT/GPTZero, fast/creative/enhanced — that are never explained. Advertised success rates run 99.8%, 99%+, and "100% human."
6. **Misconduct is deleted from the discourse and replaced with protection.** Drawing on van Leeuwen's deletion and substitution, the analysis shows Website 2 reframing humanization as protection against false positives — presenting detection as inequitable and humanizing as a rational, ethically defensible act — and as an efficiency gain for a student managing competing demands. Website 3 goes furthest, positioning itself as a supporter and collaborator doing "the heavy lifting," with a student testimonial stating that professors can identify AI text and that the service allows submission with confidence. Nothing in the copy frames the practice as misconduct.
7. **The mask slips where the performance cannot be substantiated.** No site provided a registered business address despite terms-of-service pages and contact forms. Logos carry no explaining text or source link. Social-media icons lead to blank, empty or sparsely populated pages, with Website 3's Facebook presence (over 3,000 followers, multi-language content) contradicting empty YouTube and TikTok profiles. Website 2's second testimonial video obscures the speaker's face while the others do not. Website 3 claims both 30 and 50 supported languages. The authors read these as leakage: errors and inconsistencies that disrupt the performance and expose how much of it is curated.
8. **Detectors and humanizers sell opposite sides of one classification.** In the political-economy reading, detectors and humanizers offer competing ways of making authorship legible: one sells a classification, the other sells the promise of changing it. Commercial actors increasingly participate in defining what students encounter as reasonable, trustworthy and legitimate practice, with humanizers acting as a counter-epistemic authority claiming a guidance function that displaces institutional sources. Uploading assessed work to such a site raises retention, reuse, third-party access and consent questions that public-facing assurances cannot resolve — the mirror of the University of Southampton declining to renew its Turnitin contract over license terms that would have permitted student submissions to train AI services.
9. **Students occupy four commercial roles at once.** As users of AI production tools, subjects of detection, customers for evasion services, and potential sources of behavioral and textual data, they are not exercising unconstrained agency so much as responding to a commercial architecture with no structural incentive to release them.

## What this means for practice

- **Administrators.** Read humanizer sites as a diagnostic signal rather than a policing problem: their existence measures how strongly your assessment demands a performance of independent authorship, and better [[ai-detection|detectors]] or network blocks do not disrupt the cycle while structural [[assessment-validity|assessment reform]] does. Then audit both sides of the market you are implicated in, because the same student text is an asset to detector vendors and to humanizer platforms — so retention, training-use, storage-location and consent terms belong in procurement, not in a privacy policy.
- **Assessment professionals.** Redesign toward [[process-oriented-assessment|process-visible]], interactive tasks whose value cannot be produced by reformatted text. The target is not the tool category but the demand structure that makes paid evasion worth buying.
- **Instructors.** Read humanizer copy as a concentrated version of ordinary AI marketing. Deleting the concern, borrowing institutional iconography, and mystifying with proprietary capability and success rates appear in softened form across mainstream [[generative-ai|GenAI]] promotion, which is why [[reducing-ai-misuse|marketing literacy]] belongs in integrity education.
- **Equity leads.** Treat the false-positive protection framing as a recruitment strategy aimed at students with reason to fear detection. Differential detector error for non-native English writing sits alongside evidence that performance varies by task and disciplinary convention, while paid access and repeated rewriting add financial and linguistic costs — so students whose work is legitimate are among the most persuadable customers.
- **Researchers.** The backstage is unexamined: who operates these services, from which jurisdiction, with what data practices, and whether they actually evade detection are all questions the study explicitly does not answer.

## Limitations

- Only the front stage is observable, so the study describes discursive effects, not the knowledge, intentions or operations of owners and users. Who operates the sites, from where, and how the tools work cannot be established.
- Humanizer efficacy in bypassing detection was not tested, and the authors stress that other studies of edited or humanized text should not be read as tests of these three services.
- Google-only search approximated plausible student behavior but cannot establish how students actually encounter these services, which may also circulate through social media, peers and messaging platforms.
- There is no student-use survey, no audited user counts and no evidence of audience reception, so vendor statistics and the authors' reading of persuasive design cannot establish uptake or effects.
- The three detailed cases were purposively selected for high rubric scores rather than representativeness, and limited pilot agreement (Krippendorff's α = 0.48) constrains confidence in those rankings. The rubric is an exploratory framework for directing analytical attention, not a measurement instrument.
- Sites change, disappear and re-emerge, which the authors name as a barrier to replication.
