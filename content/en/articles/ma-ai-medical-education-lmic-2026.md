> **Synthesis:** Ma and colleagues (2026) ran a [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|scoping review]] of AI in [[medical-education|medical education]] across [[global-south|low- and middle-income countries]], screening 8,136 records down to 33 studies from 2015 to 2025. The evidence is unevenly distributed: four studies came from low-income countries, fifteen from lower-middle-income and fourteen from upper-middle-income economies, with China and India dominating. Reported uses cluster around [[generative-ai|generative AI]] for writing and communication, clinical reasoning practice and digital literacy. The review's central point is that adoption tracks infrastructure and institutional capacity rather than technology availability alone, and its authors decline to classify countries by national AI adoption levels because the underlying studies did not assess those factors consistently.

*Scoping review of PubMed and Web of Science, English-language publications from March 2015 to March 2025, using the World Bank 2024-2025 income classification. Narrative synthesis with descriptive coding; no country-level adoption scoring.*

## Key Findings

1. **The pool is thin and concentrated.** Of 8,136 records identified, 6,058 remained after deduplication, 465 reached full-text assessment and 33 were included.
2. **Income levels split sharply.** Four studies came from low-income countries, fifteen from lower-middle-income and fourteen from upper-middle-income economies.
3. **Two countries dominate.** China and India contributed the largest number of included studies, with smaller clusters from Africa, the Middle East and North Africa, and Latin America.
4. **Applications track resources.** Low-income settings reported accessible conversational tools used for assignments, exam preparation and [[writing-education|academic writing]]. Higher-resource settings reported specialized systems such as prenatal screening tools, virtual case teaching platforms and clinical support systems.
5. **Reported benefits are process outcomes.** Learning efficiency, reduced time on academic tasks, digital literacy, [[personalized-learning|personalized learning]] and immediate feedback recur across studies, largely in upper-middle-income settings.
6. **Challenges are structural.** Infrastructure availability, [[privacy|data privacy]], accessibility, user acceptance, language adaptation, network access and [[trust|trust in AI]] systems were all reported.
7. **Adoption does not equal capacity.** The authors found no consistent national-level assessment of policy environments or public investment, so they decline to rank countries by AI adoption.
## What the included studies actually show

Generative AI, and ChatGPT above all, was the most frequently reported technology. Uses clustered into supporting medical students' writing, communication skills, learning activities and academic tasks. Speech-recognition technologies, [[online-teaching-and-learning|virtual learning]] systems and, in better-resourced settings, specialized clinical systems also appeared.

Outcomes were reported rather than measured against comparison groups. Studies associated AI use with greater learning efficiency, shorter time on academic tasks and support for educational workflows, particularly in upper-middle-income countries. Digital literacy and personalized learning improvements appeared in several studies. Several studies placed AI inside simulation-based teaching and clinical reasoning training.

The pattern by income level is the review's most useful observation. In low-income countries, applications mainly involved accessible conversational tools such as Snapchat AI, used for assignment completion, examination preparation and academic writing. In lower- and upper-middle-income countries, general-purpose tools including ChatGPT, Google Bard and Perplexity supported [[self-directed-learning|self-directed learning]], case-scenario analysis, [[automated-question-generation|item generation]], language tasks and educational communication, while educators used them to generate multiple-choice questions and respond to learners. In some higher-resource settings, specialized systems appeared for simulation teaching, academic writing support, literature searching, translation and [[case-based-learning|case-based learning]].

Publication volume also shifted. Few eligible studies appeared before 2022; the number and diversity of AI-related medical education studies increased afterwards, particularly once generative AI tools arrived.

## Reading the benefit claims carefully

The review's own coding makes the evidential status clear. Each included study was marked favorable, unfavorable, mixed or insufficient for seven dimensions: workflow improvement, reduced learning time, digital literacy, personalized learning, user friendliness, user distrust and compatibility with existing infrastructure. A single study could be favorable on one dimension and unfavorable on another, and the authors state explicitly that these symbols are descriptive only, not a quality or risk-of-bias assessment.

That matters because the reported benefits sit almost entirely in middle-income settings, and the low-income evidence base is four studies. The authors flag their own search as a likely cause of the imbalance: restricting to English-language publications in two databases, using AND operators in PubMed and title-only searches in Web of Science, would all reduce sensitivity and skew geographical coverage.

## What this means for practice

- **Medical educators.** Treat the benefit findings as reported associations, not effect sizes. Where a study reports gains in efficiency or digital literacy, check whether it also reports the infrastructure and training conditions that produced them.
- **Faculty developers.** Localization is the recurring gap. Language adaptation, network access and trust appear as barriers in resource-constrained settings, so preparation has to cover those before tool selection.
- **Policymakers.** The review argues for technology localization, capacity building for educators and learners, institutional and policy support, and international collaboration. It does not argue for a purchasing decision.
- **Curriculum designers.** The clearest curriculum signal is integration: studies from Pakistan identified limits in institutional facilities and curriculum integration, and Uganda highlighted the need for formal policy guidance.
- **Researchers.** The open gaps are [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal]] evidence, low-income settings, and consistent measurement of policy environments and infrastructure capacity.

## Limitations

- Predominantly middle-income evidence, which limits transferability across LMIC contexts.
- Most included studies used cross-sectional or observational designs, so long-term impact and implementation outcomes cannot be assessed.
- External structural and policy factors were not assessed consistently across studies, so no national comparisons of adoption level are offered.
- The search was restricted to English-language publications in PubMed and Web of Science, with narrow search syntax that may have omitted relevant work.
- The review is an "article in press"; the final edited version will replace it.
