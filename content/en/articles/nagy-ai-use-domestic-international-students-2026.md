> **Synthesis:** Nagy, Lukács, and Torkos ask whether domestic and international students differ in their actual [[generative-ai|generative AI]]-use patterns within the same institution. They surveyed 767 students at the University of Nyíregyháza, a Hungarian university of applied sciences (667 domestic, 100 international), with an anonymous cross-sectional online questionnaire covering use frequency, application areas, incorrect responses, subscription services, interest in an AI course, and perceptions of AI's future impact on work; a Two-Step cluster analysis then identified user profiles. International students reported using AI across more application areas (about 3.6 versus 2.2 per user) and were more likely to use subscription services (27.6% versus 9.9%), differences that remained after adjustment for age and gender; their higher use frequency did not survive the same adjustment. Four profiles emerged — Low-Activity Users, Versatile General-Purpose Users, Intensive Technical Users, and Study-Oriented Basic Users — with international students concentrated in the two broader profiles. The evidence is [[self-report-measures|self-reported]], cross-sectional, and drawn from one institution, and the four-cluster solution was only partly stable in split-sample checks.

## Key Findings

- **A frequency gap that demographics explain.** In the unadjusted comparison 20.0% of domestic students and only 2.0% of international students had never used AI, and the ordinal difference was statistically significant with a small effect; after adjustment for age and gender the association disappeared.
- **Breadth persisted where frequency did not.** International AI users reported using AI across more application areas than domestic users (means of about 3.6 and 2.2), and the difference remained statistically significant after demographic adjustment.
- **The pattern was uneven across purposes.** Travel (63.3% versus 12.4%) and financial assistance (32.7% versus 6.8%) showed the largest gaps; studying (92.7% versus 89.8%) and entertainment (20.0% versus 25.5%) did not differ significantly.
- **Paid services and course interest went further.** Subscription-based use was 27.6% among international students against 9.9% among domestic students, and the gap held after adjustment; interest in a university AI course (75.0% versus 65.4%) became significant only after adjustment.
- **Shared experiences and shared expectations.** Reports of at least one incorrect AI response were common in both groups (71.8% versus 79.6%) and not significantly different, and perceptions of AI's future impact on work were similar, with most students in both groups expecting an impact above half.
- **Four user profiles.** Cluster analysis produced Low-Activity Users (17.9% of cases), Versatile General-Purpose Users (27.2%), Intensive Technical Users (19.2%), and Study-Oriented Basic Users (35.8%).
- **Status sorted into the broader profiles.** Among international students, 59.0% fell into the Versatile General-Purpose profile and 23.0% into the Intensive Technical profile; domestic students spread across all four clusters.

## One institution, two student groups

The study surveyed [[higher-ed|higher education]] students at the University of Nyíregyháza between January and February 2026, where the total student population is approximately 5800, including about 530 international students. The questionnaire was distributed through the university's electronic study administration system to all enrolled students, and 767 valid responses formed the sample: 667 domestic students (87.0%) and 100 international students (13.0%), the latter representing about 18.9% of the university's international population. An English version with the same items supported comparability; translation-back-translation, cognitive interviews, and a pilot preceded the main collection, and a test–retest subsample of 30 participants produced item correlations between 0.89 and 0.96. The two groups differed demographically: 64.3% of domestic respondents were female with a mean age of 32.06 years, while 76.0% of international respondents were male with a mean age of 23.12 years. Group comparisons therefore combined non-parametric and categorical tests with effect-size measures and Holm adjustment, supplemented by regressions adjusted for age and gender, and a [[latent-profile-analysis|Two-Step cluster analysis]] that treated AI use as a multidimensional behavior rather than a test of [[technology-acceptance-model|technology-acceptance models]] such as TAM or UTAUT.

## Frequency, breadth, and purpose

Reported frequency favored international students before adjustment. Among domestic students, 20.0% had never used AI, 36.7% used it once or twice a month, 31.3% several times a week, and 11.9% daily; among international students the corresponding figures were 2.0%, 42.0%, 51.0%, and 5.0%. The ordinal difference was statistically significant with a small effect size, and a chi-square test of the full distribution agreed; after adjustment for age and gender, however, student status was no longer associated with frequency, and a sensitivity analysis contrasting weekly or daily use with less frequent or no use reached the same conclusion. Application-area analyses were restricted to the 629 respondents who had used AI (531 domestic, 98 international). Studying dominated both groups (92.7% domestic, 89.8% international), but international users reported markedly more travel-related use (63.3% versus 12.4%), personal development (65.3% versus 35.6%), financial assistance (32.7% versus 6.8%), essay writing (45.9% versus 30.9%), programming (29.6% versus 16.6%), and other purposes (8.2% versus 0.2%), all surviving multiple-comparison adjustment; entertainment did not differ. Counting the number of areas each user selected gave means of 3.60 for international and 2.15 for domestic users, a difference that remained significant after adjustment.

## Subscriptions, course interest, and shared perceptions

Subscription-based AI services were used by 27.6% of international students against 9.9% of domestic students, and the association remained significant after demographic adjustment. Interest in a university-level AI course was descriptively higher among international students (75.0% versus 65.4%) but not significant in the unadjusted test; after adjustment for age and gender it became significant. Experiences of incorrect AI responses were common in both groups (71.8% of domestic and 79.6% of international users) and did not differ significantly, before or after adjustment. Perceptions of AI's future impact on work were also similar: 81.3% of domestic students placed that impact above 50%, and so did most international students, with no significant group difference.

## Four user profiles

Cluster analysis used ten inputs — use frequency, eight application areas, and basic versus subscription service — and retained a four-cluster solution as the most substantively interpretable. Low-Activity Users (137 cases, 17.9%) had almost no engagement: 98.5% had never used AI, 19.7% reported use for studying, and 2.9% used subscription services. Versatile General-Purpose Users (208 cases, 27.2%) combined broad purposes — studying 79.8%, personal development 57.2%, travel 55.8%, entertainment 52.4% — with 52.9% using AI weekly or daily and 8.7% subscribing. Intensive Technical Users (147 cases, 19.2%) were the most committed: 74.8% used AI weekly or daily, 31.3% daily, 96.6% used it for studying, 68.7% for programming, and 41.5% subscribed. Study-Oriented Basic Users (274 cases, 35.8%) reported AI use for studying alone (100.0%), plus essay writing (32.5%) and personal development (21.2%), with no programming, entertainment, or travel use and no subscriptions. Cluster membership was significantly associated with student status — a clear case of [[differential-effects-across-learner-groups|differential effects across learner groups]] — and 82.0% of international students sat in the two broader profiles against a more even domestic spread. A split-sample re-estimation reproduced the Low-Activity and Study-Oriented profiles but redistributed the two active profiles, so the authors treat the typology as exploratory.

## What this means for practice

- **Administrators.** Avoid one-size-fits-all AI guidance: the four profiles differed in frequency, purposes, and subscription use, so differentiated institutional approaches are better supported than a single baseline assumption.
- **Faculty developers.** Treat expressed demand as real: 65.4% of domestic and 75.0% of international students said they would take a university-level AI course.
- **Instructors.** Plan for a wide entering range, from the 20.0% of domestic students who had never used AI to daily technical users, rather than assuming uniform prior exposure.
- **Assessment designers.** Keep self-reported frequency and self-reported breadth distinct: the group difference in breadth survived demographic adjustment while the difference in frequency did not.
- **Researchers.** Treat the four-profile typology as a hypothesis to test; its split-sample stability was only partial, and no digital-competence or training-need data accompanied it.

## Limitations

- One Hungarian university with voluntary online participation, so the sample cannot be considered representative of Hungarian or international higher-education students.
- Cross-sectional [[self-report-measures|self-report]] data cannot establish causal relationships, and several key constructs rested on single items.
- No formal statistical test of measurement invariance was conducted across the Hungarian and English questionnaire versions.
- International students were analyzed as one group, with no breakdown by country, cultural background, or field of study; digital competence ([[ai-literacy|AI literacy]]), academic performance, prior AI training, and technological self-efficacy were not measured.
- Adjustment covered age and gender only, so residual confounding remains possible, and the four-cluster solution should be read as exploratory given its partial stability.
