> **Synthesis:** A qualitative comparative document analysis of 35 course syllabi from one large US public research university shows how institutional guidance on generative AI becomes student-facing rules. The university delegated substantial authority to instructors and set five recurring principles: instructor discretion, clear communication, disclosure and attribution, student responsibility, and compliance with privacy and data-security expectations. Course-level enactment was highly uneven. Eleven syllabi were silent on student AI use, 11 were prohibitive, five permitted specified uses, two broadly permitted AI with responsibility safeguards, and six treated AI or machine learning as an object of learning. Six syllabi were explicitly aligned with institutional guidance, seven implicitly aligned, eight elaborated it, three were minimal and 11 were silent; none contradicted a specific institutional requirement. Authentic or independently produced work was the most common rationale, appearing in 18 of the 24 non-silent syllabi. Governance patterns crossed disciplinary boundaries, while all six pedagogical cases were in AI-adjacent courses. The study documents formal rules only; it has no evidence about classroom enactment or student interpretation.

## Key Findings
1. **Institutional guidance is delegated, not uniform.** The university recognized multiple acceptable course-level approaches and left instructors free to set their own rules under five recurring principles.
2. **Course-level policy is highly heterogeneous.** Students at the same institution could encounter silence, categorical prohibition, bounded permission, broad permission with safeguards, or sustained pedagogical engagement.
3. **Silence and prohibition were equally common.** Each appeared in 11 of the 35 syllabi, and both occurred in Education and non-Education courses.
4. **Variation was not contradiction.** No syllabus was coded as directly inconsistent with a specific institutional requirement; the more consequential problem was uneven articulation of expectations.
5. **Authentic work outranked plagiarism as a rationale.** Among non-silent syllabi, authentic or independent work appeared more often than academic-integrity language, shifting the concern from rule-breaking to whether submitted work still evidences learning.
6. **Disciplinary location explains less than course context.** Prohibitive and pedagogical stances appeared across fields, and all six pedagogical cases were courses where AI or machine learning was itself the subject of study.
7. **Privacy and equity were largely absent from course rules.** Privacy was not foregrounded in any syllabus rationale, and equity, ethics or social responsibility appeared in only three.

## What the study measured and how

The primary unit of analysis was the syllabus, treated as a durable student-facing record of formal governance rather than as evidence of classroom practice. The corpus comprised 35 course documents used between 2023 and 2026: 22 from the College of Education and 13 from other units. From 53 documents initially collected, 18 were excluded — nine duplicates, four predating the study period, three incomplete and two that were not course syllabi. The sample was purposive and exploratory, not statistically representative, and the non-Education group was not treated as a matched control group.

Each document was reviewed in full and coded for primary governance stance, secondary or mixed features, institutional alignment, rationale, discourse register and course context. Six stances were defined, though one — Delegated — was retained only as a theoretical possibility: a rule that AI was prohibited unless the instructor granted permission established a substantive default and was coded Prohibitive. Stances were non-mutually exclusive in their secondary features, and the authors kept discrepant cases in the analysis rather than removing them. Classification was interpreted by one researcher; a codebook, coding matrix, audit trail and textual excerpts were retained for review.

The five institutional principles were that instructors could determine whether and how AI was used; expectations should be clearly communicated; permitted use should be disclosed and attributed where appropriate; students remained responsible for accuracy, integrity and appropriateness; and use should comply with privacy, confidentiality and data-security expectations. Because institutional access to a tool and authorization to use it in assessed work were treated as separate governance questions, a restrictive syllabus was not automatically classified as inconsistent.

## What the syllabus corpus shows

The five represented primary stances were Silent (11; 31.4%), Prohibitive (11; 31.4%), Conditional (5; 14.3%), Permissive (2; 5.7%) and Pedagogical (6; 17.1%); no syllabus met the refined criteria for Delegated. Approval categories were similarly split: Explicitly aligned (6; 17.1%), Implicitly aligned (7; 20.0%), Elaborative (8; 22.9%), Minimally articulated (3; 8.6%) and Silent (11; 31.4%), with none potentially inconsistent.

Across the 24 non-silent syllabi, rationales included authentic or independent work (18; 75%), [[academic-integrity]] (12; 50%), disclosure or attribution (12; 50%), learning protection (9; 37.5%), professional competency or [[ai-literacy]] (9; 37.5%), verification or accuracy (5; 20.8%), [[assessment]] validity (4; 16.7%), equity, ethics or social responsibility (3; 12.5%) and environmental or social consequences (3; 12.5%). No syllabus explicitly foregrounded privacy or confidentiality. Discourse registers included compliance and attribution (13; 54.2%), control and sanction (11; 45.8%), judgment and responsibility (9; 37.5%), professional competency (9; 37.5%), learning protection (7; 29.2%) and critical or ethical engagement (3; 12.5%).

Group differences were smaller than expected. Seven of 22 Education syllabi were Silent and seven were Prohibitive, compared with four of 13 in each category in the non-Education group. Conditional policies appeared only in Education (5), while Pedagogical policies were more common outside it (4 of 13 versus 2 of 22). All six Pedagogical cases were AI- or machine-learning-adjacent. The discussion frames the result as [[governance]] that is epistemically situated rather than simply discipline determined: course purpose, assessment design, authorship expectations and proximity to AI-related content explained several patterns better than institutional location.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** State explicitly whether AI is permitted, prohibited or assignment-dependent, and identify which functions fall on each side of the line.
- **Instructors.** Explain the rationale for a rule in terms of the learning the assessment is meant to evidence, not only in terms of misconduct.
- **Institutions.** Preserve legitimate course-level discretion while setting minimum expectations for clarity, attribution and verification in syllabus templates.
- **Institutions.** Extend templates to privacy, confidential data and equity, which were largely absent from the course-level language studied.
- **Curriculum designers.** Treat AI-adjacent courses as places where students can encounter critical and professional models of AI governance, not only restrictions.

## Limitations
- The corpus was purposive, single-institution and small; 35 syllabi came from voluntary contributions and do not represent all courses in either group.
- Syllabi document formal written policy, not classroom enactment; assignment instructions, learning-management systems and discussion may have changed what students actually encountered.
- One included document was a public course-information page rather than a complete enrolled-course syllabus, and another contained conflicting semester labels.
- Institutional guidance changed during the 2023–2026 window, and complete historical versions were not always recoverable.
- Assigning one primary stance simplifies documents with different rules across assignments, even though secondary and mixed features were recorded.
- All classifications reflect one researcher's interpretation; the study contains no direct evidence about instructor reasoning, student interpretation or professional behavior.
