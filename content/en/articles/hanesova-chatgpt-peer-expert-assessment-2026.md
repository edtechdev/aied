> **Synthesis:** Hanesová and Rovňanová ask how assessment evidence generated by ChatGPT Edu compares with that of uncalibrated peers and one expert when all three judge the same pre-service primary teachers' language teaching competence. In one Slovak university course, 27 female pre-service teachers each produced a concept map, lesson plan, audio recording, and checked transcript of a short peer-directed microteaching episode; the corpus comprised 135 reports (27 ChatGPT Edu, 81 peer, 27 expert). AI-assisted thematic analysis of the corpus, plus an independent human audit of nine cases (45 reports), produced three themes: From Presence to Proof, Active Is Not Enough, and Agreement Is Layered. Verdicts sometimes converged while justifications differed: identifying a pedagogical feature was weaker evidence than showing its enactment, alignment, or consequences. Ten of 27 ChatGPT reports acknowledged limits on judging acoustic features, so claims requiring acoustic evidence were excluded. The study is a comparative thematic analysis embedded in regular coursework, not an experiment, and it does not show that combining evaluator perspectives improves validity or teacher learning.

## Key Findings

- **Same criteria, different evidence.** All three evaluator conditions judged the same four materials against the same eight criteria, yet similar verdicts often rested on different observations and reasoning.
- **Presence is not proof.** Reports could name a method such as TPR or a game without showing how it would be enacted; one expert noted of a claimed game, "I do not see a game in the lesson preparation."
- **Activity is not agency.** Movement, pair work, and games indicated participation opportunities but did not establish communicative choice or language production; the expert in Case 10 observed that learners had "no possibility of choice."
- **Motivation is not engagement.** Designed appeal and predicted boredom could coexist: the expert predicted the Robot Game "would be interesting for a while, but gradually become boring" because its instructions demanded too little.
- **Access is not inclusion.** Multimodal access and proposed adaptations did not demonstrate differentiated provision under plausible classroom conditions.
- **Agreement is layered.** Verdict convergence, evidential justification, diagnostic specificity, and the assessment object could diverge independently; reports addressing different objects were not necessarily disagreeing.
- **Length is not quality.** ChatGPT Edu reports averaged about 455.7 words against 185.5 for peer and 226.5 for expert reports, a descriptive difference that says nothing about accuracy or usefulness.

## One course, 135 reports

The study used a comparative qualitative design within an interpretivist paradigm, embedded in an accredited [[language-learning|language-teaching]] methodology course in a [[teacher-education|teacher education]] program at Matej Bel University in Slovakia. The cohort was 27 female pre-service primary teachers aged 22–45 in a Master's program; documented prior English attainment stood at B1 for 15 participants (55.6%), B2 for 10 (37.0%), and C1 for two (7.4%). Each participant designed an activity sequence intended for primary pupils, then taught it to peers in a 5–10 minute university microteaching episode; recordings captured peer-directed teaching, not children's responses. Every case carried a concept map, lesson plan, audio recording, and recognition-checked transcript. All evaluators applied the same eight criteria, derived from the Slovak Professional Standard for Beginning Teachers and the CEFR. The analytical corpus held 135 reports: 27 from ChatGPT Edu, 81 [[peer-assessment|peer assessments]] (three per case), and 27 expert reports. A first ChatGPT Edu round in April served partly as procedural familiarization and was excluded; only July reports entered the corpus. Analysis combined AI-assisted coding and theme development in a separate paid account with an independent human audit of nine availability-based cases (45 reports). All three themes recurred in every case, and a semantic check confirmed 221 of 243 case-by-subtheme occurrences.

## From presence to proof

The first theme concerned the evidential threshold at which a recognized feature became a claim about teaching quality. In Case 02, ChatGPT Edu accepted the stated methods as appropriate, while the expert distinguished the methodological label from any evidence of enactment in the written preparation. Appropriateness was relational rather than inherent: in Case 09, ChatGPT Edu and peers praised the Robot Game's clarity and movement format, whereas the expert judged it "clear and comprehensible, but at too low a level" for the age group. Alignment added a further tier. In Case 07 the expert identified a mismatch between objectives and the language foregrounded in the activity: the objectives named ingredients and describing a pizza, while the activity focused on the phrase "I like." Reports that connected objectives, activities, and expected outcomes offered stronger evidence of coherence than reports that listed suitable components separately. The authors tie this to [[evaluative-judgment|evaluative judgment]] and to professional knowledge that cannot be reduced to checklists; the practical implication they draw is for [[assessment-validity|assessment validity]], since agreement in overall verdict did not guarantee equivalent construct representation.

## Active is not enough

The second theme separated learner activity from agency, engagement, and inclusion. Participation formats indicated opportunity, not what learners would actually say or choose. In Case 10, ChatGPT Edu highlighted movement between stations, while the expert acknowledged the design but proposed "simple production, rather than only answering with one word." Motivational design similarly fell short of evidenced engagement: Case 09 paired the game's predicted appeal with the expert's prediction of fading interest, and Case 15 juxtaposed anticipated appeal with reservations about sustained dialogue. Multimodal access was not equivalent to differentiated inclusion: in Case 10 the expert questioned whether the proposed organization would be feasible "in a larger group without an assistant" for several pupils with special educational needs. Across the theme, these were predictions about likely classroom effects, not observations, because the sequences were never taught to children. For [[formative-assessment|formative feedback]], the point is that feedback can probe the support for claims about language use, participation, and inclusion instead of merely noting provision.

## Agreement is layered

The third theme treated convergence and divergence as separable layers. Case 05 showed all three evaluator conditions judging a lesson positively while foregrounding different evidence: clear objectives and TPR for ChatGPT Edu, the puppet and movement for peers, and CLIL connections and instructional sequencing for the expert. The same feature could carry different meanings — familiarity could signal accessibility or insufficient challenge — and reports could address different assessment objects, as in Case 26, where ChatGPT Edu emphasized planned structure while the expert noted "very little direct oral communication in the foreign language overall." Those comments were not directly contradictory. This layering bounds what [[ai-feedback-quality|AI feedback quality]] can claim: [[automated-assessment|automated assessment]] can cover criteria systematically without establishing evidential strength. The supplementary length analysis made the same point descriptively — ChatGPT Edu reports were roughly 2.46 times the length of peer reports and 2.01 times the expert reports — without demonstrating greater diagnostic accuracy. An independent audit of nine cases intersected with the themes and changed none of them.

## What this means for practice

- **Teacher educators.** Ask students to compare ChatGPT, peer, and expert feedback on the same artifact and state what evidence supports each claim, rather than treating any perspective as authoritative.
- **Assessment designers.** Require explicit links from observation to inference: criterion coverage and fluent output are not evidence of validity.
- **Instructors.** Distinguish observation from prediction when reviewing lessons — naming a method is not evidence that it was enacted, and predicted engagement is not observed learning.
- **Students.** Treat ChatGPT feedback as one bounded perspective, and treat claims about pronunciation, stress, and intonation as unsupported unless the system's processing of audio is verified.
- **Researchers.** Record product, account, access route, model, prompts, settings, and supplied materials so that AI-assisted assessment studies can be replicated and audited.

## Limitations

- The comparison took place in regular accredited coursework, not an experiment, and conditions differed beyond evaluator identity: peers were uncalibrated novices, one experienced professional represented the expert condition, and ChatGPT Edu followed a structured prompt.
- The task constrained construct coverage: participants taught peers for 5–10 minutes and never enacted their sequences with primary pupils, so claims about engagement, inclusion, and learning were predictions, not observations.
- ChatGPT Edu's processing of acoustic information could not be verified, so claims requiring acoustic evidence were excluded from cross-evaluator comparison, limiting conclusions about spoken performance.
- The two AI rounds cannot establish within-model consistency; complete April–July pairs were unavailable and underlying model identifiers were unverified.
- The independent human audit covered nine availability-based cases (45 reports), was not random or representative, involved no formal adjudication, and did not verify assessment claims against the original teaching materials.
- The first author was instructor, expert evaluator, and lead interpreter; the expert assessments were temporally and informationally independent, but person-independence was not preserved during analysis.
