> **Synthesis:** Atar and Tekin studied how 58 final-year English-as-a-foreign-language pre-service teachers in Türkiye planned and taught lessons with [[generative-ai|ChatGPT]], Gemini and DeepSeek. Using reflective essays from all participants and single observations of 13 lessons, the authors identified three forms of agency that arose around perceived tool limitations: recontextualizing generic output to local circumstances, substituting or rejecting unsuitable activities, and improvising during lessons. The paper argues that limitations can function as occasions for professional judgment rather than simply as deficits, and reads the three forms against the iterational, projective and practical-evaluative dimensions of an ecological agency model. Because tool order and topics were self-selected, the between-tool patterns are tentative. The authors describe all counts as descriptive and warn that limitations did not reliably produce agency for every participant.

## Key Findings
1. **A large cohort contributed writing, a smaller one observation.** All 58 pre-service teachers wrote reflective essays, while 13 agreed to a single observed lesson each.
2. **Three forms of agency were distinguished.** Adapting generic content locally, discarding and replacing it, and adjusting in the moment were treated as different responses.
3. **Lack of contextualization was the most frequent complaint.** Participants often found plans generic or idealised, with DeepSeek and ChatGPT named more often than Gemini for this problem.
4. **Timing was the most consistently corroborated limitation.** Unrealistic activity durations were reported independently for all three tools and were linked to in-lesson adjustment.
5. **Not every limitation led to a visible response.** Some participants described frustration or wasted time, and some activities were implemented largely as written.
6. **Tool comparisons rest on aggregate counts.** Self-chosen topics and orders mean the paper does not establish that a particular tool caused a particular form of agency.
7. **[[teacher-education|Teacher education]] is the main application.** The authors propose training that deliberately exposes candidates to imperfect AI plans and asks them to adapt, reject and improvise.

## What the study measured

The participants were 58 final-year EFL pre-service teachers, 40 female and 18 male, aged 21–27, at one Turkish state university; they formed about 87% of the 67 final-year students in the program. All were completing a year-long practicum in public schools, with placements in state high schools for 33 participants and middle schools for 25. Each prepared three lesson plans, one with each free tool: GPT-4o in ChatGPT, Gemini 2.0 Flash and DeepSeek-V3. A validated sample prompt gave a common starting point, but candidates could adapt it and use follow-up prompts. Thirteen participants were each observed for one 40-minute lesson; no data were collected from school students.

The essays were analyzed thematically through a codebook approach with hybrid coding. Two rounds of independent checking on two essays each produced 86% and 96.4% agreement, used to refine the coding scheme rather than to report a formal reliability coefficient. Decision rules separated recontextualizing changes, which kept the core of an activity while adapting it locally, from substitutive changes, which discarded an activity before the lesson, and improvisational changes, which altered flow, timing or format during the lesson. Counts of limitations are participant-by-tool reports summed across three tools, while counts of agency are participants who described a form at least once; the paper states that these counts are not directly comparable.

## What the responses looked like

Lack of contextualization was reported in N = 90 participant-by-tool reports, followed by repetitive or cliché activities at N = 68, inappropriate or boring activities at N = 61, and timing and pacing issues at N = 45. Recontextualizing agency was described by N = 42 participants: one turned a generic DeepSeek grammar task into an activity tied to Ramadan cleaning, and in an observed 11th-grade lesson a teacher replaced unfamiliar Western pop-culture examples with figures and experiences from students' own lives. Substitutive agency was described by N = 35 participants, who discarded warm-ups judged too childish, redundant or unsuitable. Improvisational agency was described by N = 23 participants, including a teacher who shifted a small-group debate to whole-class show of hands when [[group-work|group work]] broke down, and another who filled unplanned minutes with a modal-verb quiz.

Tool-level differences were modest. Lack of contextualization was reported 34 times for DeepSeek, 32 for ChatGPT and 24 for Gemini; timing issues were 17 for Gemini, 15 for ChatGPT and 13 for DeepSeek. Repetitive output was reported more often for ChatGPT at 28 than Gemini at 19, but participants disagreed about which tool was least creative. Technical or interface friction was coded separately: six participants reported it for DeepSeek, four for Gemini and one for ChatGPT, including verification obstacles and difficulty exporting a usable Word document. The authors treat these as small counts that may reflect access tiers or temporary service conditions. A further caution is that some participants did not respond agentively: one described Gemini as disappointing because of repeated generation delays, and another found the process highly time-consuming.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Treat imperfect AI-generated plans as material for professional judgment rather than as products ready for direct classroom use.
- **Instructors.** Check activity timing against the actual class size, room layout and proficiency level before teaching a generated plan.
- **Instructors.** Keep a repertoire of in-the-moment adaptations, since some generated activities will fail only once students respond to them.
- **Curriculum designers.** Give pre-service teachers repeated practice with several tools, including tasks that require adaptation, rejection and live adjustment.
- **Researchers.** Use purpose-designed comparisons with fixed topics and recorded tool order before attributing differences to a particular [[conversational-ai|chatbot]].

## Limitations
- The cohort came from one Turkish university and may not represent other pre-service teachers or contexts.
- Observations covered only 13 of 58 participants on a single occasion each, and planning decisions were otherwise accessible only through essays.
- Each participant used each tool for a self-selected topic in a self-chosen, unrecorded order, so tool differences may be confounded with topic, grade, prompts and practice effects.
- The essays were written for a course taught by one of the authors, whose guidelines asked whether students had changed AI plans; social desirability may have amplified adaptation accounts.
- The agreement checks covered only four essays, the findings rest on aggregate counts rather than a cross-tabulation of agency by tool, and the specific tools will change as models evolve.
