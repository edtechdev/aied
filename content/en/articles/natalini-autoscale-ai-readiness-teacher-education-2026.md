> **Synthesis:** Natalini (2026) proposes the A.U.T.O. Scale, a multidimensional instrument for assessing AI readiness among pre-service teachers by combining the [[technology-acceptance-model|Technology Acceptance Model]], the Theory of Planned Behavior and research on [[anxiety-and-stress|AI anxiety]]. The scale has four dimensions — "AI-Related Concerns and Anxiety" (nine items), "Perceived Usefulness of AI for Teaching" (eight), "Behavioural Intention and Use Tendency" (seven) and "Perceived Ease of Use and Optimisation" (four) — all rated on a five-point Likert scale. The article sets out the theoretical derivation, the item pool, scoring rules including reverse-scoring the anxiety factor, and four research questions. It reports no participant data: no sample, factor analysis, reliability coefficient or validity estimate appears, and the author presents the four-factor structure as a measurement hypothesis to be tested empirically. The contribution is an instrument design, not a validation.

## Key Findings

- **Four factors carry the item pool.** Nine items tap concerns and anxiety, eight tap perceived usefulness for teaching, seven tap behavioral intention and use tendency, and four tap perceived ease of use and optimization; all use a five-point Likert response format from strongly disagree to strongly agree.
- **Three established theories carry the construct.** Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use come from the Technology Acceptance Model, behavioral intention and its normative antecedents from the Theory of Planned Behavior, and the affective dimension from work on AI anxiety.
- **Anxiety is reframed as an ethical and professional concern.** Rather than technophobia, the items locate worry in privacy protection, algorithmic bias, plagiarism, loss of empathy, de-professionalization and threats to students' [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]].
- **The anxiety factor needs reverse scoring.** Because Factor A is oppositely polarized, summing it into a composite acceptance index requires reversing those items; the paper is explicit that no overall score should be assumed before factor analysis supports it.
- **The proposed relationships are hypotheses, not results.** Perceived usefulness and ease of use are expected to relate positively to behavioral intention and anxiety negatively, but the author states these must be empirically tested.
- **Background variables are built into the design.** Age, gender, year of study, specialization pathway and prior familiarity with [[generative-ai|generative AI]] are collected as potential covariates, predictors or moderators.
- **Intended uses are diagnostic and evaluative.** The scale is offered for identifying AI-readiness profiles, evaluating training interventions and supporting [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal]] pre-post designs once its structure is validated.

## Three theoretical pillars

The framework rests on three bodies of work. The first is Davis's Technology Acceptance Model, which predicts behavioral intention from perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use; extensions applied to education describe prospective teachers weighing efficiency gains against [[personalized-learning|personalization]] benefits. The second is Ajzen's Theory of Planned Behavior, which adds subjective norms and perceived behavioral control to the intention-behavior link. The third is the literature on [[anxiety-and-stress|AI anxiety]] and [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]], which the author uses to argue that worry about AI is rooted in specific ethical and pedagogical concerns rather than generic resistance to technology. Existing instruments — the General Attitudes Towards Artificial Intelligence Scale, the Artificial Intelligence Anxiety Scale and [[tpack|TPACK]] extensions that add an AI-readiness dimension — supply adapted items. The result is positioned as a theory-driven integration rather than a new construct invented from scratch, which places it alongside other scale-development work in [[teacher-education|teacher education]] such as readiness and reliance measures.

## The four factors and their items

Factor A concerns the negative affective dimension: worries about reduced human interaction and empathy, weakened reasoning and critical thinking, the pace of technological change, [[privacy|student privacy]] and data processing, bias, verification of originality, unequal access, and one's own competence. Factor U captures the cognitive-evaluative judgment that AI is a strategic ally for personalizing learning, supporting [[inclusive-learning|inclusive teaching]] for students with special educational needs, generating creative stimuli and reducing time spent on planning, assessment and administration. Factor T covers intention and stated propensity: using AI for planning and assessment, designing activities oriented toward critical thinking, regular use where appropriate, continued exploration, experimentation during practicums, and participation in training. Factor O covers ease of learning, integrating, and using AI tools for preparation and selected teaching tasks. The four factors deliberately place cognitive, affective, behavioral, ethical and pedagogical aspects on the same evaluative level, which is the paper's central conceptual claim.

## Scoring, validation and intended use

Scoring guidance is procedural rather than empirical. Items are scored on the five-point scale; Factor A requires reversal if a positively oriented composite is wanted; and the author recommends exploratory factor analysis followed, where feasible, by confirmatory factor analysis on an independent sample, together with internal consistency and convergent and discriminant validity. Measurement invariance across time points is flagged as a prerequisite for any longitudinal comparison. Whether the four factors are correlated but distinct dimensions or subordinate to a broader general construct of AI readiness is left as an open empirical question. The scale's practical promise is to support [[teacher-education|teacher education programs]] in diagnosing readiness profiles and in evaluating interventions aimed at critical, responsible and sustainable AI use, but none of that promise is demonstrated here.

## Why this is a proposal rather than a validation

The author describes the four-dimensional structure as "a measurement hypothesis that must be empirically tested," and the discussion frames the scale's relevance in terms of what subsequent analyses will examine. There is no participant sample, no item-response data, no factor loadings, no reliability coefficient and no comparison group anywhere in the article. That makes the paper a design and theoretical synthesis in the mold of other instrument proposals in the [[educational-measurement|measurement]] literature: useful as a blueprint and as a source of items, but carrying no evidence that the intended respondents answer it in the intended way. Readers who need a validated readiness measure should look to instruments that report psychometric data, while treating the A.U.T.O. item pool as material awaiting validation.

## What this means for practice

- **Teacher educators.** Use the four dimensions as a diagnostic lens when planning AI training: concerns and anxiety, perceived usefulness, behavioral intention and perceived ease of use behave differently, and a program that targets only skills will miss the affective and ethical layer.
- **Researchers.** Treat the scale as unvalidated. Run exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses on independent samples, report internal consistency and validity evidence, and test measurement invariance before comparing scores over time.
- **Assessment designers.** Do not sum the items into a single readiness score yet. Reverse-score the anxiety factor explicitly and publish the scoring rule, because the paper warns that a composite is not licensed until the factor structure supports it.
- **Faculty developers.** Design activities that address the ethical concerns the items name — privacy, bias, de-professionalization, critical thinking — rather than assuming that familiarity alone lowers resistance to AI.

## Limitations

- No empirical data are reported: there is no sample, no factor analysis, no reliability coefficient and no validity evidence, so the four-factor structure remains a hypothesis.
- The item pool is adapted from existing scales and recontextualized for [[ai-education|AI in education]], but that adaptation is untested and may not transfer across languages or systems.
- The framework was developed with Italian prospective teachers in mind, and the article does not establish cross-context comparability.
- Whether the four factors are distinct or subordinate under a higher-order AI-readiness construct is unresolved, so any composite interpretation is premature.
- The article is a single-author proposal published without a printed DOI, which limits traceability to the version of record.
