> **Synthesis:** Liang, Poot, Schuck, and Heusler (2026) treat [[higher-ed|university]] quantum mechanics textbooks as a record of [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] choices, comparing 36 English-language texts from the earliest publications through September 2026. They code topic selection, sequencing, and contextualization across three dimensions — mathematical tools, phenomena and experiments, and theoretical concepts — and synthesize the patterns into five entry-point archetypes: matter-wave, spin, path, qubit, and axiom. The study finds that identical topics occupy widely different [[curriculum-design|curricular roles]] depending on where they are placed and how they are framed, so the familiar "wave-first" and "spin-first" labels obscure more than they reveal. Only one textbook, Townsend, is coded as a standalone spin-first opening, while 10 of the 14 advanced texts incorporate the axiom-first archetype. The authors finish with a practical roadmap that turns the framework into guiding questions for textbook selection and [[learning-design|course design]], arguing that no single profile is universally optimal. The work is a direct contribution to [[physics-education|physics education]] and to how [[transfer-of-learning|transfer]] across the undergraduate-to-graduate trajectory is planned.

*Purposive comparative content analysis of 36 textbooks, with one author applying the entry-point and orientation frameworks and a second coder checking the topic-level codebook on four randomly selected texts.*

## Key Findings

- **The wave–spin binary is too thin.** "Wave-first" and "spin-first" lack consistent definitions, and most textbooks combine archetypes concurrently or sequentially in their opening chapters rather than following one.
- **Entry points exceed the familiar pair.** Alongside wave-first and spin-first, the analysis identifies path-first, qubit-first, and axiom-first archetypes, each with distinct mathematical tools, phenomena, and theoretical concepts.
- **First physical system and first formalism need not align.** Photon polarization frequently serves as a finite-dimensional entry even in texts that develop wave mechanics first, and some texts open with abstract tools before any continuous-variable example.
- **Only one standalone spin-first text.** Townsend is the sole textbook coded as a standalone spin-first entry point; every other spin-based opening integrates another approach concurrently or sequentially.
- **Advanced texts reorganize openings.** 10 of the 14 advanced texts incorporate the axiom-first archetype, so a student moving from a wave-oriented course meets a different organization of the theory, not merely harder topics.
- **Orientation is not difficulty.** Townsend and Simon both receive a low mathematical-orientation rating despite very different mathematical complexity, because in both, mathematics serves physical and technological discussion rather than organizing the curriculum.
- **Publication date is a poor proxy for modernity.** Modern experimental and application contexts appear in both recent and earlier texts, so course modernization need not be equated with quantum information alone.

## Topic placement is a pedagogical choice

The study codes each textbook across three dimensions: mathematical tools, phenomena and experiments, and theoretical concepts. Within mathematical tools, it tracks wave mechanics, matrix mechanics, Dirac notation, path integrals, density matrices, and second quantization; within physical systems, first worked infinite- and finite-dimensional examples, qubits, and first conceptual single-photon systems; within theoretical concepts, eight foundational postulates, six physical principles, and five topics in foundations and interpretations. The central empirical result is that a topic's pedagogical role is not intrinsic to the topic. The same formalism can anchor an abstract opening, unify wave and matrix mechanics later in a book, or appear as an optional extension, and authors' prefaces show that similar goals — conceptual clarity, mathematical accessibility, preparation for modern applications — motivate opposite structural arrangements. That finding challenges any separation of topics into a fixed canonical core plus later foundational or modern additions, and it is the reason the authors treat contextualization, not just coverage, as the object of comparison.

## Five entry-point archetypes

The study retains "wave-first" and "spin-first" but grounds them in the concrete topics associated with each dimension, then adds three archetypes drawn from the 36 textbooks: "path-first" (interference and path amplitudes, opened by the double slit or Mach–Zehnder interferometers), "qubit-first" (quantum information and technology, opened with Dirac notation, tensor products, and density operators), and "axiom-first" (postulational and formal, opened from Hilbert space and operator theory). The labels name recurring multidimensional configurations rather than rigid categories, and a single topic can support several archetypes depending on framing: Dirac notation can anchor spin-, qubit-, or axiom-first openings, and Stern–Gerlach experiments can introduce spin measurement, frame two-state systems as qubits, or illustrate measurement axioms. Most textbooks combine archetypes. The authors mark concurrent integration with "+" and sequential combination with a comma, which exposes structure that binary labels hide — historical wave openings consolidated by postulates in Liboff, optical path interference preceding wave mechanics in Dunningham and Zwiebach, and the compound trajectory of Cohen-Tannoudji. Neither path-first nor qubit-first appears as a standalone opening; both occur only in combination.

## Orientations sustained across the whole book

Beyond the opening chapter, the authors rate each textbook's overall orientation on three non-exclusive dimensions: mathematical, experiment and application, and theoretical physics. Across the 36 textbooks, 21 show a single dominant orientation — 1 led by mathematics, 6 by experiment and application, and 14 by theoretical physics — while the remaining 15 combine two or all three orientations at equally high ratings. The ratings describe the structural role of a dimension, not technical difficulty, which separates two questions that curricular comparisons easily conflate: how demanding the material is, and whether its development organizes the curriculum or supports other goals. Prefaces make the trade-offs explicit, as when Kok states that mathematical techniques are introduced strictly on a "need-to-know" basis to avoid overshadowing the physical discussion. The "modernity" sub-indicator within experiment and application orientation records whether foregrounded contexts are historical experiments, established twentieth-century technologies such as lasers, or sustained contemporary applications — and it identifies modern contexts in earlier volumes as well as recent ones.

## What this means for practice

- **[[teacher-role|Instructors]].** Do not choose a textbook from its opening chapter or its label; compare how the mathematical, experimental, and theoretical dimensions are balanced across the whole book against your course goals and students' [[prior-knowledge|preparation]].
- **Curriculum designers.** Treat the introductory-to-advanced transition as a change in how quantum mechanics is organized, not only a change in topic difficulty, and make the shift in framing explicit for students whose prior course stayed wave-oriented.
- **Faculty developers.** Use the roadmap's guiding questions — intended orientation, entry point and opening sequence, mathematical trajectory, role of physical examples, organization of foundational statements, treatment of foundations and interpretations, and contemporary applications — to structure a textbook review before adoption.
- **Researchers.** Treat textbook structure as an object of study in its own right, and test empirically how concurrent and sequential entry-point combinations and curricular alignment shape student understanding across levels.

## Limitations

- The 36 textbooks were purposively selected to maximize structural variation across English-language texts, so reported counts describe the analyzed sample rather than market prevalence or historical trends.
- Chapter-level placement captures macro-structural design, not fine-grained instructional time, worked-example solutions, end-of-chapter exercises, local transitions among verbal, mathematical, and graphical representations, or digital supplements.
- Prefaces capture only rationales authors state explicitly.
- Entry-point classifications and orientation ratings are holistic [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] syntheses by one author; only the chapter-level topic locations were double-coded, on a subset of texts.
- Textbooks represent intended curricula rather than enacted instruction, so the findings motivate rather than replace classroom studies of how these structures affect [[learners]].
