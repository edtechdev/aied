> **Synthesis:** Chen and Liu (2026) synthesize 59 studies with a combined sample of 20,391 teachers and pre-service teachers across 24 countries to ask how [[self-efficacy|teacher self-efficacy]] in [[teacher-ai-competency|Teacher-AI collaboration]] (TSE-AI) is conceptualized, measured and related to practice. Their answer is that TSE-AI is not a single trait but a dynamic, context-sensitive integration of operational confidence and [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] confidence, centered on a [[teacher-role|teacher]]'s belief that they can critically evaluate AI output and take part in joint instructional decisions. The review finds a bidirectional relationship: efficacy supports [[human-ai-collaboration|collaboration]] with AI, and successful collaboration builds efficacy. Calibration is the pivot — appropriately calibrated efficacy translates into deeper collaboration, while exaggerated efficacy predicts [[cognitive-offloading|overreliance]] on AI and may erode the teacher's own instructional and classroom-management skill. Measurement is the weak point: three instrument families are in use and none has been validated as a standard reference.

## Key Findings
1. **The evidence base is recent and large but uneven.** Fifty-nine studies covering 20,391 participants in 24 countries met inclusion; 36 were published in 2025 and 14 in 2024, together 84.7% of the total.
2. **Three instrument families measure TSE-AI, and none is standard.** Studies adapted classic Teacher Efficacy Scales, built domain-specific AI self-efficacy scales such as the TAICS scale, or borrowed technology self-efficacy scales, leaving scores incomparable across studies.
3. **TSE-AI is conceptualized as a dynamic integration, not a trait.** The construct combines operational confidence with pedagogical efficacy, and its core is the belief that one can critically evaluate AI output and share in pedagogical decision-making.
4. **The relationship with collaboration runs in both directions.** Efficacy predicts willingness, depth of use and instructional interaction, while successful AI-supported teaching and institutional support in turn strengthen efficacy.
5. **Calibration separates productive from harmful confidence.** Appropriately calibrated efficacy supports effective collaboration; exaggerated efficacy may lead to overreliance and undermine core instructional and classroom-management skills.
6. **Antecedents cluster into person, behavior and environment.** Mastery experiences, structured AI activities such as prompt generation and output evaluation, and institutional AI support build efficacy; verbal persuasion alone showed no significant effect in one included study.
7. **Quality is moderate and the geography is narrow.** The [[mixed-methods-research|Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool]] rated 81.4% of included studies high or moderate, but nine studies (18.6%) were low, and samples concentrated in Asia, North America, the Middle East and the European Union.

## How the review was built

The search followed PRISMA across five databases (Web of Science, ERIC, Elsevier, Taylor and Francis, and ACM Digital Library), ran in January 2025 with a second screen on 31 December 2025, and began with 1,881 records. After 166 duplicates were removed, 1,715 titles and abstracts were screened; 259 reports were sought, 249 full texts were assessed, and 59 studies were included. Two authors coded independently, with a Cohen's Kappa of 0.86 on the categorization of scale types and measured content, and resolved disagreements by discussion. The review restricted inclusion to English-language studies published between 2021 and 2025 that measured both AI-related collaboration and self-efficacy, excluding reviews and algorithm-only work.

That design explains what the review can and cannot say. It is a thematic synthesis, not a [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|meta-analysis]]: the authors classify TSE-AI as an antecedent, mediator or outcome and describe recurring themes rather than pooling effect sizes. The coding of study quality was used to weight the synthesis, with high- and moderate-quality studies given greater weight and low-quality studies treated as supplementary evidence.

## What TSE-AI is, and how it is measured

The conceptual core comes from Bandura's social cognitive theory: 53 of the 59 studies grounded their measure there. TSE-AI is described as a higher-order [[motivation|motivational]] belief that builds on, but is distinct from, the knowledge base captured by [[tpack|TPACK]] and the operational confidence captured by the [[technology-acceptance-model]]. Knowing how to operate a tool is not the same as believing one can judge its output, correct its errors and lead a lesson in which it plays a part.

The instruments diverge along that distinction. Adapted Teacher Efficacy Scales reword established items for AI-mediated teaching; domain-specific scales such as TAICS measure AI knowledge, pedagogy, [[assessment|assessment]], ethics, human-centered education and professional engagement; technology self-efficacy scales measure confidence with specific technical tasks. [[generative-ai|Generative AI]] self-efficacy is an emerging subcategory. Because no scale has been validated as a standard reference, cross-study comparison of levels is unsafe, and the review treats the heterogeneity itself as a finding.

## What predicts and sustains it

Antecedents fall into three groups. Person-level factors include motivational and affective states, [[ethics|ethical awareness]], and general technology self-efficacy. Behavioral factors include structured practice with AI: teachers who generated prompts, evaluated outputs, adapted content and planned lessons reported stronger efficacy and stronger intentions. Environmental factors include institutional AI support, school conditions, and [[educational-development|professional development]]. The path from support to practice is described as indirect: support raises efficacy, efficacy changes cognitive and motivational experience, and that in turn shapes willingness to collaborate.

The review is careful about the reverse direction. Collaboration can also erode efficacy when teachers experience ill-defined roles, technological dependence or uncritical [[trust|trust in AI]] output. That bidirectional framing matters for [[professional-training]]: a workshop that raises confidence without raising the skill to evaluate output may produce the overconfident pattern the review warns about.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Treat confidence with AI as something to calibrate, not maximize. Build routines in which you check an output against evidence and your own subject knowledge before accepting it, and notice when a fluent answer is doing the judging for you.
- **Faculty developers.** Design professional learning around structured AI tasks — prompt generation, output evaluation, error correction, lesson redesign — rather than around tool demonstrations, because those are the experiences the reviewed studies associate with efficacy.
- **Administrators.** Pair any AI roll-out with explicit role definitions and support. Ill-defined collaboration roles and unsupported expectations were associated with eroded efficacy, not just with slower adoption.
- **Researchers.** Do not compare TSE-AI levels across studies until an instrument is validated as a standard. Report which instrument family was used, and prefer designs that can separate selection into AI use from the effect of using it.

## Limitations

- Fifty-nine studies, English-language and published 2021–2025 only, so earlier work and non-English research are absent.
- The synthesis is thematic rather than statistical; no pooled effect size is reported and the authors do not claim one.
- Most included studies are cross-sectional and [[self-report-measures|self-reported]], which limits claims about direction and about actual classroom behavior.
- Measurement heterogeneity is unresolved: no instrument is validated as a standard, so the review maps constructs rather than estimating their relationships precisely.
- Samples concentrate in Asia, North America, the Middle East and the European Union, with research in South America and Africa described as sparse.
