> **Synthesis:** Dada surveys 412 academic librarians across 38 Nigerian universities, polytechnics and colleges of education to ask how they engage students on [[information-literacy|information literacy]] and [[ai-literacy|AI instruction]], how competent they feel, what blocks them and what predicts effectiveness. The pattern is a competence paradox: librarians rate themselves high on traditional information-literacy delivery (M = 4.02) and database instruction (M = 4.07), but their composite AI competency sits below the scale midpoint (M = 2.86), with the weakest items being designing AI literacy objectives (M = 2.70) and assessing AI literacy outcomes (M = 2.62). Face-to-face reference and formal instruction dominate engagement; AI orientation sessions are the least-used mode (M = 2.77), and 50.7% say they rarely run them.

## Key Findings

1. The survey drew 412 usable responses from 450 instruments (91.6%) across 38 federal and state institutions, using stratified random sampling by institution type, geopolitical zone and librarian cadre; fieldwork ran March–May 2023.
2. Traditional engagement dominates: in-person reference consultation has M = 4.10 (SD = 0.96) and formal library instruction M = 4.05 (SD = 0.93), against embedded librarianship M = 3.05 and AI tool orientation M = 2.77 (the lowest of eight modes).
3. For AI tool orientation sessions, 50.7% selected "rarely" and only 7.3% selected "always".
4. Self-reported competence is high for traditional work — database search and retrieval instruction M = 4.07 (61.2% high) and information-literacy instruction M = 4.02 (58.7% high).
5. Composite AI competency is below the midpoint at M = 2.86 (SD = 0.71); understanding AI concepts M = 2.88, evaluating the credibility of AI-generated content M = 3.07, teaching ethical AI use M = 3.00, integrating AI into information-literacy sessions M = 2.78.
6. The weakest items are instructional design and assessment: designing AI literacy learning objectives M = 2.70 (47.1% low) and assessing students' AI literacy outcomes M = 2.62 (51.9% low).
7. Infrastructure is the top barrier: inadequate internet connectivity and digital infrastructure drew 87.4% agreement (M = 4.38, SD = 0.74), followed by the absence of a formal AI literacy curriculum in LIS training at 84.2% (M = 4.27) and limited professional development at 82.5% (M = 4.20).
8. Attitudinal barriers rank last: student low awareness or motivation 71.4% (M = 3.89), heavy workload 68.9% (M = 3.79) and ethical or copyright concerns 61.2% (M = 3.55).
9. The regression model explains 52% of variance in instructional effectiveness (R² = 0.52, F(3, 408) = 148.27, p < 0.001): engagement frequency β = 0.41, prior AI training β = 0.33 and perceived administrative support β = 0.29, all significant.
10. The author recommends embedding AI literacy in library frameworks, mandating structured professional development and adding AI instruction to national library standards.

## Confidence and competence diverge

The most useful result is the gap between two self-ratings on the same instrument. Librarians who feel competent teaching database search do not feel competent designing AI literacy instruction or assessing whether it worked — and those are not the same skill at a higher difficulty. One is a delivery skill, the other a design skill, and the survey locates the deficit precisely at the design and assessment end.

That matters because the barriers list is often read as a funding story. Infrastructure is indeed the top-rated barrier, but the next two are curricular and developmental: no formal AI content in LIS training and limited professional development. A library that solves connectivity and buys tools without touching those will move the infrastructure score and leave the competency score where it is.

## What the regression does and does not establish

Engagement frequency is the strongest predictor of self-reported effectiveness, followed by prior training and administrative support. All three are perceptions measured in the same instrument at the same moment, which means the model describes a coherent attitude structure rather than a causal chain. A librarian who runs more sessions and has had training reports that the sessions work; whether students learned more is not measured.

The study is also silent on sampling and response bias: a 91.6% response rate is presented as a strength, and no limitations section discusses generalizability. The population is one country's academic librarians, surveyed in 2023 before the current generation of agentic tools. Those boundaries should travel with every number.

## What this means for practice

- **Train for design and assessment, not awareness.** The weakest self-rated skills are writing AI literacy objectives and assessing outcomes; a workshop on tools does not address either.
- **Make AI orientation a scheduled mode.** It is the least-used engagement mode in the survey, which suggests it is nobody's assigned job rather than that it was tried and rejected.
- **Fix infrastructure and curriculum together.** Connectivity is the top barrier, but the curricular gap ranks second; treating either alone leaves the other in place.
- **Give the work administrative cover.** Perceived support is a significant predictor; a librarian running AI instruction without a policy or a mandate is carrying a risk the institution has not accepted.

## Limitations

- The design is a single cross-sectional survey from March–May 2023; it cannot support causal claims, and the author describes the work as a diagnostic snapshot.
- All competency and effectiveness measures are self-report, and the study does not validate them against observed teaching or student outcomes.
- The article has no dedicated limitations section and does not discuss sampling, response bias or generalizability.
- The reported item counts in the method (12 engagement items, 14 barrier items) do not match the eight modes and ten barriers reported in the tables.
- The sample is confined to Nigerian tertiary institutions and predates the current generation of generative and agentic tools.
