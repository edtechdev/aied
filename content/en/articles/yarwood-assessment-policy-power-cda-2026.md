> **Synthesis:** Yarwood (2026) analyzed assessment policy documents from five UK [[higher-ed|universities]] using Fairclough's critical discourse analysis and found four dominant discursive themes: efficiency, surveillance, standardization and pastoral power. Each one narrows the space for flexible, student-centered assessment. Formal policy texts set structural barriers, and wider institutional discourse reinforces them. Where [[generative-ai|artificial intelligence]] appears at all it sits in supplementary guidance rather than core policy, and it is framed through detection and misconduct rather than as a formative tool.

*Critical discourse analysis of published assessment policy documents from five UK universities, read across Fairclough's textual, discursive-practice and social-practice dimensions. Document analysis only; no staff or student participants.*

## Key Findings

1. **Four themes recur.** Efficiency, surveillance, standardization and pastoral power each appear across the documents, and they interact rather than standing alone.
2. **Efficiency is an [[assessment|assessment design]] value.** Fixed deadlines, capped reassessment and restricted extensions are written as institutional priorities, which pushes out experimentation and iterative work.
3. **Surveillance is the default posture.** [[ai-detection|Plagiarism detection]] software, mandatory submission systems and institutional ownership of examination scripts construct students as potential violators and academics as regulators.
4. **AI was largely absent from the core policy texts.** Where it appeared at all it sat in supplementary guidance and was framed around misconduct detection. The authors treat that marginal position as itself a finding: AI has not entered foundational assessment governance.
5. **Standardisation privileges measurability.** Uniform grade boundaries, centralised moderation and quantified outcomes marginalize interpretive judgment and creative demonstration of learning.
6. **Support is conditional.** Extensions and special consideration require formal evidence and panel approval, so flexibility becomes a concession administered by the institution rather than a [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] entitlement.
7. **Formal policy is not the whole story.** Broader institutional discourse and risk-averse culture maintain the same constraints, and the author argues both must be addressed.
## How the analysis was done

The study reads each document at three levels: the text itself, the discursive practice that produces and circulates it, and the social practice it sustains. That structure is what lets the author move from repeated words to institutional effects, since the argument is about how policy language constructs who students and staff are allowed to be. A word-frequency analysis supports the thematic coding without driving it.

## What the themes add up to

Taken together the themes describe an audit culture rather than a set of isolated rules. standardized grading feeds surveillance because moderation and common scales are monitoring mechanisms as well as quality controls. Surveillance feeds efficiency because procedural compliance is cheap to verify. Pastoral arrangements look like a counterweight but operate inside the same framework of approval and verification, which keeps institutional authority intact while appearing responsive.

The consequence for innovation is specific. Models the author names, including phenomenon-based learning, competency-based assessment and ungrading, require discretion and variation that the policies described here do not accommodate.

## Artificial intelligence in the picture

The paper does not evaluate AI as a technology; it examines the discursive environment in which institutions frame it. Two observations matter. AI-specific language is peripheral rather than central, so institutions have not yet decided where AI belongs in assessment governance. And where the wider literature does address AI, it positions the technology inside existing surveillant logics, emphasizing misconduct detection over pedagogical possibility. The author links this to a genuine knowledge gap rather than only to managerial control.

That leads to the paper's sharpest caveat. Repositioning AI toward formative support requires policy that delegates authority and accountability together; delegating agency to individual academics without corresponding frameworks leaves people self-governing from whatever guidance they can find.

## What this means for practice

- **Policymakers.** If you want flexible assessment, write flexibility into the policy text rather than relying on local discretion that the text contradicts.
- **Administrators.** Treat detection-centered AI guidance as a symptom, not a position. Supplementary documents that only govern misconduct will keep teaching formative uses informal and uneven.
- **Faculty developers.** Expect to spend time translating between policy language and classroom judgment, because the two are currently constructed against each other.
- **Instructional designers.** Disciplinary differences matter most where standardization bites. Build the case for variation explicitly rather than assuming a shared assessment format serves every field.
- **Researchers.** Policy [[educational-nlp|text analysis]] cannot show how these constraints are negotiated in practice. Interviews or ethnographic work would test whether the patterns identified here hold in lived experience.

## Limitations

- Documents only. Staff and student interpretation and enactment are outside the study, so the findings describe institutional discourse rather than lived practice.
- Variation in how detailed each university's documents are shapes how strongly each theme can be read.
- Five UK institutions, so the patterns are indicative of that system rather than generalizable.
