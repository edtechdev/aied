> **Synthesis:** Hosseini and colleagues (2026) develop a conceptual framework, the Critical AI Engagement Framework (CAIEF), for rethinking [[ai-literacy]] as it applies to researchers. They argue that treating literacy as a set of individual competencies leaves the institutional, economic and colonial conditions of AI-mediated knowledge production untouched. The framework sets out three dimensions — epistemic posture, structural consciousness and epistemic accompaniment — that together condition [[agency|epistemic agency]] through critical praxis, and describes positions researchers move between rather than stages they pass through. It distinguishes [[cognitive-offloading|cognitive offloading]], the delegation of mental effort, from epistemic offloading, the delegation of work that forms, interprets or justifies a knowledge claim. An AI system can reproduce the form of accompaniment without the reciprocal vulnerability that makes a relation accountable, so epistemic work can be delegated while responsibility cannot. Counts quoted from other frameworks are attributed readings, not findings of this article. This article develops a conceptual framework and reports no empirical data of its own: there is no sample, instrument or measured outcome, and every figure it cites from other studies is an attributed claim rather than its own finding.

## Key Findings
1. **A framework instead of a competency list.** CAIEF is proposed as an interrogative tool for examining how AI engagement affects a researcher's capacity to know, judge, remain accountable and act collectively, not as a replacement checklist of skills.
2. **Three dimensions condition epistemic agency.** Epistemic posture, structural consciousness and epistemic accompaniment operate simultaneously; critical praxis connects recognition to deliberate individual and collective action.
3. **Positions are not stages.** The framework maps where researchers stand in relation to a task, a phase and the relations available; a researcher may hold different positions within one project, and criticality is not the same as refusal or heavier use.
4. **Offloading has two distinct forms.** Cognitive offloading delegates effort; epistemic offloading delegates work that forms, selects, interprets or justifies a knowledge claim, and the two carry different accountability demands.
5. **AI can mimic accompaniment.** Dialogic prompting and iteration can look like a relation that holds a researcher answerable, but without reciprocal vulnerability nothing on the other side has scholarly standing at stake.
6. **The framework applies across the research lifecycle.** It is worked through from literature searching and framing to publication and dissemination, and the authors state it is equally relevant to teaching.
7. **Disclosure alone cannot secure integrity.** The authors recast integrity as a question of the relations through which researchers remain answerable for what becomes knowledge, including participants and communities outside the academy.

## Reframing AI literacy as structural and epistemic practice
Existing frameworks, as the authors describe them, identify competency dimensions, map them onto a developmental progression and make the more capable individual the goal. Long and Magerko's (2020) framework is characterized as offering seventeen competencies across five categories; the heptagon attributed to Hackl et al. (2026) adds institutional complexity while keeping the individual as the locus of change. A table compares how selected frameworks and critical accounts handle five features of AI-mediated research practice; the authors state that none of the seven sources compared takes researchers as its focus. These are the authors' readings, not measurements.

Drawing on critical pedagogy, decolonial thinking, academic literacies and Ubuntu-informed relationality, the paper argues that the knowing researcher should be understood as relationally constituted rather than as a self-contained cognitive agent. Structural consciousness asks who develops and governs the systems researchers use, whose data and labor sustain them, what pressures encourage adoption and how access is distributed. Ubuntu is not treated as a settled or homogeneous African AI ethics framework: its role is to shift the question from what a researcher should know and do to through what relations the capacity to know, judge and be answerable is formed. Critical consciousness follows Freire's conscientização — perceiving social, political and economic contradictions and acting against them — which the authors connect to research shaped by extraction, exclusion, authority and accountability.

## The framework in concrete terms
CAIEF is organized along three axes. Epistemic posture (X) runs from epistemic deference (X1) through critical interrogation (X2), epistemic agency (X3) and collective or relational agency (X4). Structural consciousness (Y) runs from individualized engagement (Y1) through partial structural awareness (Y2) to structural consciousness (Y3). Their combinations form the twelve positions the authors set out; each position can be held under any of three conditions of epistemic accompaniment (Z): unaccompanied or extractive offloading (Z1), asymmetric or institutional accompaniment (Z2), and reciprocally vulnerable, community-grounded accompaniment (Z3). The axes are ordered but explicitly not developmental stages, and cells in the accompaniment depth are combinations of position and relation rather than additional positions.

Epistemic accompaniment is defined as a state in which a claim passes through a relation capable of holding the researcher answerable because the other party is also exposed to being wrong. Reciprocal vulnerability is necessary but not sufficient: risk, credit and voice may be unevenly shared, as when a supervisor absorbs a supervisee's labor without credit and answerability runs one way. Peer review and audit are described as accountability without accompaniment, since reviewers assess a claim from outside the relation that produced it. The authors present the claim that an LLM cannot provide accompaniment as a normative position: accountability resides with human and sociotechnical actors, including researchers, teams, institutions and developers.

Offloading is treated as neutral only by competency approaches. In the paper's vocabulary, cognitive offloading is the delegation of mental effort through physical action or external resources; epistemic offloading is the narrower case in which delegated work contributes to forming, selecting, interpreting or justifying a knowledge claim. Formatting, transcription and routine language correction are not treated as equivalent to identifying a research gap, interpreting qualitative data, choosing authoritative scholarship or synthesizing theoretical disagreement.

To apply the framework, the authors list six epistemic relations: access, interpretation, authority, verification, agency and justice. They trace CAIEF through stages of research practice — framing the literature, methodology and design, data and evidence, analysis and interpretation, writing and synthesis, and publication and dissemination — asking what may be delegated, what becomes invisible, what verification remains and where accompaniment may be needed. Both tables are illustrative rather than exhaustive. The conclusion is that integrity cannot be secured by disclosure alone: researchers may delegate epistemic work, not responsibility for what becomes knowledge.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Treat AI literacy in teaching as a structural and epistemic practice rather than a set of prompt skills, and ask whose knowledge a tool makes visible or invisible.
- **Researchers.** Before delegating a task, name the epistemic work involved and check whether the claim still passes through a relation that can contest your judgment and hold you answerable.
- **Research teams.** Make authority, credit, voice and risk explicit when supervisors, co-authors, participants or community partners shape an interpretation; a relation is not accompaniment simply because another person is present.
- **Institutions.** Move beyond disclosure-only integrity policies by documenting what epistemic task an AI system performed and by supporting relations in which scholars can be held answerable for claims.

## Limitations
- The article is a conceptual analysis with no sample, instrument, measured outcome or evaluation of the framework; its propositions and readings of prior work are not tested here.
- As a conceptual framework, CAIEF cannot determine how much agency a given position allows; the authors explicitly leave that as an empirical question.
- It was articulated from within UK and South African higher education and with a focus on text-based generative AI; application to other disciplines, national contexts and forms of AI-mediated research remains to be examined.
- The framework is normative: it values structural consciousness and relational agency, and the authors offer it as one situated, revisable account open to adaptation, contestation or refusal rather than a universalizing model.
- The comparison of prior frameworks records the authors' characterizations rather than an empirical comparison, and the epistemic relations and research lifecycle tables are described as illustrative rather than exhaustive.
- The claim that generative AI cannot provide accompaniment is presented as a normative position rather than as a self-evident or empirically tested one.
