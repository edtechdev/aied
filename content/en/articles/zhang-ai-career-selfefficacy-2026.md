> **Synthesis:** Students reading about AI's effect on the labor market are not neutral observers of their own futures, and this study asks how that reading reaches their [[self-efficacy|confidence in making career decisions]]. Zhang surveyed 415 Chinese [[higher-ed|college students]] and modeled two pathways: positive perceived AI career impacts were associated with stronger decision-making confidence through career planning, while negative perceived impacts had no direct association with confidence at all but were associated with lower confidence through [[anxiety-and-stress|career anxiety]]. Both valences matter, but through different intermediates — a planning route for optimism and an affective route for pessimism. The model accounted for over half the variance in decision-making confidence. The design is cross-sectional and [[self-report-measures|self-reported]], so the paths describe a plausible ordering of associations rather than a demonstrated sequence.

## Key Findings

1. **Optimism and pessimism took different routes.** Believing AI would help one's career was linked to more confidence in career decisions; believing it would hurt was not linked to confidence directly at all.
2. **Planning carried the optimistic route.** Students who saw AI favorably planned their careers more, and planning in turn went with stronger decision-making confidence, so the positive perception worked through behavior rather than directly.
3. **Anxiety carried the pessimistic route.** Students who saw AI as a threat were more anxious about careers, and that anxiety went with weaker confidence — a route about as strong as the planning one, in the opposite direction.
4. **Together they explained over half of decision confidence.** That is a large share for a model with two mediators, and it supports treating the two valences as separate inputs rather than folding them into one attitude score.
5. **Collapsing the two would hide the mechanism.** A single "AI attitudes" score would average a planning effect and an anxiety effect that run in opposite directions, which is the paper's case for measuring them separately.
6. **A second sample held up the measurement.** An independent group of 246 respondents reproduced acceptable loadings and reliability, though the career planning scale remained the weakest of the measures.

## How the model was estimated

The main sample was 415 college students recruited through a commercial online survey panel (Wenjuanxing) with purposive sampling; a second, independent sample of 246 responses was used to check the measurement model. Perceived career impacts of AI were split into positive and negative perceptions, and the model placed career planning and career anxiety as parallel mediators between those perceptions and career decision-making self-efficacy.

Estimation used partial least squares structural equation modeling, with a cross-path robustness model and a reverse model to probe alternative orderings — the reverse specification found self-efficacy predicting career planning at β = 0.534, which the authors report as a reason to treat the direction of that relationship as open. The career planning measure was adapted and shortened: four of its original ten items were dropped because their loadings fell below 0.60, and the retained items emphasize awareness, exploration and goal planning while career-related action, knowledge acquisition and adjustment are less represented.

## What the two pathways imply for support

The practical distinction is that optimism and pessimism enter the system at different points. Positive perceptions work through planning behavior, which is something a course, an advising relationship or a career center can support directly. Negative perceptions work through anxiety, which responds to different interventions — accurate information about occupational change, and structured ways to act under uncertainty rather than reassurance alone.

The estimates behind that contrast are consistent in size. Positive perceptions predicted career planning at β = 0.488 and planning predicted self-efficacy at β = 0.361, giving an indirect path of 0.176, while the direct path from positive perceptions to self-efficacy was β = 0.186. Negative perceptions predicted career anxiety more strongly (β = 0.588), anxiety predicted lower self-efficacy (β = −0.314), and the indirect path was −0.184; the direct path from negative perceptions was β = 0.029 and not distinguishable from zero (p = 0.406). The two mediators plus the direct paths accounted for 54.6% of the variance in self-efficacy, 0.346 of the variance in anxiety, and a smaller share for planning. The validation sample showed loadings from 0.617 to 0.835 and composite reliabilities from 0.835 to 0.927.

Neither pathway was tested prospectively, and the reverse model's finding that self-efficacy predicts planning just as plausibly is a reminder that the causal story is not settled. What the study does establish is that the two valences should not be collapsed: a single "AI attitude" scale would hide the planning and anxiety routes that the data separate.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors and advisors:** Separate the two conversations. Students who are optimistic about AI need planning support that turns confidence into steps; students who are anxious need information and manageable action, not encouragement to feel better.
- **Administrators:** Treat career anxiety as a [[curriculum-design|curriculum]] issue, not only a counseling one. The anxiety route predicts lower decision-making confidence, and planning support is the route the positive pathway runs through.
- **Researchers:** Measure positive and negative perceptions separately. Collapsing them averages two mechanisms with opposite signs, and the paper's own model depends on the split.

## Limitations

- The sample came from a commercial online panel with purposive sampling rather than probability sampling, and over-represents science and engineering students.
- All constructs are self-reported and measured on one occasion, so the model establishes associations consistent with the proposed ordering, not temporal or causal sequence.
- The independent validation sample showed a career planning average variance extracted of 0.459, slightly below the conventional 0.50 threshold.
- The career planning measure lost four of its ten original items in validation, narrowing its content coverage relative to the original multidimensional scale.
- The reverse model fit the data plausibly, leaving the direction between self-efficacy and career planning open.
