> **Synthesis:** Almashour and colleagues (2026) tested whether a fluent [[generative-ai|generative-AI]] revision cycle leaves L2 writers with a confidence their unaided writing does not support. Across 108 paired drafts from four intact [[writing-education|academic writing]] classes at a Jordanian [[higher-ed|university]], [[rubrics|rubric]] scores rose by an average of 3.36 points on a 4–20 scale after AI-supported revision, a very large within-subject shift. Post-revision confidence correlated only moderately with writing produced without AI 7–14 days later — a relationship that explains roughly a fifth of the variance, and confidence exceeded the rescaled delayed score by 0.78 points on average. Learners whose confidence ran above that band explained their revisions more often as copying or surface acceptance, while rewriting was more frequent inside the band. The authors treat the "algorithmic fluency illusion" as a provisional [[trust-calibration|calibration]] hypothesis rather than a demonstrated effect, and the study's real contribution is architectural: it separates the quality of a mediated text, the writer's [[self-assessment|self-judgment]], and what the writer can still do alone.

## Key Findings

1. **AI-supported revision improved the product substantially.** Mean rubric scores rose from 10.58 to 13.94 on a 4–20 scale, a gain of 3.36 points, and every observed gain was positive.
2. **Gains were spread across all four rubric dimensions.** Cohesion, academic phrasing, syntactic control and rhetorical clarity each improved by roughly 0.83–0.85 points, so no single feature carried the effect.
3. **Confidence tracked later independent writing only moderately.** Post-revision confidence correlated .46 with unaided writing 7–14 days later, explaining about 21% of between-student variation.
4. **Confidence ran ahead of demonstrated performance.** Mean confidence exceeded the rescaled delayed score by 0.78 points; under a descriptive band, 50 of 105 students (47.6%) sat above it and 46 (43.8%) within it.
5. **Above-band students accounted for revisions differently.** Among 315 coded revision-explanation pairs, copying and surface acceptance were proportionally more frequent above the band, whereas rewriting was more frequent within it.
6. **The authors decline to claim an illusion.** Confidence responses showed limited internal consistency, the band labels are numerical positions rather than validated calibration states, and mapping rubric totals onto confidence endpoints does not establish equivalence.
## From revised draft to delayed unaided text

The design keeps three products apart that AI feedback research routinely merges. Students wrote an initial text, revised it with a restricted AI interface, rated their confidence, and then wrote a comparable argumentative text without AI 7–14 days later. Revision quality, self-judgment and unaided performance were measured as separate outcomes rather than treated as one learning gain. That separation is what makes the [[learning-gains|learning gain]] question answerable: a large score change on the revised draft says nothing on its own about what the writer acquired, because the revision can be correct and fluent while the underlying skill stays where it was.

## What the explanations add

The explanation codes are the study's most practically suggestive result, and also its weakest evidence. Students who rated their confidence above the band more often described their revisions as copying or accepting surface changes, while students inside the band more often described rewriting. Read alongside the [[metacognition|metacognitive]] literature, this suggests that the difference between helpful and hollow AI revision may be visible in how a student accounts for what they changed — an account a course can ask for cheaply. The authors are careful here: the profiles differ across groups, but without random assignment the differences may reflect baseline writing ability, task difficulty or response style.

## Why the mediation problem is the point

The paper's framing is that AI-mediated revision changes the object being assessed. A rubric applied to a revised draft scores a joint product of the writer and the system, while [[self-efficacy|self-efficacy]] judgments about that product are formed from the same fluent artifact. Earlier work on AI writing support often read improved drafts as improved writing, and this study is designed to break that inference by measuring the unaided text. Its conclusion for measurement is narrow and useful: mediated textual quality, self-judgment and subsequent independent writing are three different outcomes, and an evaluation that reports only the first has not measured learning. The same argument applies to any [[formative-assessment|formative]] cycle where a tool, a peer or a tutor improves the artifact before it is judged.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Ask students to explain, in writing, what each AI-suggested change altered and why they accepted it. The explanation code is the closest this study comes to a diagnostic of uptake, and it costs one sentence per revision.
- **Instructors.** Pair AI-supported revision with at least one short unaided text on a related prompt. The 7–14 day gap matters more than the exact interval, because a delayed task is what separates polished mediation from retained writing.
- **Assessment designers.** Grade the revised text and the unaided text separately, and report both. Treating a strong AI-revised draft as evidence of writing proficiency is exactly the inference this study cannot support.
- **Faculty developers.** Frame confidence as data, not as a feeling to correct. Students in this sample were not wildly overconfident; the moderate correlation means confidence is informative but not a substitute for evidence.

## Limitations

- Participants came from four intact classes at one university, recruited by purposive course-based sampling, with no random allocation and no alternative revision condition.
- Prior AI use was [[self-report-measures|self-reported]] and was not entered as a covariate, and the recorded ages, writing experience and proficiency describe a narrow instructional setting.
- The delayed task came 7–14 days after revision and used one short argumentative genre, which supports a claim about near-term independent writing, not enduring acquisition.
- The confidence measure showed limited internal consistency, and the ±0.75 band is a descriptive device whose counts change under other thresholds.
- The restricted interface and a specified model snapshot make the conditions explicit rather than representative; a different system version or prompt could change how students revise.
