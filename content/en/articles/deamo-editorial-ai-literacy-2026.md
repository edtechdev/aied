> **Synthesis:** [[generative-ai|Generative AI]] is reshaping the reading and writing practices that underpin academic literacy in [[higher-ed|higher education]], and this editorial proposes a three-part conceptual model for interpreting the change. The model names three interconnected mechanisms: [[cognitive-offloading|cognitive offloading]], the use of external resources to lower a task's mental demands; cognitive delegation, the point at which externalization reaches operations intrinsic to learning; and an epistemic shift, a change in the conditions under which knowledge is judged legitimate rather than a change in knowledge itself. Together these frame the transformation as a reconfiguration of students' participation in scholarly epistemic practices, not merely a technological upgrade, and they call for redefining academic literacy to include critical judgment about what may be delegated without losing meaning-making or intellectual autonomy. The article is a position paper: it reports no empirical data, no sample, and no outcomes.

## Key Findings

1. **A three-mechanism model.** The editorial proposes three interconnected mechanisms — cognitive offloading, cognitive delegation, and epistemic shift — to interpret how generative AI changes academic literacy.
2. **Offloading versus delegation.** Cognitive offloading uses external resources to reduce mental demands without replacing active engagement; delegation begins when externalization reaches operations intrinsic to learning.
3. **Delegation changes epistemic value.** The editorial argues that cognitive delegation alters not just how academic work is done but the relationship between what students submit and the intellectual activity that gives it value, especially in writing.
4. **The shift is about conditions, not content.** An epistemic shift concerns how knowledge gains legitimacy in academic communities rather than knowledge itself, and becomes visible when systems supply summaries and apparently authoritative answers that displace readers' interpretative work.
5. **Academic literacy must be redefined.** The editorial contends it can no longer mean reading and writing effectively alone, but must include critical judgment about when and how much generative AI should contribute to intellectual work.
6. **Instruction must be explicit and progressive.** The editorial argues that integrating generative AI in a critical, pedagogically informed way is neither spontaneous nor incidental and requires sustained, structured teacher education.

## The proposal in concrete terms

The editorial builds its model in three steps. First, cognitive offloading is described as an adaptive strategy in which external resources reduce a task's demands without replacing the learner's active intellectual engagement. Generative systems mark a qualitative change because they produce outputs that perform functions once requiring learners to interpret information, evaluate evidence, construct arguments, and revise their own work. The editorial illustrates the boundary with examples drawn from the Research Topic: it describes ChatGPT used as a scaffold that supports reading comprehension and learner autonomy while keeping the reader active in interpretation, and it notes reports of Indonesian and Taiwanese academics who keep using generative AI even when responses are inaccurate, a dependence that runs past adaptive offloading. The central question it raises is not whether external resources can support learning, but which intellectual operations must remain the learner's responsibility.

Second, cognitive delegation is presented as a qualitative transition beyond offloading because it changes the relationship between the products students submit and the intellectual activities that give those products their epistemic value. The editorial points to AI-assisted feedback tools that take on subprocesses such as problem identification and option selection, long a part of a writer's revision practice; it warns that this can lower short-term effort yet weaken writers' agency and willingness to engage critically with their own texts. It contrasts this with Didactext-guided prompting that lets GPT-4 act as a writing assistant offering differentiated support across planning, drafting, and revision while preserving the writer's role in knowledge construction.

Third, an epistemic shift is defined as a change in the conditions that determine how knowledge acquires legitimacy within academic communities, not a change in knowledge itself. Understanding a scientific text is framed as an inferential activity: readers interpret concepts, evaluate evidence, identify assumptions, and connect new ideas to prior knowledge. Summaries, explanations, and apparently authoritative responses can replace part of that interpretative work. The editorial stresses that the difficulty is not only inaccurate outputs, unsupported claims, or fabricated references, but the gradual displacement of the critical activity through which readers evaluate, question, and appropriate knowledge as their own.

## What the model does and does not claim

The editorial offers the three mechanisms as an interpretative lens rather than a measured result. Drawing on a range of theoretical perspectives, methodological approaches, and educational settings, it argues that work in this area has gradually moved from evaluating technological effectiveness toward broader educational consequences for academic literacy, learning processes, and students' participation in disciplinary practices. It cites evidence suggesting that, although generative AI can enhance textual fluency and linguistic accuracy, these benefits may come at the expense of authorial voice, epistemic agency, and students' perception of cognitive effort.

The argument is also extended to teacher preparation and beyond. The editorial describes pre-service teachers who adopted a cautious stance toward AI-assisted writing tools for self-assessment, with direct experience fostering more informed judgments, and platforms that worked best when adapted to student needs within pedagogical practices supported by targeted teacher education. It notes that pre-service language and literature teachers developed the required understanding across successive stages of preparation, and contends such integration is neither spontaneous nor incidental. Looking further ahead, it raises unresolved questions about educational equity, the distribution of cognitive labor, intellectual autonomy, and the long-term consequences of embedding AI agents in wearable and ambient technologies. Its closing position is that the challenge is to ensure innovation strengthens rather than displaces the cognitive and epistemic foundations on which academic literacy depends.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Frame reading and writing tasks so that interpretation, evidence evaluation, and argument construction stay in the learner's hands, using generative AI as a scaffold rather than a substitute for those operations.
- **Instructors.** When adopting AI-assisted feedback on writing, check that tools are not taking over problem identification and option selection, since the editorial warns this can weaken writers' agency and critical engagement with their own texts.
- **Instructors.** Build explicit, progressive instruction that develops students' capacity to judge whether, how, and to what extent generative AI should contribute, because the editorial argues such integration is neither spontaneous nor incidental.
- **Researchers.** Use the three-mechanism lens to distinguish adaptive offloading from delegation and from shifts in the conditions of legitimacy, rather than treating the transformation as a purely technological change.

## Limitations

- This is an editorial and position paper: it proposes a conceptual model but reports no empirical data, no sample, no instrument, and no measured outcomes.
- The three mechanisms are advanced as an interpretative framework rather than a tested model, so their validity, boundaries, and relative weight are not established here.
- The examples drawn from the Research Topic illustrate the proposal but are not synthesized or analyzed as evidence within the editorial; they serve as illustrations, not as this paper's own findings.
- No comparison conditions are reported, so the editorial cannot show whether one mechanism matters more than another or how the three interact in practice.
- The forward-looking claims about wearable and ambient technologies and postsecondary learning are posed as unresolved questions, not demonstrated effects.
