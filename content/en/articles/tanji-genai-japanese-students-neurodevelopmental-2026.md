> **Synthesis:** Tanji, Uchida, Onodera and Sato ask whether [[generative-ai|generative AI]] serves Japanese university students who report [[neurodiversity|neurodevelopmental disorders]] differently from their peers, or whether it behaves as a mainstream tool used by everyone. In a cross-sectional online survey of 477 university and graduate students recruited from a research panel, participants were divided by self-report into a DD group of 118 (a diagnosis or physician-identified traits) and a non-DD group of 359, then compared on frequency of use, self-rated skills and literacy, perceived benefits, support needs, and learning difficulties. The DD group scored higher on all five difficulty subscales, yet no statistically significant group difference appeared on any generative AI measure. Within the DD group, greater decoding difficulties went with lower self-rated skills, although that association faded in sensitivity analyses, and students who had received reasonable accommodations reported higher support needs. Both classifications rest on self-report rather than clinical verification, the design is cross-sectional, and the panel sample was mostly women and undergraduates.

## Key Findings

- **Mainstream use across both groups.** No statistically significant difference appeared between the DD and non-DD groups on days of generative AI use per week, self-rated skills and literacy, perceived benefits, or support needs, and the reported effect sizes were all small.
- **Difficulty did not track use.** The DD group scored higher on all five learning-difficulty subscales, but those difficulties were not significantly associated with how often the students used generative AI.
- **One fragile signal in decoding.** Within the DD group, students with greater decoding difficulties rated their own generative AI skills and literacy lower; the association weakened and lost significance when non-users were excluded and when only diagnosed students were analyzed.
- **Accommodation experience marked support.** Within the DD group, students who had received reasonable accommodations reported greater listening, decoding, and writing difficulties and higher support needs, and the support difference survived adjustment for subgroup and difficulty.
- **Similar shape of relationships.** Skills and literacy, perceived benefits, and support needs correlated positively in both groups, and none of the three correlation coefficients differed significantly between groups.
- **Voice and images edged up.** The DD group used voice and image interactions more often than the non-DD group, while text chat remained the dominant mode in both.

## Who was surveyed and how they were grouped

The study was a cross-sectional online questionnaire survey of Japanese undergraduate and graduate students recruited from a panel maintained by a web research company. Of the 477 respondents, aged 18 to 29 years, 146 (30.6%) were men and 331 (69.4%) were women; 424 (88.9%) were undergraduates and 53 (11.1%) were graduate students. Eighty-seven participants (18.2%) reported having received reasonable accommodations at university. Analysis sorted participants by self-report into a DD group of 118 (24.7%) — 67 (14.0%) reporting a diagnosis and 51 (10.7%) reporting physician-identified traits without a diagnosis — and a non-DD group of 359 (75.3%). The authors stress that both classifications were self-reported and never clinically verified, and that the non-DD group may contain students with undiagnosed conditions. They frame the comparison through the second and third layers of the [[digital-divide|digital divide]]: in a country where reported generative AI experience exceeds 90%, the question is no longer access but skills and outcomes.

Learning difficulties were measured with the 28-item Learning Difficulty Scale for Postsecondary students, whose five subscales cover reading comprehension, listening and inattention, decoding, writing and dictation, and memory and learning; Cronbach's alpha ranged from 0.86 to 0.94. Three generative AI measures of ten items each covered self-rated skills and literacy, perceived benefits, and support needs, with alphas of 0.92 or 0.93. An exploratory factor analysis found one dominant factor per scale, each explaining about 60% of the variance, so the authors used each scale's mean. ChatGPT was the most common tool (363 respondents, 76.1%), followed by Gemini (174, 36.5%) and Copilot (65, 13.6%); 60 respondents (12.6%) used none of these. Text input dominated (379 respondents, 79.5%), with voice (17, 3.6%) and images (10, 2.1%) far behind; 71 (14.9%) reported no interaction at all.

## Difficulties separated the groups; the AI indicators did not

All five LDSP28 subscales were significantly higher in the DD group, with the largest effect for listening difficulties and inattention, followed by memory and learning, reading comprehension, and writing and dictation; decoding had the smallest effect. The generative AI results pointed the other way. No statistically significant difference appeared between groups on days of use per week (means 2.97 versus 2.69), skills and literacy (2.80 versus 2.82), perceived benefits (2.76 versus 2.68), or support needs (2.53 versus 2.50), and the absolute effect sizes were 0.08 or smaller. A clear difference did appear in interaction mode: text chat was most common in both groups (72.0% in the DD group versus 81.9% in the non-DD group), but the DD group used voice (6.8% versus 2.5%) and images (5.9% versus 0.8%) more often, while non-interaction was similar (15.3% versus 14.8%).

Within both groups the three indicators correlated positively: skills and literacy with perceived benefits (0.63 in the DD group and 0.52 in the non-DD group), perceived benefits with support needs (0.45 and 0.49), and skills and literacy with support needs (0.30 and 0.25). None of the between-group differences in these coefficients reached significance. Within the DD group the only association to survive adjustment was a negative one between decoding difficulties and self-rated skills and literacy. It stayed in the same direction when the 51 non-users were excluded but lost significance after adjustment, and it was weaker again when only the 67 diagnosed students were analyzed. The authors treat the finding as preliminary and connect it to the wider effort to build generative [[ai-literacy]] scales for university students.

## Accommodation experience and the support that remains

Dividing the DD group by accommodation experience showed that the 52 students who had received accommodations reported higher support needs than the 66 who had not, together with greater listening, decoding, and writing difficulties. Reading comprehension and memory and learning did not differ significantly. A regression adjusting for subgroup and all five difficulty subscales left support needs higher among accommodated students. When only diagnosed students were examined, the support-needs difference no longer reached significance after adjustment. A sensitivity power analysis indicated that the accommodation comparison could detect only effects of about 0.30 or larger, and the main group comparison only about 0.17 or larger, so smaller differences may have been missed.

The authors conclude that generative AI appears to function as a mainstream technology rather than as a compensation tool reserved for students with neurodevelopmental disorders, and they draw three implications that they flag as untested. Support should follow the profile of learning difficulties rather than diagnostic status alone. Because typing prompts, reading output, and judging validity impose a reading burden, [[accessibility]] support such as voice input, text-to-speech, and control over output volume matters, and fits the multiple means of representation called for by [[universal-design-for-learning|universal design for learning]]. Finally, students may be unable to articulate what they need, so a chance to try the tools may surface needs that a direct request for support misses; disability services can help students combine generative AI with [[assistive-technology|accessibility features]]. The highest-rated support needs concerned knowing how work would be evaluated and knowing the rules for safe use.

## What this means for practice

- **Faculty developers.** Offer training that pairs generative AI with accessibility features such as voice input and text-to-speech, since reading burden is the mechanism the authors propose for the decoding finding.
- **Instructors.** State plainly how work will be evaluated when generative AI is used and what the safety rules are; those two items drew the highest support-needs ratings.
- **Administrators.** Do not decide provision on diagnostic status alone; plan support around the profile of learning difficulties, with reading-related accessibility first.
- **Students.** Try the tools before being asked what support you need, because a low support-needs score may reflect difficulty naming the problem rather than the absence of one.
- **Researchers.** Replace self-ratings with performance-based measures and add longitudinal designs; a cross-sectional survey cannot show direction.

## Limitations

- Group assignment and every generative AI indicator are [[self-report-measures|self-reported]]; diagnoses were not clinically verified, and skills and literacy are self-ratings rather than assessed ability.
- The cross-sectional design cannot establish causality or temporal order for the associations among skills, benefits, support needs, and accommodation experience.
- The DD group was dominated by ADHD and ASD, while developmental dyslexia and specific learning disorder made up about 8% of that group, so the reading-related finding was not tested on a reading-focused sample.
- Participants came from an online panel rather than random sampling and were mostly women (69.4%) and undergraduates (88.9%), which limits generalization to Japanese university students.
- The three generative AI scales were developed for this study. Reliability was high (alpha 0.92 to 0.93), but validity was not fully examined and measurement invariance across the DD and non-DD groups was not tested.
- Non-significant differences do not establish equivalence: the study could detect only effects of about 0.30 or larger within the DD group and about 0.17 or larger across groups.
