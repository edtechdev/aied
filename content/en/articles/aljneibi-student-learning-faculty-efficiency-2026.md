> **Synthesis:** Aljneibi and Frick surveyed 128 students and faculty members at four universities in the United Arab Emirates to ask how AI use relates to perceived student learning outcomes and faculty efficiency. Reported AI use showed moderate positive relationships with both outcomes, and it remained a significant predictor of each in regression models. Institutional readiness was not an independent predictor of either result. The outcomes were [[self-report-measures|self-reported]] perceptions gathered at one point in time, so the study describes associations and does not establish that AI improved learning or productivity.

## Key Findings
1. **AI use tracked perceived learning outcomes.** Respondents who reported more AI use also reported more positive student learning outcomes.
2. **AI use tracked perceived faculty efficiency.** The same positive relationship held for the other outcome, faculty efficiency.
3. **Institutional readiness did not predict either outcome.** Once AI use was accounted for, readiness was not an independent predictor of perceived learning outcomes or faculty efficiency.
4. **The two outcomes moved together.** Perceived learning outcomes and perceived faculty efficiency were more closely related to each other than either was to institutional readiness.
5. **The evidence is perceptual and cross-sectional.** All four measures came from a single online survey, so the results cannot show that AI use caused any difference in perceived outcomes.

## What the survey measured

The study was framed as applied, problem-focused social science in UAE [[higher-ed|higher education]], set against the UAE National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence 2031 and an earlier national AI framework from 2017. The authors combined cognitive [[learning-theories|learning theory]], the [[technology-acceptance-model|Technology Acceptance Model]] and diffusion of innovation theory as a conceptual backdrop, with AI Utilization treated as the independent variable and Student Learning Outcomes and Faculty Efficiency as outcomes. Institutional Readiness was entered as a second predictor in the regression models.

A [[quantitative-research|quantitative]], cross-sectional survey was administered through SurveyMonkey to students and staff at the University of Sharjah, Higher College of Technology, University of the United Arab Emirates and City University of Ajman. Sampling was stratified convenience sampling: two groups of 64 participants made up the total of 128. Participation was voluntary and encouraged through institutional email and reminders. The questionnaire used a five-point Likert scale, drew on earlier literature and the Technology Acceptance Model, and was piloted with 10 volunteer students and selected faculty members. Cronbach's alpha was 0.85, which the authors treat as high internal consistency. Responses were exported to IBM SPSS Statistics for cleaning, coding and analysis.

The demographic profile was mixed. Half the respondents were students and half were faculty. Age bands were 28.1% at 18–24 years, 18% at 25–34, 19.5% at 35–44, 19.5% at 45–54 and 10.2% above 55. Years of involvement in higher education were concentrated at 4–6 years (45.3%) and 1–3 years (30.5%). On frequency of AI tool use, 35.2% used tools a few times a month, 25% a few times a week, 20.3% rarely and 13.3% daily. On institutional support for AI, 39.8% reported support to a large extent, 21.9% to some extent, 17.2% to a small extent, 10.9% not at all and 10.2% to a very large extent.

All four core variables sat in the middle-to-upper part of the five-point scale. AI Utilization had a mean of 3.21 (SD = 1.17), Student Learning Outcomes a mean of 3.41 (SD = 1.12), Faculty Efficiency a mean of 3.34 (SD = 1.10), and Institutional Readiness a mean of 3.41 (SD = 1.11). The standard deviations suggested moderate variability in how respondents answered.

## Associations, not effects

The correlation analysis found AI Utilization significantly related to Student Learning Outcomes (r = .438, p < .001) and to Faculty Efficiency (r = .394, p < .001). The strongest relationship in the matrix was between Student Learning Outcomes and Faculty Efficiency (r = .520, p < .001). Institutional Readiness was not significantly correlated with the other three variables; the reported relationships with AI Utilization, learning outcomes and faculty efficiency were r = .163 (p = .066), r = .126 (p = .157) and r = .100 (p = .263).

Two multiple regressions tested the predictors together. For Student Learning Outcomes the model was significant, F(2,125) = 14.81, p < .001. AI Utilization was a strong positive predictor (β = .428, p < .001; unstandardized B = .408), while Institutional Readiness was not (β = .056, p = .492). The authors describe the AI result as a one-unit increase in utilization being associated with an increase of .408 units in perceived learning outcomes, and note that the design permits association but not causality.

For Faculty Efficiency the model was also significant, F(2,125) = 11.56, p < .001. AI Utilization again emerged as a predictor (β = .389, p < .001; unstandardized B = .365), while Institutional Readiness did not (β = .036, p = .663). The paper reports this as a significant statistical association between AI use and perceived faculty efficiency, with no causal interpretation.

Several additional checks sit alongside those headline models. One-sample t-tests against zero were significant for AI Utilization (t(127) = 30.92, p < .001, Cohen's d = 2.73), Student Learning Outcomes (t(127) = 34.46, p < .001, d = 3.05) and Faculty Efficiency (t(127) = 34.21, p < .001, d = 3.02); the authors describe these as very large effects, though the test value was zero rather than the neutral point of the scale. Analysis of variance separated groups by level of the independent variable: Student Learning Outcomes showed F(4,123) = 8.37, p < .001, and Faculty Efficiency F(4,123) = 5.96, p < .001, with Levene's test significant for learning outcomes (p = .023).

Normality was not supported. Shapiro–Wilk tests indicated significant deviation for AI Utilization (W = .885, p < .001), Student Learning Outcomes (W = .879, p < .001), Faculty Efficiency (W = .886, p < .001) and Institutional Readiness (W = .906, p < .001). The authors note that non-parametric tests may be better suited to these data. In a factor analysis, communalities ranged from .148 to .639 and extracted values from .009 to .575, with "Grading and assessment" highest at .575 and "training or support" lowest at .009; several items were flagged for reconsideration.

The paper rejects its null hypothesis and accepts the alternative that AI improves student learning experiences and faculty efficiency, while repeatedly stating that the cross-sectional correlational design yields associations rather than causal effects. The conclusions recommend that administrators support practical AI implementation, invest in user-friendly platforms and technical support, and build [[educational-development|faculty development]] around confidence and digital competence, alongside policy work on [[ethics]], privacy and algorithmic transparency.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Use AI for routine preparation and feedback tasks where the survey suggests efficiency is perceived to improve, while keeping judgment about learning quality with the instructor.
- **Institutions.** Invest in usable platforms and adequate technical support before expecting efficiency gains; the paper explicitly recommends user-friendly tools and faculty development programs.
- **Administrators.** Measure institutional readiness directly rather than assuming it follows from adoption; it was a separate factor here and did not predict either outcome.
- **Policymakers.** Pair adoption with frameworks for ethics, [[privacy|data privacy]] and algorithmic transparency, as the paper recommends for higher education planning.
- **Researchers.** Use [[longitudinal-research|longitudinal]] or mixed-method designs to test whether perceptions of learning and efficiency change as AI becomes routine, since this survey captured a single moment.

## Limitations

- The sample was small and drawn from four institutions through stratified convenience sampling and voluntary email response, so selection bias is possible and generalization to the wider higher education sector is limited.
- The results are based on self-reported perceptions collected on one occasion; they describe perceived learning outcomes and faculty efficiency, not actual educational performance.
- The cross-sectional design does not support cause-and-effect conclusions, and the authors state explicitly that no causal inference about the effects of AI can be made.
- All four variables deviated significantly from normality on formal testing, and the homogeneity-of-variance test was significant for one outcome, so some inferential tests rest on assumptions the data do not meet.
- Reporting is internally inconsistent: the demographic table and the results narrative give different gender percentages and different figures for non-use of AI, and the regression prose mixes standardized and unstandardized coefficients.
