> **Synthesis:** Tañajura and Paglinawan asked how multigrade teachers in rural schools in the Philippines perceive and use artificial intelligence. In a qualitative case study, ten (10) AI-exposed teachers from Kadingilan II District, Division of Bukidnon, took part in semi-structured interviews, focus group discussions and classroom observations during School Year 2026–2027. The teachers described AI as a supportive assistant for lesson preparation, differentiated materials and assessment, while insisting that teacher judgment, creativity and personal contact with learners remain essential. They reported limited internet connectivity, inaccurate or unsuitable generated content, cultural mismatch and the risk of over-reliance, and they named practical training, devices, offline resources, ethical guidance and peer collaboration as needed support. The reported benefits are teachers' experiences and perceptions; the study did not measure teaching effectiveness or learner achievement.

## Key Findings
1. **Teachers cast AI as an assistant, not a substitute.** Participants described a practical helper for managing several grade levels at once and said it should complement, not replace, teacher judgment.
2. **Preparation was the main reported use.** Teachers said they used AI for lesson plans, worksheets, quizzes, reading passages and activities pitched at different grade levels.
3. **Perceived benefits centered on time and ideas.** Participants reported faster preparation, fresh activity ideas and simpler routine tasks, but these accounts were not timed or otherwise measured.
4. **Access and content quality were recurring barriers.** Teachers named limited internet connectivity, inaccurate generated information, difficult vocabulary, activities that missed learning objectives and culturally unsuitable examples.
5. **Experiences were not uniform.** Most participants described difficulties, while one teacher reported none; the authors say the reason cannot be determined from that response.
6. **Support needs were concrete and local.** Teachers asked for hands-on training, reliable connectivity, adequate devices, offline AI resources, technical assistance, peer collaboration and clear rules on privacy, ethics and academic integrity.

## How the study was conducted

The study used a qualitative case study design because the aim was to understand teachers' experiences and instructional practices rather than measure effectiveness or establish causal relationships. It took place in Kadingilan II District, Division of Bukidnon, Municipality of Kadingilan, during School Year 2026–2027, a setting where multigrade teaching is common in rural schools. Teachers were invited if they had at least one year of multigrade teaching experience and exposure to AI tools. A total of 13 teachers were approached; ten (10) agreed to participate and completed the consent form, while three (3) declined. Participants were purposively selected, and teachers without AI exposure or from other districts were excluded.

Researchers used three prepared instruments: a semi-structured interview guide, a focus group discussion guide and a classroom observation checklist. Transcripts were returned to participants for confirmation, and recordings, transcripts and observation notes were stored securely. The paper states that the inquiry did not measure learners' academic performance, compare teaching effectiveness between AI-assisted and non-AI-assisted instruction, or establish causal effects of AI integration. It also states that the findings were limited to the ten participants and were not intended to generalize to all multigrade teachers.

## What teachers described in their classrooms

The results are organized around five themes. In the first, teachers portrayed AI as a supportive tool: T4 called it a "practical helper, not a replacement," and T7 said it "should complement, not replace teacher's judgment." In the second, teachers described using AI to generate differentiated lesson plans and materials; T1 reported "differentiated lesson plans, worksheets, quizzes," and T3 described suggesting "a simpler activity for Grade 3, more challenging for Grade 4." Classroom observations noted differentiated materials and grade-appropriate activities, but the authors state that the observations did not establish that AI produced those materials or that they improved learner achievement.

The third theme covers perceived efficiency and creativity. T1 named "saving time… sparks creativity… personalize learning materials," while T6 said AI "simplifies process… less hassle, easy to do tasks." The authors caution that the time savings were self-reported and that creativity in planning does not establish better learner participation, understanding or performance. The fourth theme gathers challenges: T1 identified "lack of internet," T2 said "AI-generated materials may be inaccurate," and participants described cultural mismatch and over-reliance. T6 reported no difficulties, which the authors say cannot be explained from the available response. The fifth theme records support needs: practical training on adapting materials across grade levels, reliable internet, digital devices, offline AI resources, and guidance on checking generated information, data privacy and academic integrity. Teachers also suggested technical assistance, peer collaboration, community awareness and appropriate workload policies.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Check generated material for accuracy, curriculum fit and cultural relevance before using it, and keep the final instructional decision with the teacher.
- **Instructors.** Share reviewed, curriculum-aligned materials with colleagues and adapt them across grade levels rather than relying on unedited output.
- **School administrators.** Use Learning Action Cell (LAC) sessions and hands-on workshops for AI-assisted lesson planning and assessment, and promote downloadable resources where connectivity is limited.
- **Teacher educators.** Build training around prompting, differentiated materials and verification, because teachers asked for practice tied to their actual classroom responsibilities.
- **Policymakers.** Strengthen guidance on data privacy, academic integrity and the accuracy of AI-generated content, and consider devices and offline tools for rural schools.

## Limitations

- **Single district, ten participants.** All participants were purposively selected AI-exposed teachers from one district in the Division of Bukidnon; three of the 13 teachers approached declined. The authors state that the findings are context-specific and not intended to generalize.
- **Reported experience, not measured effect.** Time savings, creativity and perceived efficiency come from interviews and focus groups. Observations documented materials and activities but could not establish AI authorship, implementation quality or improved learning.
- **No learning outcomes or comparison.** The design did not measure learner achievement, compare AI-assisted with non-AI-assisted teaching, or support causal claims about AI integration.
- **Unexplained variation.** One teacher reported no challenges; the paper lists possible reasons but states that none can be confirmed without further clarification.
- **One school year, one setting.** Data were gathered during School Year 2026–2027 in rural multigrade classrooms, so transfer to other locations, grade configurations or later conditions has not been tested.
