> **Synthesis:** Reardon's HAIML is a conceptual framework: a single-author white paper (Version 1.0, April 2026; EdArXiv v2, August 2026) proposing how [[higher-ed|higher education]] should structure [[generative-ai|generative AI]] use so learners keep the interpretive and metacognitive work. Its three interconnected layers, Experiential AI Use, Metacognitive Reflection, and Ethical Decision-Making, move students from direct experience to [[reflection]] to judgment, with [[agency]] as the organizing principle and reflection as the bridge between using AI and learning from it. A companion set of Four AI Use Levels, from Level 1 (No AI Use) to Level 4 (AI-Integrated Creation and Evaluation), helps instructors align AI permission with an assignment's objectives rather than rank assignments. The distinction worth keeping: [[ai-literacy]] frameworks ask what learners know about AI and can do with it, while HAIML asks how AI changes their thinking, confidence, and decisions. The paper reports no sample, no outcome measures, and no test of the model, so its claims are design guidance.

## Key Findings

1. **Framework, not evaluation.** Version 1.0 (April 2026; revised August 2026) reports no sample, no outcome data, and no test of the model.
2. **Three layers.** HAIML sequences Experiential AI Use, Metacognitive Reflection, and Ethical Decision-Making from experience to reflection to judgment.
3. **Four AI Use Levels.** Level 1 (No AI Use) through Level 4 (AI-Integrated Creation and Evaluation) align AI permission with learning objectives, not a ranking of assignments.
4. **Reflection as the bridge.** The second layer carries the framework's distinctive claim: students examine how AI shaped their thinking, confidence, and revisions at every level, including Level 1.
5. **Agency as the organizing principle.** Following Bandura (2001), AI can generate recommendations but cannot hold responsibility, so judgment stays with the learner.
6. **Two modes of thinking.** Kahneman's (2011) System 1 and System 2 explain why fluent AI output invites fast acceptance and why HAIML asks students to slow down and verify.

## The problem HAIML targets

HAIML's concern is that [[generative-ai]] already performs cognitive work that belonged to the learner, from brainstorming to drafting arguments and evaluating alternatives. That creates [[cognitive-offloading]], relying on external tools to reduce mental effort. Cognitive offloading is not new or bad, but generative AI can absorb so much thinking that students disengage from processes education is meant to develop. A second hazard is automation bias, the tendency to place excessive trust in automated systems and accept their recommendations without sufficient evaluation. The paper adds the illusion of understanding, where a clear explanation feels like comprehension, and questions of authorship: if AI drafts a paragraph, what remains the student's contribution? These concerns frame the framework's target, [[trust-calibration]] in [[student-ai-interaction]].

## Three layers, one progression

HAIML responds with three interconnected layers. Experiential AI Use gives students structured, authentic encounters with AI, not unrestricted use: brainstorming, feedback on writing, alternative perspectives, decision simulations. Metacognitive Reflection, the paper's center and main distinction from other approaches, asks students to examine how AI influenced their ideas, assumptions, confidence, revisions, and understanding. Reflection is part of learning rather than an activity after it, with prompts about what was verified and how the work might have differed without AI. Ethical Decision-Making asks students to weigh responsibility, transparency, authorship, accountability, fairness, and judgment, on the premise that AI generates content but cannot assume responsibility for decisions. [[agency]] anchors all three layers. The progression runs from experience to [[reflection]] to judgment, and the paper insists that [[metacognition]] turns AI use into learning rather than task completion.

## Four AI Use Levels

The framework is paired with Four AI Use Levels for aligning AI with instructional goals. Level 1 requires independent work for assessing individual knowledge and foundational skills; students may still reflect on why independence mattered. Level 2 permits AI for brainstorming, clarification, examples, and planning while the final work remains primarily student-generated. Level 3 makes AI a collaborative partner for ideas, feedback, revision, and problem solving, with students evaluating and deciding. Level 4 allows extensive AI use across creation, analysis, evaluation, and revision, often asking students to compare human and AI contributions and name limitations. The paper is explicit that the levels do not rank assignments from better to worse and that more AI is not always preferable. Reflection remains central at all four, which distinguishes HAIML from policies built around disclosure or compliance.

## Theoretical grounding

HAIML draws on established psychology, not new evidence. Bandura's work on self-efficacy and agency supplies the premise that learners must keep seeing themselves as capable participants; the paper quotes Bandura (2001): "to be an agent is to intentionally make things happen by one's actions" (p. 2). Zimmerman's account of [[self-regulated-learning]] contributes planning, monitoring, and evaluation, extended to decisions about when and how much to rely on AI. Kolb's experiential learning cycle grounds the first layer's insistence that experience alone does not teach. Schraw and Dennison on [[metacognition]], Bjork and colleagues on illusions of learning, Kahneman's System 1 and System 2, Klein's naturalistic decision-making, and research on automation bias (Parasuraman & Riley, 1997; Cummings, 2004) complete the case for deliberate evaluation. The stated gap: [[ai-literacy]] and responsible-use frameworks address what learners know and do with AI, but seldom how AI changes thinking itself.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Write the AI use level (Level 1 to Level 4) into each assignment's instructions and attach a short reflection prompt asking how AI changed the student's thinking, not only whether it was used.
- **Instructors.** At Level 1, still ask for reflection on why independent work mattered; reflection belongs to every level, not only assignments that permit AI.
- **Instructional designers.** Keep analysis, evaluation, decision-making, and reflection with the student at Levels 2 to 4, using AI for brainstorming and feedback without letting it produce the final artifact.
- **Faculty developers.** Frame AI guidance around [[self-assessment]] and [[trust-calibration]] rather than detection: teach students to verify outputs, name assumptions they accepted, and judge when human reasoning should take precedence.

## Limitations

- It is a conceptual framework with no sample, intervention, or outcome data; it offers design rationale, not evidence that HAIML improves learning, agency, or ethical judgment.
- The layers and levels are not operationalized: the paper offers reflection prompts but no validated instrument or fidelity measure, so implementations will vary in what counts as reflection.
- It was developed in [[higher-ed|higher education]] and claims adaptability to workforce, leadership, and healthcare training without reporting trials.
- It is a single-author preprint (Version 1.0, April 2026; revised August 2026) that builds its case from prior theory rather than a systematic review, and the author notes that HAIML 2.0 is in preparation.
