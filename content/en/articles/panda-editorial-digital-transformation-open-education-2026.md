> **Synthesis:** Panda (2026), writing as chief editor, uses the editorial for volume 13, issue 3 of the Journal of Learning for Development to argue a thesis about digital transformation. In open and flexible learning, he holds, it is pedagogic transformation rather than technology adoption, and a technology takes hold only when it fits the pedagogy and the local context. This is an editorial, not a study. No data were collected and no method is applied beyond a narrative chronology and a summary of the issue's contents. The issue carries eight research articles, six case studies and two book reviews, each summarized briefly, so those 14 papers form the backdrop rather than evidence the editorial gathers. It advances no statistic of its own and makes no causal claim it could test, so it can be assessed for coherence and for how faithfully it renders the papers it summarizes.

## Key Findings
1. It treats technology as cumulative rather than substitutive: teachers appear as the earliest teaching technology in 1500 BC, print in 1450, the postal service in 1850, radio in 1920 and television in 1950. The digital sequence then runs from computer-based instruction in 1970 to generative AI in the 2020s, and each earlier technology still serves open and distance learning.
2. It names the pedagogic shifts that define the field's recent history: from delivering content to designing learning, from individual to collaborative and networked learning, from fixed classrooms to flexible and blended arrangements, from summative and formative assessment to assessment as learning, and from behaviorist to constructivist and connectivist models.
3. It argues that open and distance learning has moved from a course material and tutoring system to a networked, interactive and intelligent one, with both operating in parallel for some time to come.
4. Its central condition is that technology finds credence in education only when it aligns with appropriate pedagogy applied in local contexts, which makes pedagogy the unit of change rather than the tool.
5. It credits AI with making learner experience more personalized, immersive, interactive, micro-learning oriented and skill-based, while citing reviews that record ethical challenges and call for re-examination of pedagogical models.
6. It reports the issue's findings as directions rather than effects: AI literacy positively related to teachers' teaching efficiency and to students' digital confidence and ethical decision-making, digital competence positively related to autonomous learning, and teacher workload carrying more predictive weight than ICT competency or laboratory resources for perceived performance in open distance learning.
7. It concludes that institutional adoption of generative AI has to weigh ecosystem, efficacy, ethics and pedagogic soundness, and that the teacher's role now includes that of learning manager.

## A technological history in which nothing is replaced

The chronology is the editorial's evidentiary backbone, and it is built to make one point: waves of technology accumulate in education instead of displacing what came before. Print, post, telephone, radio and television each entered teaching, and the digital sequence that follows, computer-based instruction, satellite delivery, personal computers, conferencing, the web, learning management systems, wikis, [[open-education|open educational resources]], virtual reality, two generations of MOOCs, digital badges, blockchain and generative AI, is presented as a continuation of the same pattern.

The pandemic supplies the recent test case. Emergency remote teaching is distinguished from online learning as a matter of design, and the editorial draws on two studies to describe what persisted afterwards. In the Canadian case it cites, teachers who had been pushed onto varied tools and platforms during closures later used that experience for experiential learning, reflective practice and digital literacy, with authentic experience in practice offered as the route to meaningful integration rather than being tool-driven. In a developing country context it cites a shift towards open educational resources, institutional repositories, multimedia teaching and teacher capacity building. Both are reported as claims about teachers, not as measured effects.

The section closes on continuing professional development, and on the Commonwealth of Learning's framing of support as a combination of policy, technology and capacity building within institutional cultures that reward innovation in inclusive pedagogies. That framing recurs in the conclusion, where investment decisions are set against a list of governmental priorities: skilling, employability, future skills, return on investment.

## Pedagogy as the unit of change

The middle section is a compressed history of learning theory, running from behaviorism through cognitivism and constructivism to connectivism, and then a claim about what that history did to practice. Curriculum, learning theory and pedagogy are related, and pedagogies connect to strategies, instructional design and methods, which is why the editorial treats a change in pedagogy as the substantive event and a change in platform as its occasion.

The specific shifts it lists are worth separating from the general claim, because they are the part a reader can check against their own institution. Content delivery became learning design. Individualized study became collaborative and networked study. The fixed classroom became flexible and blended provision. Assessment of learning became assessment as learning. Discipline-bound curricula became interdisciplinary ones. Behaviorist models of learner support became constructivist and connectivist ones. Underneath them sits a reorientation of learner support, from one-way mass media and two-way conferencing to synchronous and asynchronous interaction, with blogs for individual reflection, wikis for collaborative reflection, discussion forums for inquiry, and e-portfolios for self-management.

The claim that follows is the editorial's thesis in one sentence: digital transformation in open and flexible learning is not confined to technology transformation; it is essentially pedagogic transformation, moving from content-centered and institution-centered practice to learner-centered practice. The corollary is normative. Institutions need a workable relationship between technology, pedagogy and context, one in which pedagogy supplies the meaning that the technology's possibilities lack.

## What the issue's fourteen papers add, and what they do not

The editorial's summaries are short and consistently directional. Digital competence is reported as positively related to autonomous learning in the first paper. AI literacy is reported as positively related to teaching efficiency among higher education teachers in the second, and to digital confidence and ethical decision-making among higher education students in the third. The robotics study, identified as the fifth paper, finds challenge-based learning effective for conceptual understanding while arguing that more student autonomy is needed for procedural knowledge. A science education study reports that teacher ICT competency, laboratory resources and workload all relate to perceived student performance in open distance learning, with workload the stronger predictor. A mixed-methods study finds game-based learning more effective than conventional teaching in school-level open distance learning, on teamwork, critical thinking and algorithmic reasoning. The final research paper validates a learner experience and satisfaction scale.

No coefficients, sample sizes, response rates or effect sizes appear in the editorial, and it does not describe the designs beyond labels such as mixed methods. A reader who wants the numbers has to go to the papers, and a reader who wants to know whether these relationships survived controls cannot tell from this text. The editorial also stages one caution against its own enthusiasm, citing a critical review by Nicole D'Silva which holds that AI can simulate some linguistic, logical-mathematical and spatial functions but remains limited in adapting to socially situated and morally accountable human intelligences. That caution sits next to the positive findings without being reconciled with them.

## What this means for practice

For instructors the editorial's argument is a sequencing rule rather than a tool recommendation. Decide what the learning design requires, then ask what the technology can do for it, and treat the reverse order as the source of most failed adoption. The list of shifts is usable as a checklist for course redesign: whether assessment is being used to generate learning or only to certify it, whether learners work on something shared or alone, whether the course assumes a fixed place and time. The teacher-as-learning-manager formulation is the practical core, since it puts coordination of tools, resources and support inside the teaching role rather than beside it. Faculty developers can read the same argument as a warning against platform training that leaves pedagogy untouched.

## Limitations

- It is an editorial. There is no method, no sample and no analysis, and its historical chronology is asserted from a set of cited sources rather than assembled from a defined corpus, so the periodization cannot be checked against the text itself.
- Its summaries of the issue's papers report direction only. No effect sizes, sample sizes or designs are given, the findings it reports as positive relationships are correlational by phrasing, and the editorial draws no distinction between studies that support its pedagogic thesis and studies that merely fit its vocabulary.
- The central claim, that technology works only when it aligns with pedagogy in local context, is stated as a conclusion rather than tested. The editorial reports no case in which a well-aligned technology failed or a poorly aligned one succeeded, which is what the claim would need.
- The account of the field is written from a Commonwealth open schooling and open university vantage point, and its treatment of developing and underdeveloped economies is framed around investment and infrastructure deficits without examining provision in those systems directly.
