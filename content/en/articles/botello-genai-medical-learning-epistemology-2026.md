> **Synthesis:** Botello and colleagues (2026) argue that the educational meaning of generative AI in medicine lies less in measured performance gains than in what happens to the learner's reasoning. As explanations, syntheses and interpretations become available on demand, medical learning moves from information scarcity to algorithmic abundance, and part of the inferential work of clinical reasoning is shared with the system. The authors set out a continuum from cognitive augmentation, through epistemic vulnerability, to epistemic dependency, and propose epistemological resilience as the educational response: engaging with AI while retaining ownership of judgment, reflective oversight of inference, and willingness to question plausible accounts when evidence is incomplete. They argue that performance-centered outcomes cannot by themselves show whether habits of verification, tolerance of ambiguity and independent judgment are being cultivated or displaced. The article reports no empirical data: it advances a conceptual argument with proposed trajectories and pedagogical principles, with no sample, instrument, measured outcome or test of its claims.

## Key Findings
1. **The central question is epistemic, not technical.** The paper argues that gains in satisfaction, communication and short-term achievement do not establish that learners are developing the capacities needed for responsible clinical judgment under uncertainty.
2. **AI use is framed as a continuum, not a binary.** At one end algorithmic support augments reasoning; in the middle it becomes an increasing reference point for judgment; at the other it becomes the default source of reasoning. Where a learner sits depends more on the educational environment than on the tool itself.
3. **The concern is redistribution of cognitive work.** Explanatory synthesis, organization of information and hypothesis generation increasingly occur in interaction with models rather than solely inside the learner's deliberation. The worry is where reasoning habitually begins and how it is monitored, not that students cease to think.
4. **Visible success can coexist with inferential fragility.** Efficient, polished performance may mask reduced metacognitive monitoring, weaker source verification and less tolerance of ambiguity; performance-centered indicators may not detect that shift.
5. **Epistemological resilience is proposed as the aim.** It means keeping responsibility for judgments, reflecting on one's own inferential process and interrogating fluent explanations when information is contested or incomplete.
6. **Classroom design should preserve productive effort.** The paper calls for justification before AI consultation, comparison of human and AI reasoning, evidence triangulation, think-aloud reflection and discussion of unresolved clinical cases.

## The continuum and its proposed trajectories
The paper's figure sets the transition from informational scarcity to algorithmic abundance against the positions it distinguishes in AI-mediated medical learning. In cognitive augmentation the system functions as cognitive support, learner engagement is active and reflective, inferential effort is preserved, metacognitive monitoring is high, uncertainty is actively maintained and source verification is systematic; judgment remains predominantly learner-driven. In epistemic vulnerability AI supplies increasing reference points for judgment, engagement becomes selective and progressively reduced, inferential effort is diminished in selected tasks, monitoring is variable, uncertainty is progressively reduced and verification is inconsistent; judgment becomes shared between learner and AI. In epistemic dependency AI is the default source of reasoning, engagement is predominantly passive, inferential effort is habitually delegated, monitoring is limited, uncertainty is frequently avoided and verification is minimal; judgment is predominantly algorithmically mediated. The educational implications attached to these positions are expanded learning opportunities, risk of inferential fragility and risk of epistemic dependency.

The theoretical anchor is distributed cognition and cognitive scaffolding: external supports have long helped learners internalize reasoning and move toward autonomy. The authors use that tradition to ask when algorithmic support continues to promote independent reasoning and when it may foster enduring dependence. They also caution that much of the available empirical literature concerns chatbot or conversational language models, so evidence from multimodal, simulation-based or more deeply embedded systems may not yet represent the environments under discussion. Their argument is deliberately non-deterministic: algorithmic participation in reasoning is not itself the problem, and the same process can produce augmentation or vulnerability depending on how learning is organized.

## What epistemological resilience would require
The paper defines epistemological resilience as the capacity to engage with algorithmic systems while retaining ownership of judgment, maintaining reflective oversight of inferential processes and remaining willing to interrogate plausible explanations amid ambiguity, disagreement and incomplete evidence. Its tables map educational domains to objectives and example approaches. Epistemic [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] means understanding what generative systems can do and where their limitations lie, illustrated by analysis of hallucinations and exercises in communicating uncertainty. Verification practices include evidence triangulation and source checking. Metacognitive reflection uses think-aloud protocols and reflective debriefing. Comparative reasoning uses parallel human-AI diagnostic exercises. Structured cognitive friction keeps justification of decisions before AI consultation. Uncertainty navigation uses discussion of unresolved clinical cases. Reflective oversight means critiquing and revising AI-generated recommendations.

The authors stress that these principles do not require universal access to sophisticated technology or specialized curricula; they depend on reasoning-focused pedagogy, reflective supervision and explicit discussion of uncertainty during AI-assisted activities. The stated objective is not generalized skepticism toward AI but continuous intellectual oversight of algorithmic support. Clinical judgment remains the responsibility of the clinician even when the inference was partly shaped by a model, because responsibility cannot be delegated with the task.

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors.** Ask learners to state assumptions, offer a preliminary interpretation and identify what evidence would change their view before they consult a generative system on a case.
- **Instructors.** Use think-aloud accounts and reflective debriefing after AI-supported work so students examine how they monitored their own inference rather than only the answer produced.
- **Assessment designers.** Add process evidence such as defended interpretations, comparison of alternatives and critique of AI output, because short-term performance scores cannot by themselves show whether inferential effort is preserved.
- **Faculty developers.** Build epistemic AI literacy around how models produce outputs, where their limits lie and why persuasive responses still need checking, while avoiding a blanket injunction against AI use.
- **Researchers.** Test the proposed strategies directly by measuring unaided reasoning, source verification, metacognitive monitoring and tolerance of ambiguity, rather than treating the continuum or the tables as validated findings.

## Limitations
- The article is a Perspective and reports no empirical data; no sample, instrument, outcome or analysis tests the continuum, the trajectories or the proposed educational principles.
- It is a conceptual argument grounded in cited literature rather than a systematic review; no search protocol, screening procedure or formal synthesis method is reported.
- The claim that current evaluation frameworks cannot detect changes in the cognitive architecture of learning is argued, not demonstrated with a measurement study.
- The recommended approaches in the tables have not been piloted, and the paper offers no procedure for determining where a learner sits on the continuum.
- As the authors note, the empirical literature they draw on is dominated by chatbot and conversational models, which may not represent multimodal, simulation-based or embedded generative systems.
