> **Synthesis:** Nerantzi, Palfreyman and Pavlopoulou (2026) reconstruct a panel conversation among three people at the University of Leeds — two postgraduate students, one of whom is also an academic, and a further academic — about the university's red, amber and green traffic light system for [[generative-ai|generative AI]] use in [[assessment|assessment]]. The framework has run across all faculties since 2023 and assigns each assignment a color: red bans AI, amber allows it within rules the module tutor sets, green teaches and encourages responsible use. The three voices agree that the colors reduced early uncertainty about [[academic-integrity|academic integrity]] but disagree about whether the system still earns its place, and their sharpest criticism lands on amber, which each tutor interprets differently, and on the framework's silence about learning: a red assignment says nothing about whether a student may use AI to study. The conversation also works through assignments the three taught or took under amber and green, including a strategy module where the academic, as a student, documented her AI use in a dedicated appendix. The authors close by arguing that the traffic light has served its purpose and should be replaced by something that connects learning and assessment and keeps [[evaluative-judgment|human judgment]] and collaboration at the center.

*No primary data: this is a reflective conversation curated and edited from a conference panel, and every figure below is the authors' account of their own institution rather than a measured finding.*

## Key Findings

- **The framework looks simple and is not.** The authors return repeatedly to interpretation as the failure point. Amber is "vague and interpreted very differently" and amounts to "a very broad gray area" in which each module leader writes their own rules; red and green are legible as instructions but not as pedagogy. John, who supports colleagues moving modules to green, says many students and staff do not fully understand the scheme and that it needs simplification, while doubting that an infographic, podcast or animated video would be enough.
- **A red assignment contradicts an institution that wants students to learn with AI.** The authors treat the ban as a category error rather than a safeguarding measure. If a program teaches [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]], banning the tool at the point of submission separates the place where students practice from the place where they are judged, and the paper asks directly whether it is right to invite AI use in learning and forbid it in the assignment that follows.
- **The system governs assessment and is silent on learning.** This is the paper's central complaint. A color attached to an assignment answers "may I use this here?" but not "may I use this to understand the reading?", and the authors argue that the useful question is what [[student-ai-interaction|students do with the tool]] while they are learning, not only what they hand in.
- **Transparency and documentation carry more weight than the color code.** Maria describes keeping her use visible as the thing that changed her approach: it forced her to ask why she wanted the tool, whether she needed it, and what it could not do. Chrissi, taking a green assignment as a student, kept a dedicated appendix recording what she did — seeking clarification, summarizing, reducing word count, applying a matrix from the literature, visualizing ideas, and generating a podcast interview from the assignment — and argues that evidence of how AI was used belongs inside the submission rather than beside it.
- **The academic's own account is of augmentation, not outsourcing.** Chrissi reports reading widely before opening a tool, editing every output against her own ideas, and treating word-count reduction as an exercise in deciding what mattered against the criteria. The tools named are Secure Copilot, Napkin AI and NotebookLM, and she frames the sequence as iterative sense-making rather than efficiency.
- **Green assignments move design toward real-world work with a reflective component.** John has led seven postgraduate modules rated green since late 2024 and describes assessments that are case-study oriented and always include reflection on AI use, including what went wrong. He reports that most students use AI to sharpen [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]] and submission quality, that students describe the experience as not feeling like cheating, and that industry contacts respond well.
- **The student's stated risk under green is loss of voice.** Maria names the challenges plainly: keeping her own critical voice, preventing the tool from taking over the creative part, and staying careful about bias, authorship and accuracy. The paper does not resolve this; it treats the tension as the design problem a replacement framework would have to handle.
- **The authors' conclusion is that the framework is outdated.** They position the traffic light as a useful response to a moment of urgency that no longer matches how AI is woven through study, and they call for a flexible framework that connects learning and assessment and recognizes [[agency|learner agency]] and relational, trust-based practice. They explicitly refuse a technological fix, quoting Amiel et al. (2025) on the trap of treating AI as a complete solution to complex educational problems.

## How the study was done

The material is a panel discussion at the Assessment in the Age of Generative AI event hosted by the Media and Learning Association on 16 October 2025, part of the TaLAI Erasmus+ project. The three participants knew one another in two roles at once: Maria was a secondary school teacher of Greek and a student on the MA in Digital Education supervised by Chrissi; Chrissi was also a student on the Leeds Executive Leadership Apprenticeship; John was her tutor on that course and an advocate for responsible AI use in the Business School.

The authors describe the article as dialogic and reflective. The conversation was reconstructed and edited into three parts that follow the red, amber and green categories, with a closing synthesis written after revisiting the dialogue and their shared notes. The acknowledgements state the mechanics: detailed dialogic notes prepared before the event were expanded afterwards and inserted into Secure Copilot, and the output was then critically reviewed and developed by the authors. There was no sampling frame, no interview protocol, no coding procedure and no participant group beyond the three authors.

Two institutional facts frame the discussion. The traffic light has been in place since 2023 and is used across all faculties, and as of August 2025 only about half of UK higher education institutions had such a framework. In the Business School in 2024/25, around 85% of taught modules, undergraduate and postgraduate, carried red assignments, while the university's stated goal is to move all assessments to green as soon as possible.

## What this means for practice

- **A color code needs a written local definition, or it is not a policy.** Amber is the test case: if the rule is "allowed within rules set by the module leader," students in two modules are living under two policies and cannot generalize. A department that keeps a traffic light should publish, per module, what amber permits, what must be disclosed, and what evidence of use is expected.
- **Assess the process, not only the artifact.** The concrete practices described here — an appendix documenting tool, task and purpose; a reflective component asking what worked and what did not; a podcast interview generated from the assignment and then listened to — all make the learning visible. They also make the work harder to outsource, because the student has to account for decisions.
- **Model use before requiring it.** Both academics describe showing their own AI use, including in course and assessment design, as what built trust. A policy that students experience as policing while staff use tools quietly teaches the opposite of the transparency it asks for.
- **Do not ban in the assignment what you encourage in the module.** The red category creates a split the authors find indefensible once [[reflection|reflective]] and process evidence is available. Where a task genuinely must be unaided, say why in terms of the competence being certified, as the paper does when it asks what a machine cannot do in seconds.
- **Treat the framework as transitional.** The authors' own next step is a collaborative inquiry using the seven TaLAI principles, which is a reasonable template for a department: run the review as a structured inquiry with students in the room rather than as a communications refresh.

## Limitations

- **There is no dataset.** Three colleagues who already work together reconstruct a conversation they had on a panel, and the synthesis is their shared interpretation. Agreement is produced by the format as much as observed, so the paper documents a position rather than testing one.
- **One institution, and a distinctive one.** Leeds had a framework in 2023, a stated goal of moving everything to green, and a Business School where 85% of taught modules were red in 2024/25. How the argument travels to institutions without a color code, or with a different one, is not examined.
- **The examples are the authors' own modules.** The positive cases come from people who designed them and believe in them, and the student perspective is one student who is also supervised by a co-author.
- **The claim that the framework is outdated is argued, not evidenced.** No student survey, no comparison of outcomes across colors, and no analysis of the institutional guidance itself is presented.
- **The alternative is gestured at rather than specified.** The paper calls for a flexible framework connecting learning and assessment, and states an intention to pursue collaborative inquiry, but does not say what the replacement contains.
