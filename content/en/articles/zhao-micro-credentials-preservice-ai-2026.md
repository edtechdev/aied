> **Synthesis:** Zhao, Kang and Shen ask how a teacher-education institution can respond to AI faster than conventional degree structures allow. Over one semester at a Chinese normal university, they traced an AI-focused micro-credential program for pre-service teachers from its launch in March 2025 through its first cohort, combining analysis of 11 institutional documents, semi-structured interviews with two administrators, two instructors and 10 students, a satisfaction questionnaire returned by 47 of 50 enrolled students, and comparative coding of 100 paired pre- and post-course lesson plans. They report that coercive, mimetic and normative pressures drove adoption, that cross-faculty collaboration and a university–industry–school division of labor carried it out, and that satisfied students produced revised lesson plans with stronger associations among AI-related technological, pedagogical and content codes. The authors are explicit that this is one institution's first semester, with no control group and self-reported satisfaction, so the evidence concerns initial adoption rather than durable institutionalization or classroom effect.

## Key Findings

- **Three pressures, one decision.** [[educational-policy-ai|National policy]], peer institutions and professional expectations converged: the Ministry of Education's "Double Thousand Plan," reference to Beijing Normal University's micro-credential practice, and competency and employability expectations.
- **Organized across boundaries.** Implementation ran through an Office of Academic Affairs-led network, a tripartite division of labor with enterprises building platforms and K-12 schools hosting practice, and a three-semester progression from resource development to instructional integration to practical application.
- **Recruitment was rebuilt mid-course.** A written exam plus interview created excessive load and weak enrollment, so the office switched to open enrollment without entry exams; access widened, but prior knowledge became far more varied.
- **Satisfaction was high.** The five-item measure averaged 3.61 on a four-point scale; teaching attitude scored highest at 3.70 and learning outcomes and assessment methods lowest at 3.55.
- **Lesson plans shifted toward AI.** Revised plans showed stronger links between AI technological knowledge and both technological pedagogical knowledge and content knowledge, and weaker general-technology connections; the difference appeared on the first network axis, with no mean difference on the second.
- **Flexibility reproduced rigidity.** Open enrollment plus a fixed course sequence left the standardized curriculum misaligned with diverse learners, so credential-level flexibility did not become instructional flexibility.
- **Four frictions persisted.** Heavy academic workload, campus distance, uneven prior knowledge and limited hands-on practice constrained participation despite positive perceptions.

## A single case, three analytical lenses

The design was a qualitative single-case study of one [[teacher-education|teacher education]] institution, bounded from the program's launch in March 2025 to the close of its first semester in December 2025. Course capacity was 50 students, and the interviewed students were 10 of them: 4 male and 6 female, aged on average 19.8 years. Each interview lasted about 30 minutes. Alongside the interviews, the team retrieved 11 formal programmatic artifacts — calls for micro-credential applications, management policies, program proposals, implementation schemes and enrollment brochures — and analyzed 100 lesson plans, an initial and a revised design from each of 50 students.

Three frameworks divided the work. Institutional Theory explained why the program was adopted; [[activity-theory-aied|Activity Theory]] organized the account of implementation; and [[tpack|TPACK]] shaped the lesson-plan codes, with the technology dimension split into general technological knowledge and AI technological knowledge. Two researchers independently coded the interview transcripts into meaning-bearing units, reaching Cohen's κ = 0.91. For the lesson plans, two trained coders blind-coded a random 20% subset (10 pre-test and 10 post-test plans), reaching κ = 0.85; one coder then coded the remaining 80% in randomized order, blind to whether each plan was pre- or post-course.

## Why the program was built and how it ran

The drivers operated at three levels. At the macro level, [[educational-policy-ai|national policy]] supplied coercive pressure: the "Double Thousand Plan" promotes 1000 micro-credentials and 1000 vocational training courses, and the program's construction guidelines require alignment with it. At the meso level, mimetic pressure came from peers — administrators pointed to Beijing Normal University's micro-credentials and argued that traditional revision cycles could not keep pace with AI, so the credential allowed the university to "move fast in small steps." Article 13 of the guidelines states that the program is not limited to existing disciplinary catalogs. At the micro level, normative pressure came from professional competency standards, from students' fear of being replaced by AI, and from a requirement that every course include hands-on components.

Implementation was then described through the six elements of an activity system. The subject was pre-service teachers and the object was AI competency, certified by a micro-credential verifiable through China's HE Student Information and Credit System. Rules were set by an Office of Academic Affairs that funded teams from any discipline provided their proposals met two tests: enhancing student competencies and aligning with societal needs. Community spanned the School of Education, the Teacher Competency Development Center and training bases, while a tripartite division of labor gave enterprises the platforms, K-12 schools the practice sites, and university units the teaching. The mediating tool was a phased [[curriculum-design|curriculum]]: a first "Resource level" semester, a second "Instructional level" semester, and a third "Practical level" semester ending in an integrative capstone. The system's contradiction appeared in recruitment. A selection-oriented written exam plus interview overloaded students and depressed enrollment, so the office moved to an experiential-oriented model with open enrollment and no entry exams — which eased that tension and produced a new one, between a standardized curriculum and a much more varied student body.

## What the first semester produced

Of the 50 enrolled students, 47 completed the anonymous satisfaction questionnaire after the final session, a response rate of 94%. Overall satisfaction was M = 3.61, SD = 0.48 on a four-point scale. Teaching attitude scored highest (M = 3.70, SD = 0.55), followed by teaching methods (M = 3.64, SD = 0.57), learning resources (M = 3.62, SD = 0.57), and then learning outcomes and assessment methods (both M = 3.55). The authors stress that these are perceptions of the program, not measured learning.

The lesson-plan comparison used [[network-analysis|Epistemic Network Analysis]] on the 100 paired plans. Pre-test plans showed relatively strong general-technology connections; post-test plans showed denser AI-related connections, notably between AI technological knowledge and both technological pedagogical knowledge and content knowledge, alongside weaker general-technology links. A paired-samples t-test on exported network coordinates found a difference on the first axis (pre-test M = −0.308, SD = 0.177; post-test M = 0.307, SD = 0.248; t(49) = −13.84, p < 0.001, |dz| = 1.96) and no mean difference on the second; Pearson and Spearman correlations were 0.97 for both axes. The authors read this as a shift in coded associations after first-semester resource-level courses, not as evidence of changed teaching ability.

Student interviews added four challenges. Academic workload from the major program competed with hands-on micro-credential assignments. Courses sat on the old campus, and students from the new campus reported nearly one hour of commuting each way; two said [[online-teaching-and-learning|online participation]] was less effective because laboratory facilities were unavailable. [[prior-knowledge|Prior knowledge]] diverged sharply under open admission — a Music major described finding the algorithmic logic of large language models quite challenging. And students asked for more practical sessions, saying theory taught in depth was forgotten quickly without hands-on practice.

## What this means for practice

- **Teachers.** Treat the credential as one route into AI competency rather than a substitute for revising the programs around it; the study's own lesson is that responsiveness requires more than launching a new offer.
- **Administrators.** Fund differentiated support before widening access — diagnostic placement, optional prerequisites and tutoring for students without technical backgrounds, since open enrollment produced the heterogeneity.
- **Faculty developers.** Protect hands-on time. Students asked for practical sessions operating generative AI and reported forgetting theory taught without practice, and remote delivery cost them laboratory access.
- **Instructional designers.** Make flexibility reach the classroom: modular pacing within a shared sequence, rather than one fixed path that reproduces the inertia the credential was meant to escape.
- **Researchers.** Pair self-report with observation. Satisfaction was measured after the final session, and the lesson-plan shift shows coded associations, not classroom teaching.

## Limitations

- One normal university observed for a single semester, with no control group, so the lesson-plan differences cannot be attributed to the program alone.
- Satisfaction is self-reported and was collected after the final session, leaving social-desirability effects possible; interview volunteers may have been the more engaged students.
- Post-course plans were revisions made inside an AI-focused course, so added AI content may partly reflect task expectations rather than broader professional knowledge.
- Only 10 students and 4 staff members were interviewed, and quotations leaned heavily on the Office of Academic Affairs leader.
- One coder coded the remaining 80% of lesson plans beyond the jointly coded 20% subset, leaving room for coder-specific interpretation.
- Only first-semester resource-level courses had run, so the instructional- and practical-level stages of the designed progression remain untested.
