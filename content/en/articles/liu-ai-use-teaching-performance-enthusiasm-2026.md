> **Synthesis:** Liu et al. (2026) ask what happens to teachers after they adopt [[generative-ai|AI]], not whether they adopt it. With survey data from 1,661 primary and secondary school teachers in China and a serial mediation model grounded in [[theories-and-frameworks|Job Demands-Resources theory]], they find AI use positively associated with teaching performance: a direct path (β = 0.142) plus larger indirect paths through [[motivation|teacher enthusiasm]] and teaching engagement. The strongest route runs from use through enthusiasm, then engagement, to performance (β = 0.155, 37.08% of the total association). [[self-efficacy|Technological self-efficacy]] and school AI support both strengthen the AI-to-engagement link; where either is low, the link is not significant. The design is cross-sectional and self-reported, so the paper documents associations, not effects.

## Key Findings
1. **AI use is associated with better teaching performance, mostly indirectly.** The direct link is small; most of the association runs through teacher enthusiasm and teaching engagement rather than straight from use to performance.
2. **Enthusiasm moves first.** AI use is linked much more strongly to how enthusiastic teachers feel than to how hard they engage directly — the affective response carries the effect.
3. **The enthusiasm-then-engagement chain carries the largest share.** That serial route accounts for about 37% of the total association, more than either mediator on its own.
4. **Engagement is the strongest proximal predictor of performance.** Teachers who reported more engagement also reported stronger performance, and enthusiasm fed engagement in turn.
5. **Technological confidence works as a switch, not a dial.** The link between AI use and engagement is positive for teachers high in self-efficacy and effectively absent for those low in it.
6. **School AI support behaves the same way.** Under high perceived support the use–engagement link is positive; under low support it is not distinguishable from zero.
7. **Teachers report more enthusiasm than use.** Enthusiasm and engagement sit above the scale midpoint while reported AI use sits near it, so the constraint is practice, not willingness.

## AI use as a job resource, not an adoption decision

The framing is deliberately post-adoption. Where much research on [[teacher-role|teachers]] and AI asks whether they accept a tool, this study treats actual use as a pedagogical job resource: something that helps meet instructional goals, generate materials, personalize tasks, and absorb routine work. Adoption explains uptake, the authors argue, not instructional functioning.

Data come from in-service teachers in five provinces and 15 districts across China. Of 2,003 questionnaires, 1,661 survived screening for patterned and very fast responses (under 60 seconds), a valid rate of 82.93%. Primary teachers were 68.8% of the sample and middle school teachers 31.2%. Constructs were [[self-report-measures|Likert self-reports]] (α = 0.927 to 0.972), and a six-factor measurement model fit acceptably.

## The affective route from use to performance

The mechanism runs use → feeling → effort. Tools that absorb mechanical load — grading, data review, material generation — free attention for the parts of teaching teachers enjoy, which registers as enthusiasm; enthusiasm makes sustained engagement plausible; engagement is what the performance items capture.

Two details keep this honest. The direct AI-to-engagement coefficient is small (β = 0.073), so use does not automatically produce harder work — the energy is generated affectively first. And the outcome is teachers' own rating of [[human-ai-collaboration|human-machine]] practice (task design, AI-generated resources, AI-assisted feedback), not observed classroom quality or student achievement.

## When the resource pays off, and for whom

The moderation results are the most pointed. Confidence and support both amplified the AI-to-engagement association, which held among teachers high in technological self-efficacy or perceived school support and vanished among those low in either. In the confidence model the main effect of AI use on engagement was not significant on its own, reinforcing that AI is a conditional resource.

The authors read this through the resource interaction hypothesis: a job resource converts into investment only when the person has confidence to keep experimenting and the organization supplies training, infrastructure, and tolerance for trial and error. Unreliable or vague AI output, they note from adjacent work, can raise anxiety rather than relieve it, so motivational value depends on tool quality as much as presence.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors:** Read your own enthusiasm as a diagnostic, not a mood. AI use related to performance mainly by raising enjoyment and interest first (β = 0.387) and effort second; if AI work feels like extra administration, the pathway is not running.
- **Administrators:** Adoption counts are a weak success metric. The AI-to-engagement link held where teachers reported high school AI support (simple slope 0.108) and vanished where support was low (0.012) — training, technical help, and tolerance for failed experiments are the levers.
- **Administrators:** Differentiate by confidence. Teachers low in self-efficacy showed no AI-to-engagement association (-0.020), so hands-on guidance and [[professional-training|professional development]] matter more than autonomy.
- **Researchers:** Model the affective step. A two-mediator chain explained more of the association than either mediator alone (37.08% of the total effect); engagement-only models will understate why AI use changes practice.

## Limitations

- Cross-sectional, single-informant self-report; the authors state that causal inference is not possible and that reciprocal relations may hold among use, enthusiasm, engagement, and performance.
- Performance is teacher-rated on an AI-specific scale, so shared method and social desirability inflate associations; observation, peer rating, and student feedback are the proposed remedy.
- Only the motivational branch of JD-R was tested; burnout, strain, professional identity, and instructional innovation were left out.
- AI use was treated as one general resource, with no differentiation by tool type, task, or quality, and the sample is confined to Chinese primary and secondary schools.
