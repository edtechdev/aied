> **Synthesis:** Nouri, Panahi and Ghalavand report an experiential qualitative study of all 14 undergraduates in a Medical Library and Information Science program at Abadan University of Medical Sciences who were taking a 17-week research methodology course in the 2025–2026 semester. Across the course the students submitted 138 written Experience Registration Forms describing how they used [[generative-ai|generative AI]] tools — ChatGPT, Elicit, Consensus and Scite — for topic selection, problem formulation, literature review and proposal writing. Reflexive thematic analysis produced five themes and 23 subthemes. Students experienced AI as an accessible companion that reduced uncertainty and helped them start writing, and they chose tools for explanation, dialogue, credible references and round-the-clock access. Alongside that, they reported fabricated references, a drift toward waiting for answers instead of thinking, and uncertainty about whether using AI counted as support or misconduct. The authors frame AI as a cognitive mediator whose value depends on critical evaluation, and they call for critical AI literacy and institutional guidance rather than prohibition.

## Key Findings

- **AI acted as a companion, not a reference.** Students described an always-available tutor that would re-explain a concept until it made sense and removed the fear of the blank first paragraph.
- **Tool choice followed learning value.** Participants selected tools for clear explanations, iterative dialogue, scientific credibility and instant accessibility rather than technical sophistication.
- **Benefits clustered across the whole proposal process.** AI supported idea generation, question refinement, literature synthesis, academic writing and self-directed exploration.
- **[[hallucination-risk|Fabricated references]] triggered verification.** One student found a suggested article did not exist and thereafter checked every citation against scientific databases.
- **Convenience carried a cost.** Several students reported waiting for answers instead of reasoning, difficulty writing when the internet failed, and proposals that sounded more academic but felt less like their own.
- **Satisfaction was perceptual.** Students linked satisfaction to time saved, confidence, clearer understanding, immediate feedback and autonomy — perceptions of usefulness rather than measured learning.
- **Guidance was absent.** No formal instruction on prompting, hallucinations, reference verification, integrity, privacy or responsible use was provided, so students' experience reflected their own emerging judgment.

## How the study was conducted

All 14 students enrolled in the program participated (100% of the course cohort); 11 were female and 3 male, aged 19 to 25, all taking the Research Methodology course with no previous research or proposal-writing experience. Data were collected during the 17-week course in the 2025–2026 academic semester with an Experience Registration Form designed by the research team, reviewed by three faculty members and pilot-tested with three students. After each teaching session students had about one week to record how they used general-purpose and domain-specific AI tools for that topic; 138 completed forms were analyzed with MAXQDA version 10. The researchers describe the design as experiential qualitative inquiry rather than phenomenology, because the data are written records rather than interviews. Rigor followed credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability; member checking was limited to verifying the accuracy of recorded accounts.

## Five themes, one tension

The first theme captured AI as an interactive research companion that reduced uncertainty and supported progress through unfamiliar tasks, alongside worry about losing the ability to solve methodological problems alone. The second covered how students negotiated educational value when selecting tools: simplicity and comprehensibility, interactive dialogue and iterative feedback, scientific credibility, and rapid accessibility. The third mapped AI support across idea generation, research questions and objectives, literature summarization and synthesis, academic writing and [[self-directed-learning|self-directed learning]]. The fourth recorded the benefits-and-risks negotiation — hallucinated information, reduced critical engagement, perceived dependence, [[ethics|ethical uncertainty]] and concerns about academic voice. The fifth listed the conditions behind satisfaction: time efficiency, increased confidence, deeper understanding, [[feedback|immediate feedback]] and greater learning autonomy. The analysis distinguished patterns evident across participants from those that looked individual or context-specific.

## What students actually did

Participants used AI to turn broad interests into feasible topics and to revise questions and objectives; multiple alternative formulations let them compare possibilities before choosing. Domain-specific tools such as Elicit, Consensus and Scite helped them locate relevant articles and see relationships among studies, which they experienced as faster and better oriented than database searching — the authors note this reflects perceived efficiency rather than an objective assessment of search completeness. Writing support went beyond grammar to restructuring arguments and improving scientific style. One participant described becoming less dependent on the instructor: "At times, the AI even suggested how to write the subsequent sections and stages of the proposal."

Risks appeared in the same records. Participant 13 searched for an article the AI had suggested and found it did not exist; another described taking AI-generated material to class unreviewed and being unable to elaborate during the presentation. Participant 11 said that when the internet stopped working, "I felt that I could no longer continue writing." Ethical ambiguity came from the absence of clear institutional guidance, and Participant 9 reported that a later proposal "no longer felt like my own writing."

## Why verification is not yet critical AI literacy

The authors treat reference checking as an emerging protective response rather than evidence of comprehensive [[ai-literacy|critical AI literacy]], which also involves model limitations, bias, [[privacy|privacy]], data provenance, disclosure requirements and authorship boundaries. They situate the findings against quantitative work on outcomes and acceptance, arguing that lived experience fills a different gap, and they read AI as a co-regulator of learning rather than an information-retrieval system. The practical implication is staged AI activities: verify every AI-produced reference, compare explanations with authoritative sources, document and critique prompts, identify fabricated or biased output, and defend methodological decisions independently. The boundary they propose is that explanations, brainstorming and language feedback may be delegated, while question selection, evidence evaluation, method choice, interpretation and final scholarly decisions stay with the student.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Replace prohibition with staged AI tasks: ask students to verify each generated reference, compare AI explanations with a textbook, document prompts, and identify fabricated or incomplete output, so encounters with AI errors become practice in evaluation.
- **Instructors.** Make delegation boundaries explicit. Explain which parts of proposal writing AI may support and which decisions remain the student's, since participants reported uncertainty about whether AI use was support or cheating.
- **Librarians.** Teach source-verification routines alongside discovery tools. Students trusted Elicit, Consensus and Scite for orientation but needed database confirmation for citations.
- **Faculty developers.** Build critical AI literacy into research-methods courses rather than assuming it transfers, covering model limitations, bias, privacy, disclosure and authorship in addition to prompting.
- **Researchers.** Pair reflections with behavioral measures. The study cannot establish whether confidence, reliance or verification changed over the semester, so combine written accounts with interaction logs and pre/post measures of methodology knowledge.

## Limitations

- Fourteen students from one program at one institution, with the relationship to the wider cohort unclear, so the findings are context-specific rather than statistically generalizable.
- Written Experience Registration Forms offered fewer chances to probe, clarify or explore contradictions than interviews would have, constraining depth.
- The 138 records were analyzed as a collective dataset; individual trajectories were not traced, so change over the semester cannot be established.
- The findings rest on self-reported perceptions, with no objective measures of learning outcomes, methodology competence, proposal quality or critical thinking.
- Tool versions, prompts, outputs and access conditions were not controlled or documented, and participants received no formal training in prompting, verification, integrity, privacy or responsible use.
- Researcher-designed prompts may have shaped the responses, particularly on benefits, challenges and satisfaction.
