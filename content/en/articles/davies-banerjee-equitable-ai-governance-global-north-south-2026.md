> **Synthesis:** Davies, Campbell and Banerjee ask whether AI in education will narrow or widen [[equity-in-ai-education|educational equity]], and they put the question to the people who live it. A [[quantitative-research|quantitative online survey]] using Likert-scale and categorical items reached 39 adults in higher education and related computing contexts; 21 identified with the Global North, 15 with the [[global-south|Global South]] and 3 preferred not to say. Respondents expressed moderate optimism that AI could help reduce inequality between regions, but concern about [[privacy|privacy]] and [[bias-mitigation|algorithmic bias]] was near-universal, and over half said educators and students receive too little ethical guidance. Responsibility for ethical oversight was most often shared across governments, institutions, educators and students rather than left to technology companies, pointing toward collaborative [[governance|governance]] models. Data privacy, bias and fairness, and transparency topped the ethical priorities. The pattern is convergence rather than contrast: ethical attitudes looked strikingly similar across regions even where infrastructure differed. The authors present the result as an exploratory snapshot from a small voluntary sample, not a population estimate.

## Key Findings

- **Moderate optimism, not confidence.** About a third of respondents agreed or strongly agreed that AI could help reduce inequality between regions, while the largest single group chose the midpoint of the scale.
- **Risk concern is near-universal.** More than four in five rated privacy and algorithmic bias concerns at the high end of the scale, and the level of agreement was consistent across regions.
- **Guidance is seen as inadequate.** Nearly two thirds said teachers, lecturers and students do not receive enough guidance on the ethical use of AI, and only one respondent gave the highest rating.
- **Responsibility is expected to be shared.** Most respondents chose "all equally" across governments, institutions, educators, students and developers; technology companies were selected least often.
- **Privacy, fairness and transparency lead the priorities.** Data privacy ranked first, followed by bias and fairness and then transparency, while access and equity was selected least often.
- **Ethics converged while infrastructure did not.** Regional attitudes looked similar despite structural disparities, which the authors read as an internationally synchronized ethical discourse rather than a regionally isolated one.

## Design, sample and analysis

The study used a questionnaire-based design with Likert-scale and categorical items to capture awareness, attitudes, perceived risks, ethical concerns and governance expectations. Likert items ran from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Participation was voluntary and anonymous, consent and data-protection guidelines were followed, and the target population was adults aged eighteen and over engaged in higher education or related educational and computing contexts, including students, educators and professionals. Because geographical diversity mattered, sampling was non-probability and voluntary; the authors argue this is appropriate for perception research in a rapidly evolving domain, while acknowledging that it limits statistical generalizability.

Region was self-identified, with a "prefer not to say" option retained for data minimization. Of the 39 respondents, 21 (53.8%) identified with the Global North, 15 (38.5%) with the Global South, and 3 (7.7%) chose not to say. Whole-sample analyses used all 39; comparative regional analyses used the 36 who could be classified. Two items allowed multiple selections, so their percentages can sum to more than 100%. Analysis was descriptive: frequency distributions and percentages, cross-tabulation by region, and thematic categorization of limited open-text responses. The authors report raw frequencies and denominators throughout.

## What respondents said

On whether AI could help reduce inequality between regions, 14 of 39 respondents (35.9%) selected the midpoint, while 13 of 39 (33.3%) chose the top two points — 5 selected 4 and 8 selected 5. Normalizing agreement against regional totals, 6 of 15 Global South respondents (40.0%) were positive compared with 6 of 21 Global North respondents (28.6%), a difference of 11.4 percentage points; the remaining positive response came from a respondent who preferred not to say and was excluded from the regional comparison. The authors read the higher Global South positivity as consistent with work finding perceptions of AI's developmental potential tend to be higher in emerging economies, while the overall distribution still shows persistent caution.

Concern about ethical risk was pronounced: 32 of 39 respondents (82.1%) rated privacy and algorithmic bias concerns at 4 (13 respondents) or 5 (19 respondents). Confidence in institutional preparedness was low by comparison. Asked whether teachers, lecturers and students receive enough guidance on ethical AI use, 25 of 39 (64.1%) selected 1 (10 respondents) or 2 (15 respondents), and only one respondent (2.6%) selected 5. The consistency of these answers across regions leads the authors to conclude that readiness gaps are global in scope rather than confined to the Global South.

When asked who should be responsible for ensuring ethical AI use, 24 of 39 (61.5%) selected "all equally." Beyond that shared model, responsibility was attributed to educators and students (13 of 39; 33.3%), schools and universities (12 of 39; 30.8%) and governments (11 of 39; 28.2%); technology companies were selected least often (6 of 39; 15.4%). For ethical priorities, respondents named data privacy (27 of 39; 69.2%), bias and fairness (24 of 39; 61.5%), transparency (21 of 39; 53.8%), accountability (17 of 39; 43.6%) and access and equity (12 of 39; 30.8%).

## Interpretation and recommendations

The paper's central claim is convergence. Despite clear structural differences in infrastructure, governance capacity and teacher training, respondents in both regions raised similar ethical concerns, which the authors attribute to shared digital media, transnational policy frameworks and widespread public debate. They also note a shift: although access and equity dominate Global South policy discourse, respondents prioritized algorithmic ethics over infrastructural concerns, suggesting technically grounded ethical expectations.

The recommendations follow from that reading. Institutions should provide structured ethical AI training covering data protection, algorithmic bias, fairness, transparency, explainability and safe generative AI use. Governance should be participatory — ethics committees, community consultation and co-designed policy — rather than top-down. Data governance should include transparent collection policies, independent algorithmic audits, culturally representative datasets and clear model documentation. Infrastructural barriers in Global South contexts call for expanded connectivity, device access, teacher development and tools designed for low-resource environments. Systems should be localized through language adaptation, curricular alignment, validation across regional populations and low-connectivity functionality. And AI tools should incorporate explainable features, clear decision pathways and human-in-the-loop oversight. These recommendations rest on descriptive data and prior literature; none was tested as an intervention in this study.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Ask what ethical guidance students have actually received before assuming it exists; most respondents in this survey reported that educators and students get too little of it.
- **Faculty developers.** Build structured training on data protection, algorithmic bias, fairness, transparency, explainability and safe generative AI use, and treat it as continuing development rather than a one-off briefing.
- **Administrators.** Create participatory oversight such as ethics committees, community consultation and co-designed policy, and pair it with transparent data-collection policies and independent algorithmic audits.
- **Instructional designers.** Favor tools with explainable outputs, clear decision paths, human-in-the-loop oversight and low-connectivity functionality, and validate them with regional populations before deployment.
- **Researchers.** Treat these 39 voluntary responses as an exploratory snapshot, and replicate with larger stratified samples and longitudinal designs before generalizing about regions.

## Limitations

- Voluntary response sampling of 39 adults restricts generalization, and the regional comparison rests on only 36 classified respondents.
- The cross-sectional design prevents any conclusion about how attitudes change over time.
- Single-item, self-reported perception measures, plus limited open-text responses, restrict construct validity and explanatory depth.
- Region was self-identified, and three respondents who preferred not to say were excluded from regional comparisons.
- The recommendations follow from descriptive data and prior literature; none was tested as an intervention in this study.
