> **Synthesis:** A [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] inquiry in a Saudi technical-communication course examined how 13 undergraduate computing students respond when explicitly told that ChatGPT, rather than their instructor, generated their writing feedback and score. Students accepted GenAI feedback as clear and useful for [[writing-education|surface-level revision]] yet consistently reserved [[teacher-role|grading authority]] for the human instructor, drawing a sharp distinction between *feedback utility* and *evaluative authority* that reconciles competing [[trust-calibration|algorithm-aversion and appreciation]] findings. Transparency about AI involvement shifted student reflection from [[ai-feedback-quality|feedback quality]] toward broader questions of [[assessment|assessment legitimacy]], [[trust-calibration|trust]], and the [[governance|institutional]] role of the instructor. The study proposes a three-principle framework (transparency, human mediation, reflective practice) for [[ethics|ethical]] GenAI integration in writing [[assessment]].

## Transparency as an instructional intervention

Prior research on [[generative-ai|GenAI]] feedback has focused heavily on whether AI-generated feedback improves [[writing-education|writing]] performance and how useful students perceive it to be; comparatively little work asks how students interpret evaluation when they know an AI produced both the feedback and the [[summative-assessment|grade]]. This study foregrounds [[assessment|transparency]] as the central intervention: students wrote an in-class handwritten task, the scanned submissions were evaluated by ChatGPT against a rubric, and students were then explicitly told ChatGPT had generated their score and were invited to reflect on it in writing.

Using [[research-methods-aied|inductive thematic analysis]] of the 13 handwritten reflections, four themes emerged: perceived usefulness of feedback; awareness of AI's contextual and pedagogical limitations; conditional trust distinguishing feedback utility from evaluative authority; and reflection on the institutional and pedagogical role of the human instructor. The handwritten, in-class design was deliberate — it assured the work under evaluation was authentically the student's own, so that questions of [[trust-calibration|trust]], authority, and legitimacy could be examined without authorship ambiguity.

## The feedback utility / evaluative authority distinction

The most analytically distinctive finding was that participants could *simultaneously* affirm the usefulness of ChatGPT's feedback and reject ChatGPT as an appropriate final evaluator. Students repeatedly separated "this feedback is useful" from "this system should decide my grade" — treating them as analytically separate judgements rather than opposite ends of a single approval scale. Representative reflections included: "I agree with it, but with one condition, that the doctor checks the feedback to edit any mistakes," and "Doctors can use AI for [reading] feedback for exams only, but if they use it for [grading] students, problems will happen."

This task-specific pattern of [[trust-calibration|conditional trust]] extends the [[research-methods-aied|algorithm-trust]] literature. Where Dietvorst et al. document algorithm aversion and Logg et al. document algorithm appreciation, these students exhibited neither blanket aversion nor blanket appreciation — they made *separate judgements about distinct functions of the same system*, accepting AI for surface-level [[feedback]] while preserving human authority for evaluation. The authors argue this is a previously unnamed distinction that gives a more precise vocabulary for designing AI-mediated [[assessment|assessment practices]].

## Student reasoning about instructor authority

Participants articulated positive reasons — not mere technophobia — for preserving human evaluative authority in [[higher-ed|higher education]]. Four arguments recurred:

- **Knowledge of individual students:** the teacher "knows much better about students and knows good ways to grade students and support students' learning."
- **Dialogical assessment:** "ChatGPT is not like people. You talk to them and explain excuses to consider or give another chance if you fail" — framing grading as a relational practice requiring interpretation and dialogue.
- **Institutional legitimacy:** "If ChatGPT [is] checking the exams, why are we going to university? Why not learn from ChatGPT and test ourselves in ChatGPT?" — a question about the very purpose of formal higher education when assessment is delegated to AI.
- **[[bias-mitigation|Fairness]] against AI error:** "AI can make mistakes that will affect the student's grades," positioning the instructor as a safeguard against consequential error.

Students also showed critical awareness of AI limitations beyond technical error — that ChatGPT "doesn't know [the instructor's] rating system," that it "always tries to be positive," and that it could misread scanned handwriting — leading some to "take its score less seriously."

## A three-principle framework for ethical integration

The paper concludes with a framework for ethical GenAI integration in writing assessment organized around three principles: **transparency** (clear communication about AI involvement, which functions not merely as an ethical disclosure but as a pedagogical strategy in its own right), **human mediation** (instructors retain evaluative authority over AI outputs), and **reflective practice** (post-assessment reflection that shifts student attention from textual correction to systemic questions about the assessment system).

The findings are explicitly bounded — a single course, single semester, 13 male computing students at one Saudi university — but offer a transferable model: transparency about AI involvement may transform [[feedback]] from a taken-for-granted instructional act into an object of critical reflection, and post-assessment reflection is an inexpensive, easily replicated intervention for surfacing student reasoning about [[automated-assessment|automated evaluation]].
