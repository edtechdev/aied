> **Synthesis:** Ross and Kaspar propose ACLIME, a conceptual framework for agent-centered learning in immersive media environments, defined as [[pedagogical-agent]]s embedded in [[virtual-and-augmented-reality]] (AR, augmented virtuality and VR) as the focal point of learner interaction. The framework has three layers. A technical foundation of high immersion, multimodal body-based interactivity, and the visual and behavioral realism of both the agent and the learner's own virtual representation. [[learning-design|Instructional design]] principles, which moderate how those technical possibilities are translated into concrete design features. And four psychological clusters: the learner's virtual self-experience (physical presence, self-presence, [[embodied-learning]] to the point of a body ownership illusion, and cybersickness), the learner's experience of the agent ([[community-of-inquiry|social presence]], human-agent space, agent awareness), social interaction with the agent (similarity to human-human interaction, its direction, intensity and persistence, and the proteus effect), and immediate learning factors (socio-emotional reactions, [[motivation]], [[self-efficacy]], cognitive load, cognitive activation). ACLIME differs from CAMIL, CATLM-VR and TICOL by keeping the agent inside the model, covering AR alongside VR, and integrating [[learning-theories]] in the form of temporal dynamics: familiarization, the maturation of human-agent relationships, the novelty effect, and the development of cybersickness and extraneous cognitive load. It is a synthesis of theory and adjacent evidence, not an empirical test; long-term learning outcomes and learner characteristics sit deliberately outside its scope.

## Key Findings

1. **The framework names its own parts.** The technical foundation comprises high immersion and multimodal, body-based interactivity plus high visual and behavioral realism of the pedagogical agent and of the learner's virtual representation; these feed three psychological clusters (learner's virtual self-experience, learner's experience of the agent, social interaction with the pedagogical agent) plus a fourth cluster of immediate learning factors, with instructional design principles modeled as moderators and long-term learning outcomes (factual, conceptual, procedural and [[transfer-of-learning|transfer]] knowledge) factored out.
2. **Two interaction modes define the state of the art.** Following Hemminki-Reijonen and colleagues, the agent either acts as a tutor, offering guidance, encouragement, reflective questioning and explanations, or as a role-playing partner that adopts a defined role inside the scenario and enables realistic, [[situated-learning]]-style dialogue, as in a climate-change virtual field trip or a corporate negotiation [[simulation]].
3. **Immersive media differ technically in three ways.** Immersion is high because head-mounted displays deliver stereoscopic, wide-angle, high-quality stimulation that displaces physical reality along the reality-virtuality continuum from AR through AV to VR; interactivity is body-based and multimodal (movement, gaze, voice); and representation becomes virtualizable along the body avatarization continuum from real body through body accessorization and partial avatarization to full avatarization.
4. **Realism cuts both ways.** Visual and behavioral realism of the agent are treated as technical variables, and behavioral realism is claimed as the decisive predictor of social presence, because XR multiplies high-quality social cues and adds haptic channels absent from non-immersive media; agents are computer-controlled while avatars are person-controlled (virtual human agency), and Blascovich's threshold model implies low perceived agency must be offset by high behavioral realism, while the uncanny valley threatens the formation of a sound human-agent space.
5. **The psychological clusters carry named dimensions.** Learner's virtual self-experience: physical presence, self-presence, embodiment (body ownership, virtual own body agency, self-location) and cybersickness. Learner's experience of the agent: social presence, human-agent space (adapted from social space) and agent awareness, including virtual human agency. Social interaction: similarity to real-life human-human interaction, direction (task-related versus social-relational), intensity and persistence, with the proteus effect as an additional shaper.
6. **Cognitive load is the framework's explicit trade-off.** Immersion and agent realism push extraneous cognitive load up (the immersion-as-distractor hypothesis, and the agent's mere presence as a source of social cues to be processed), especially in VR compared with AR, while social interaction with the agent can lower intrinsic load through the collective working-memory effect and lower transaction costs because immersive interaction is biologically primary; the authors conclude the net direction cannot be settled on current evidence.
7. **Temporal dynamics are given their own layer.** Duration, frequency and timespan are distinguished; presence, enjoyment and group perception rise with usage duration, the human-agent space is argued to mature through the four Social Penetration Theory stages (orientation, exploratory affective exchange, affective exchange, stable exchange), the novelty effect may later depress motivation, and the developmental trajectory is modeled as an early peak then an equilibrium above non-immersive baselines.
8. **Potentials and risks are tabulated side by side (Table 1).** Physical presence, self-presence and embodiment are potentials against cybersickness as a risk; pronounced social presence is beneficial when social influence or relationship building is intended but counterproductive where uninhibited exploration is the goal, since it can promote self-presentation and impression management rather than honest self-disclosure; motivation, self-efficacy and cognitive activation are potentials against novelty-driven decline and possible cognitive overload.
9. **The research agenda is largely unfilled.** Hardly any empirical work addresses learning with pedagogical agents in immersive media, so the framework is offered as a foundation for studies that test its relationships, including mediation and moderation among agent realism, social presence, human-agent space, agent awareness and social-relational behavior, longitudinal designs with repeated within-session measurement, concrete instructional design principles, [[agentic-ai|multi-agent]] and mixed human-agent groupings, and learner characteristics and long-term outcomes that ACLIME deliberately excludes.

## The ACLIME framework and its components

The framework's guiding claim is that the characteristics of human-agent interaction in immersive media originate in technical features. High immersion and multimodal, body-based interactivity are the two features that separate immersive from non-immersive media, and they are joined by the visual and behavioral realism of the pedagogical agent and of the learner's virtual representation. These variables are the technical foundation; everything psychological is modeled as growing out of them, in keeping with the lineage of immersive learning theory the authors build on. The authors deliberately exclude environmental virtual settings and appearance attributes such as attractiveness, gender, age, weight or height, and they follow a principle of economy in leaving learner characteristics such as personality, intelligence and [[prior-knowledge|prior knowledge]] outside the model.

Instructional design principles are the framework's moderators. The authors place them between technical foundation and psychological dimensions, drawing on design science research in which design principles are prescriptive statements abstracted into meta-requirements and instantiated as concrete design features. The point of the placement is that potentials are realizable and risks mitigable only through design; because such principles are context-dependent, they are treated at conceptual level only.

The psychological clusters then hold the substance. The learner's virtual self-experience contains physical presence (the extent to which the virtual environment is experienced as the actual one, including the perceptual relocation of the self in VR), self-presence (the context-specific mental model of the self in virtual space), embodiment as the [[embodied-learning]] sense of possessing, controlling and being within a body, and cybersickness, which persists as a practical challenge with roughly 42 percent of VR headset users reporting symptoms and which recent evidence extends to AR. The learner's experience of the agent contains social presence, the human-agent space that replaces the collective notion of social space in [[collaborative-learning|collaborative learning]], and agent awareness, the learner's mental model of the agent's attributes, within which virtual human agency locates the felt distinction between agent and avatar. Social interaction with the pedagogical agent contains similarity to real-life human-human interaction, grounded in the computers-are-social-actors paradigm and in biological primary communication, and the direction, intensity and persistence of learner behavior, whose direction splits into task-related and social-relational activities, with the proteus effect as a further influence. Immediate learning factors, deliberately described as non-exhaustive, are socio-emotional reactions, motivation, self-efficacy, cognitive load and cognitive activation.

## The typology of pedagogical agents in extended reality

The paper's typology is thin by design and precise where it matters. Two interaction modes are taken from the literature: the tutor, which supports [[learners]] through guidance, encouragement, reflective questioning and explanations across the learning process, and the role-playing partner, which takes a defined role in the scenario and opens realistic, situated dialogue resembling interaction with real-world counterparts. The paper's examples show what each affords. A tutor guides learners through three-dimensional anatomical structures in natural dialogue, extending established anatomy instruction; a role-playing partner appears as a local in a virtual field trip to a climate-change-affected region, producing reciprocal exchange that builds topic understanding and emotional involvement, or as a negotiating counterpart in corporate training for purchasing and sales staff, where the simulation adapts to learner behavior and [[feedback]] iteratively improves communication and decision-making.

Agent characteristics are expressed through body and behavior, which is why the framework treats both as technical design surfaces. Appearance varies in how much of the body is represented, from head only through head and shoulders and upper body to the full body, and in features such as size, stature, gender, clothing, age, hair, skin color and physical attractiveness. Behavior may be controlled flexibly through artificial intelligence or by fixed rule-based specifications, with the development of [[llm|large language models]] enabling more flexible and natural verbal communication. Verbal output arrives as synthesized speech, pre-recorded human speech or visually presented text; nonverbal output uses gestures, gaze, facial expressions, posture and proxemics, the last of which Hall introduced and which is still implemented in few virtual agents. Realism is then classified as visual or behavioral, and the behavioral form is what must carry the interaction when perceived virtual human agency is low. What the agent cannot do is carry the whole burden: the authors warn that more realism is not always better, because the uncanny valley can produce sudden rejection and even impede relationship formation.

## Theoretical grounding: presence, embodiment and social agency

The framework's theoretical spine is the distinction between physical presence as spatial-perceptual self-localization, self-presence as virtual identity, and social presence as the experience of being with another intentional being whose mental states are perceived as accessible. Physical presence is attributed to immersion and interactivity and is expected in both VR and AR, though it manifests differently: VR relocates the self perceptually, while in AR the user stays located in the real world with virtually enriched surroundings. Embodiment is defined through body ownership, virtual own body agency and self-location, reproducing Kilteni and colleagues' triad, with representation through one's own real body in AR treated as the gold standard and the body ownership illusion as unique to immersive media, made possible by high immersion, body-based interactivity and pronounced behavioral realism of the learner's avatar. Self-presence is explicitly not equated with embodiment: the perception of one's own virtual body shapes self-presence.

Social presence theory supplies the agent-facing side. Oh and colleagues' review is used for the claim that exchanging more high-quality social cues, ideally across modalities, strengthens social presence, and Feine and colleagues' taxonomy organizes those cues as verbal (content and style), visual (kinesics, proxemics, graphical appearance), auditory (voice qualities, vocalizations) and invisible (chronemics, haptics). Because XR renders visible cues stereoscopically and can add haptic social cues such as a hand pat on the back, agent realism, particularly behavioral, is proposed as the crucial predictor of social presence here. On top of this sit the computers-are-social-actors paradigm, which explains why learners greet agents and use pleasantries, Blascovich's threshold model of social influence, where virtual human agency and behavioral realism independently push social presence past the point of human-like interaction, and Social Penetration Theory, which supplies the stage model through which self-disclosure both reflects and drives the maturation of the human-agent space. Trust toward the agent, personal connection and a sense of togetherness mark a sound human-agent space; [[privacy]] relevant perceived costs can slow its development. Cognitive load theory and its collaborative extension complete the theoretical set, adding intrinsic, extraneous and germane load, the collective working-memory effect and transaction costs to a framework that otherwise reads as social-cognitive.

## Design and research implications

The design implication is that the technical foundation should never be read as a benefit in itself. [[human-ai-collaboration]] with an agent is a cognitive trade-off whose educational value depends on design choices that mitigate extraneous cognitive load while exploiting opportunities to offload intrinsic load, and the design principles layer exists precisely to turn immersion, interactivity and realism into design features such as relevant visuals, [[pedagogy|instructional strategies]] and feedback formats. Two operational recommendations follow directly from the risk analysis: personality screening before certain scenarios, and rapid exit strategies so learners can leave an XR setting when discomfort becomes significant. The authors also recommend scheduling adequate breaks between sessions, given cybersickness after-effects that last from around ten minutes to several hours. On motivation the framework predicts that agents can counteract the novelty-driven decline by satisfying relatedness, and the deep learning hypothesis formulated for immersive media is explicitly extended to immersive agents.

The research implications are stated as a gap list. The relationships among agent realism, social presence, human-agent space, agent awareness and social-relational behavior need testing, particularly with respect to mediation and moderation; the opposing extraneous-load effects need boundary conditions established; temporal dynamics need longitudinal studies across sessions plus repeated measurement within sessions, since one-off short sessions cannot reveal where a variable sits on a developmental trajectory; concrete instructional design principles still have to be derived; more complex scenarios with multiple agents or mixed groups of agents and human learners are called for; systematic interaction analyses should check whether learner behavior toward agents really mirrors behavior toward people; and the effects of individual characteristics and of the distinctive dimensions on long-term learning outcomes should be examined empirically.

## Potentials, risks and the limits of a conceptual framework

This is a conceptual framework paper, not an empirical study, and the authors say so plainly. Hardly any empirical studies have been conducted on interaction and learning with pedagogical agents in immersive media, and some of the relationships the framework asserts are extrapolated from adjacent research areas: immersive interaction with avatars, or [[pedagogical-agent]]s in non-immersive media. The framework is also deliberately partial. It is restricted to the prototypical interaction of one learner with one pedagogical agent, leaving multiple agents and mixed agent-human groupings unconsidered, though the authors expect the basic ideas to scale. It excludes learner characteristics such as personality traits, intelligence and personal knowledge, on grounds of economy, while noting that initial work links virtual environment learning to personality. It puts environmental settings and [[multimodal]] appearance attributes of agent and avatar outside scope. It factors out long-term learning outcomes entirely, so the framework cannot yet say whether its psychological potentials convert into factual, conceptual, procedural or transfer learning. And it leaves instructional design principles at conceptual level, which means the layer that is supposed to convert potentials into practice is the least specified.

Two consequences follow for how the evidence should be read. First, several claims are explicitly unresolved rather than merely untested: whether extraneous cognitive load increases or decreases overall under immersive agent interaction, whether social presence acts as moderator or mediator of realistic social responding, and whether agent awareness raises or lowers extraneous load, all remain open in the authors' own reading. Second, the framework is offered as a foundation for future work rather than a validated model, and its temporal layer is admittedly hypothetical: the developmental trajectory in Figure 3 is exemplary, with the course of change depending on session duration, frequency, technical setup, task complexity, prior XR experience and learner characteristics. Where the paper does cite [[quantitative-research|quantitative]] anchors, such as cybersickness prevalence or the timing of symptom peaks and after-effects, those come from immersive-media research that does not involve pedagogical agents at all.

## What this means for practice

- **Designers.** Cap exposure and build a visible exit into every session, because roughly 42% of VR headset users report cybersickness symptoms, after-effects run from around ten minutes up to several hours, and the authors' own recommendations are rapid exit strategies plus scheduled breaks between sessions.
- **Designers.** Spend the agent's realism budget on behavior rather than appearance: behavioral realism is the framework's decisive predictor of social presence, while raising visual fidelity risks the uncanny valley and a sudden rejection of the agent.
- **Instructors.** Decide per scenario whether you want pronounced social presence, since it cuts both ways: it helps when the aim is social influence or relationship building, and it works against you when uninhibited exploration or honest self-disclosure is the goal, because the learner turns to impression management instead.
- **Instructors.** Screen learners for cybersickness susceptibility and keep AR available as the fallback, since the framework expects physical presence in both AR and VR while treating AR as the condition that leaves the learner located in the real room.
- **Researchers.** Instrument all four psychological clusters — virtual self-experience, experience of the agent, social interaction with the agent, and the immediate learning factors — when you evaluate an XR agent, because ACLIME factors long-term outcomes out and therefore cannot tell you whether a deployment taught anything.
