> **Synthesis:** Chan and colleagues coded the syllabi of every offering of one biology department's four core required courses — 38 syllabi in one 2025–2026 term, a 100% collection rate — and surveyed its [[teacher-role|instructors]] on which graded categories they consider vulnerable to academic dishonesty. Only **in-person proctored exams** were perceived as minimally vulnerable; on average **about a third of a student's grade (33%) was highly vulnerable and roughly 80% (81%) at least somewhat vulnerable** to [[academic-integrity|academic dishonesty]]. Outside-of-class exams were rated similarly vulnerable whether proctored or not, and the low-stakes assignments that evidence-based [[formative-assessment|assessment reform]] has promoted for thirty years are what instructors see as most exposed. The authors frame this as a grading-design problem that threatens the [[assessment-validity|validity of course grades]], not a student-morality problem.

## Context: Two Changes That Compound

Thirty years ago a biology grade rested on a few high-stakes, in-person proctored exams that made dishonesty difficult and risky. Following national calls to replace high-stakes testing with multiple low-stakes formative assessments (AAAS's *Vision and Change*, Bransford et al., 2000), grades were rebuilt around frequent assessment, and worries about cheating were set aside. The authors call this the pre-GenAI assumption and test whether it survives. Two changes compound it: the pandemic-era normalization of remote delivery, which moved assessment outside the classroom; and ubiquitous [[generative-ai|generative AI]], which makes accurate answers easy to obtain while remaining hard to detect, lowering the perceived risk that earlier deterrents relied on.

## Design: Syllabi Plus Instructor Perception

A large research-intensive US institution, whose online and in-person biology degrees are treated as equivalent, requested the study. First, the team collected a syllabus for **every offering of all four courses in one 2025–2026 semester (38 syllabi, 100% collection rate)** and coded each for graded categories and their point shares, sorted by class setting, proctoring and stakes into five: outside-of-class assignments, in-class assignments, unproctored and proctored outside-of-class exams, and proctored in-person exams. Lab components (15–40% of the grade) were excluded because syllabi did not disclose their breakdown. Second, the chair surveyed every instructor who taught any biology course that year, and **56 responded (47%)**, each rating the largest course they taught from minimally vulnerable (0) to highly vulnerable (4); differences were tested with a Kruskal–Wallis test (χ²(4) = 67.4, p < .001) with pairwise Wilcoxon tests.

## How the Grade Is Weighted

Among the **21 in-person syllabi**, all included outside-of-class assignments worth a mean of **26.6% (SD 9.1)** and 90.5% included in-class assignments at **14.6% (SD 6.9)**. In-person exams appeared in 52.4% of courses at **66.5% (SD 14.8)**, proctored outside-of-class exams in 38.1% at **54.2% (SD 2.4)**, and unproctored outside-of-class exams in 9.5% at **50% (SD 14.1)**. Among the **17 online syllabi**, every course had outside-of-class assignments worth **40.8% (SD 7.8)** and proctored outside-of-class exams worth **59.2% (SD 7.8)**, and none used unproctored outside-of-class exams. More than half the syllabi (61%) came from courses enrolling 200 or more students and 45% were fully online, so the aggregate is weighted toward the largest [[online-teaching-and-learning|online]] and in-person offerings.

## What Instructors Consider Vulnerable

Ratings were pooled across modalities. Outside-of-class assignments were **median 4 (highly vulnerable)**, proctored and unproctored outside-of-class exams both **median 3**, in-class assignments **median 2** with the widest disagreement, and in-person exams **median 0 (minimally vulnerable)**, the only category seen as essentially protected. In-person exams were significantly less vulnerable than every other category (p_adj < .01), and outside-of-class assignments significantly more vulnerable than in-class assignments (p_adj < .01); instructors read this as in-person proctoring's relative effectiveness rather than immunity. Proctored and unproctored outside-of-class exams were rated **equally vulnerable**, showing no perceived benefit from lockdown browsers. Prior literature is mixed, and those tools carry documented costs: anxiety, privacy concerns, technical problems and false accusations.

## How Much of a Grade Is Exposed

Combining weights with ratings: conservatively, treating only the highly vulnerable category, **an average of 33% of course points were highly vulnerable: 27% in person and 41% online.** Less conservatively, counting the four categories rated more vulnerable than in-person exams, **an average of 81% of course points were at least somewhat vulnerable — about 65% in person and 100% online.** This is not a claim that students are cheating, only that this share of the grade could be completed dishonestly. Because instructors retain [[agency|autonomy]] over grading, offerings differ widely: one in-person section had 10% of its points highly vulnerable, another 33%. A student earning a C on exams while cheating to an A on all highly vulnerable work would finish at a C in the first but a B in the second.

## Key Findings

1. **Only in-person proctored exams were perceived as minimally vulnerable (median 0)**; every other category was at least somewhat vulnerable (χ²(4) = 67.4, p < .001).
2. **Outside-of-class assignments were perceived as highly vulnerable (median 4)** and significantly more vulnerable than in-class assignments (p_adj < .01).
3. **Proctoring outside class made no perceived difference**: proctored and unproctored outside-of-class exams both rated median 3.
4. **A third of the grade was highly vulnerable and 81% at least somewhat vulnerable** — 27% versus 41% highly vulnerable in person versus online.
5. **Identical performance can earn different grades in different sections of the same course**, because point weights for vulnerable work sit with instructor autonomy.
6. **The authors locate the problem in grading structure, not student character**, calling for [[assessment|assessment design]] that preserves grade validity and departmental responsibility.

## Recommendations and Their Trade-offs

The proposals are graded by severity, each paired with its cost. First, **shift points from vulnerable low-stakes assessments toward work that more reliably reflects learning, such as proctored in-class work** — though removing the incentive can remove the reason engaged students did the work. Next, **ungrading** uncouples assignments from points entirely, which they theorize reduces stress and supports [[motivation|intrinsic motivation]], though a pressed student can still do low-stakes work with AI and no real cognitive effort. Eliminating outside-of-class assignments is the clearest trade-off: it removes the incentive for dishonest completion but also for honest effort. Their preferred direction keeps incentives while **enhancing proctoring and accountability**, easier in person and in smaller classes. Why do instructors keep using categories they call highly vulnerable? They point to structure: **inertia**, where [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] change is not incentivized; **lack of time or knowledge**, with [[multimodal]], more accurate models and free institutional access both arriving in September 2024; and **resistance when reform conflicts with prior best practice**, since redesigning away from graded outside-of-class work may mean giving up flipped classrooms and [[active-learning|active learning]].

## What the design does establish

The design does establish a cheap, replicable and diagnostic method: code each syllabus's graded categories by class setting, proctoring and stakes, then multiply by instructor-perceived vulnerability for a per-course exposure figure — no new data collection, and a number a [[curriculum-design|curriculum]] committee can act on. It travels beyond [[biology-education|biology]] to most of undergraduate [[stem-education|STEM]], and invites comparison with [[summative-assessment|summative redesign]] that moves certified weight onto supervised, process-visible work ([[ai-particle-physics-education-redesign-2026]]) and with the computing-education finding that redesign, not detection, is where the evidence sits ([[kumar-genai-computing-education-systematic-review-2026]]).

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Compute your course's exposure before redesigning: multiply each graded category's point weight by instructor-rated vulnerability — the procedure behind the 33% and 81% figures in these 38 syllabi.
- **Instructors.** Stop treating [[remote-proctoring|proctoring]] as the fix for take-home exams: proctored and unproctored outside-of-class exams were rated equally vulnerable (median 3); only in-person proctored exams came in at median 0.
- **Assessment designers.** Rebalance points away from vulnerable low-stakes work rather than abolishing it, and name the trade-off: removing the incentive also removes the reason engaged students did it.
- **Administrators.** Treat [[assessment-validity|grade validity]] as a departmental responsibility — audit every offering's syllabus, and use circulating assistants for in-class accountability where course size (61% enrolling 200 or more) makes monitoring impossible.
- **Researchers.** Replicate the syllabus-audit-plus-perception method in other departments, disciplines and terms: one department in one term can only predict, not demonstrate, how widespread the exposure is.

## Limitations

- **One department, one institution, one term.** As a case study it cannot speak to generalizability, only predict that the problem is widespread.
- **Perceptions, not observed dishonesty.** The vulnerability figures rest on instructor estimates of exposure, so they measure the size of the opportunity rather than its use.
- **Lecture components only.** The syllabus analysis excludes lab work that constituted 15-40% of the grade in some courses, so the percentages cover the codable graded portion.
- **Weighted toward the largest offerings.** The sample skews to large courses (61% enrolling 200+) and online sections (45%), where vulnerability concentrates, so the aggregate describes the largest offerings, not a typical small seminar.
