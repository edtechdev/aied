> **Synthesis:** Chowdhury and Khan (2026) argue that the proliferation of [[generative-ai|generative AI]] — and of [[llm|large language models]] in particular — has broken the "output-as-evidence" model that has silently underwritten [[higher-ed|higher education]] [[assessment]] for a century: the assumption that a student's artifact (an essay, a proof, or a software script) is a direct reflection of their cognitive labor. Drawing on a university-level analysis and empirical survey data, they contend the core problem is not merely [[academic-integrity|academic dishonesty]] but a deeper misalignment between assessment practices and the [[learning-gains|learning outcomes]] they are meant to measure — regimes built on artificial constraints risk measuring compliance, access, or concealment rather than genuine understanding. The paper proposes [[authentic-assessment|alternative assessment models]] that emphasize process over product, aiming to preserve [[agency|student agency]] and accountability in an automated age.

## Key Findings
1. The "output-as-evidence" model of assessment historically served as a practical, scalable way to certify competence and signal employability, but it rests on a "proportional effort" assumption that LLMs have shattered.
2. The problem extends beyond academic dishonesty to a structural misalignment: evaluation regimes that rely on artificial constraints (proctoring, detection, timed conditions) risk measuring compliance, access, or concealment instead of reasoning and judgment.
3. Institutions' typical responses — detection tools and restrictions — are ethically fraught and address the symptom rather than the cause of outcome leakage.
4. The authors advocate shifting the evaluative weight from the finished product to the [[student-engagement|process]] of learning, preserving student agency and accountability while recognizing that competence can no longer be inferred from easily-outsourced artifacts.

## Discussion
The paper reframes the AI-era assessment crisis as an [[ethics|ethical]] design problem rather than a purely technical one. Where much of the literature focuses on [[plagiarism-detection|detection]] and [[automated-assessment|automated scoring]], Chowdhury and Khan foreground the epistemic legitimacy of evaluation itself: what counts as evidence of learning when the artifact no longer requires the cognitive struggle it once indexed? Their proposed turn toward process-based and [[authentic-assessment|authentic]] assessment echoes the broader [[ai-literacy|critical AI literacy]] agenda, positioning the [[teacher-role|educator]] as a designer of evaluation experiences rather than a gatekeeper of outputs. The survey data grounds these normative arguments in [[student-experience|student experience]], showing the gulf between [[governance|institutional]] rules and how learners actually navigate AI-assisted work.
