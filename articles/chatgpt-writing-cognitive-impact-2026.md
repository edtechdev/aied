> **Synthesis:** Wagner-Kobayashi (2026) asks whether [[generative-ai|ChatGPT]] used inside a [[writing-education|writing-to-learn]] task helps students learn the material they are writing about. The writing-to-learn literature holds that composing with organization and elaboration prompts builds knowledge, and ChatGPT can supply examples and conceptual connections on demand, so the study's hypothesis (H1) was that a ChatGPT-assisted group would show a steeper pre-to-post knowledge gain than a control group. In a laboratory experiment, 35 German-speaking psychology students (n = 16 with ChatGPT, n = 19 without, after five exclusions from forty) spent 20 minutes elaborating an input text, with GPT-3.5 available only to the experimental group, and took an identical knowledge test before and after. The group × time interaction was significant (F(70) = 5.889, p = .018) but pointed the other way: the no-ChatGPT group gained more (posttest M = 8.29 vs 6.94), with an estimated coefficient of b = −1.450 against the hypothesis. Motivation (p = .009) and interest (p = .017) interacted with group, so students low in motivation or topic interest were the ones disadvantaged by ChatGPT. Takeaway: untrained, time-pressured ChatGPT use did not benefit learning and may have harmed it.

## Key Findings

1. **The hypothesis was falsified in direction, not for absence of an effect.** The predicted group × time interaction on knowledge-test performance was significant (F(70) = 5.889, p = .018), but the estimated coefficient b(group 1 | posttest) = −1.450 (reference: control group at posttest) showed that participants writing without ChatGPT achieved the steeper learning slope.
2. **The descriptive means make the reversal concrete.** On the sum-scored knowledge test the experimental group moved from M = 1.50 (SD = 1.85) at pretest to M = 6.94 (SD = 2.34) at posttest, while the control group moved from M = 0.79 (SD = 0.90) to M = 8.29 (SD = 1.95) — a larger absolute gain despite starting lower.
3. **Motivation and interest mattered only for ChatGPT users.** Neither variable produced a three-way group × time × covariable interaction (motivation F(70) = .194, p = .661; interest F(70) = 1.958, p = .166), but both entered significant group × covariable interactions (motivation F(70) = 7.183, p = .009, b = .618, t = 2.207, p = .031; interest F(70) = 6.027, p = .017, b = .665, t = 2.725, p = .008), meaning higher motivation or interest predicted a larger pre-to-post gain only inside the ChatGPT group.
4. **Including motivation removed the main effect.** Once motivation was added to the model, the group × time interaction lost significance (F(70) = 1.187, p = .280), indicating that pre-existing differences in motivation — which were higher in the ChatGPT group (M = 8.56 vs 7.26; t(68) = 2.886, p = .005, d = .697) — carry variance previously attributed to ChatGPT use; nested comparisons favored the motivation model (χ²(3) = 12.21, p < .01) and the interest model (χ²(3) = 10.91, p < .02).
5. **Nothing else predicted the effect.** Concentration, number of examples generated, number of connections generated, [[self-efficacy]] for [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulation]] of academic writing, and writing belief showed no significant influence on the main effect, and time remained significant (p < .001) in every model, so all participants learned something from the retest.
6. **Participants generated few elaborations, and especially few with ChatGPT.** The experimental group produced 2.94 examples and 1.81 connections in total, of which only 2.25 examples and 0.88 connections were generated in dialogue with ChatGPT; the authors read this as a utilization deficiency (Miller, 2000) — the tool was available but not deployed to elaborate.
7. **The authors attribute the reversal to shallow engagement under time pressure.** Participants reported focusing on quantity (hitting the word requirement) over quality, and the authors suggest ChatGPT users asked repeated questions and copied answers instead of searching for their own examples, consistent with the reduced mental effort and copy-paste behavior Kosmyna et al. (2025) observed.
8. **The finding is statistically fragile and the authors say so.** Residuals deviated from normality (Shapiro-Wilk W = 0.96, p = .004), Levene's test was significant for the motivation and interest models (t(15, 42) = 2.874, p = .004; t(13, 34) = 2.535, p = .015), the level-two sample fell short of the recommended N = 50, and the effect is described as small, so the standard error may be underestimated.

## Why a benefit was expected: writing-to-learn theory

The study starts from the claim that writing is not only output but a mode of learning: Emig's (1977) "writing as a learning mode" and the [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|meta-analytic]] evidence of Graham et al. (2020) and van Dijk et al. (2022) ground the prediction that composing about content improves knowledge of that content. The mechanism the authors target is deliberate [[metacognition|elaboration]] — generating examples and connections — supported by cognitive prompts of the kind Nückles and colleagues developed ("which examples illustrate, confirm or conflict with this content?"). [[cognitive-psychology|Cognitive accounts]] of [[writing-education|writing]] hold that this organization and elaboration work is what converts reading into durable knowledge, and Berthold, Nückles and Renkl (2007) supplied the large within-subjects effect size the design was powered around.

ChatGPT appeared to fit that mechanism almost perfectly. As the paper puts it, the tool can break complex information into understandable components, generate examples and comparisons, and make relationships between concepts explorable — the very elaborative moves the prompts request — while doing so in a time-efficient way. On that reading, [[llm|large language models]] should amplify a writing-to-learn task rather than displace it. The authors counterbalance this with the "double-edged sword" framing: concerns about [[academic-integrity|integrity]], accuracy, [[privacy]], erosion of higher-order thinking, and worsening inequality for lower-skilled [[learners]], plus Kosmyna et al.'s (2025) finding that external support tools "restructure not only task performance but also the underlying cognitive architecture". The design question they pose is therefore whether time efficiency bought with ChatGPT comes at the cost of meaningful knowledge construction. Their stated prediction, conditional on prompts being used to amplify the tool's potential, was yes for learning: writing with ChatGPT should raise learning performance.

## Design, task, and how ChatGPT use was logged

The design is a between-subjects laboratory experiment run in an experimental room in Frankfurt between late January and mid-March 2024 — 17 sessions across nine weeks, one to three participants at a time, nearly all weekday mornings between 10 and 11 a.m. Participants were recruited for a study advertised as "digital writing-to-learn" with no mention of ChatGPT, so that the control group would not adjust its effort, and sessions alternated conditions to approximate randomization. Only psychology students with German as a mother tongue were admitted, to avoid confounding discipline and language with writing-to-learn effects. Of the 40 recruited (20 per condition), five were excluded: three who never used ChatGPT to generate connections or examples, one who used it to write the full text, and one outlier who scored zero on both tests and spent only 12 minutes on a 20-minute task. The analysis sample was 35 (n = 16 experimental, n = 19 control), mean age 22.37 (SD = 2.45), 27 women (77.14%).

The task sequence was a demographic [[self-report-measures|questionnaire]], the knowledge pretest, a measure of self-efficacy for self-regulation of academic writing, then 20 minutes of writing based on an input text (a modified extract from Knaus, 2011) with the instruction to elaborate its main points by generating examples and connections. The experimental group had access to [[conversational-ai|ChatGPT]] (gpt-3.5-turbo-1106, reached through the [[higher-ed|university's]] IP registration) on a second browser tab and was told which uses were recommended; copying the writing instruction wholesale into the tool and submitting its complete output was forbidden, but how the outputs were otherwise used was left free, and every chat was stored locally only. A logging tool, InputLog (Leijten & van Waes, 2013), ran in the background (the keystroke data were not analyzed in this paper), writing time was recorded separately, and participants then rated motivation, interest and concentration and completed a manipulation check. The same knowledge test — ten open-answer questions, content-analyzed into sum scores and treated as the operationalization of [[learning-gains|learning performance]] — was administered again without ChatGPT before debriefing. Reliability of the content analysis was established on five randomly selected test sets rated by two raters (29 of 50 answers in exact agreement, 19 within 0.5 points, 2 within 1 point); the remaining 800 answers were scored by the researcher alone. Analysis used repeated-measures hierarchical linear models (RM-HLM) in SPSS 30 with person ID as a random effect and group and time as fixed effects, with one covariable added per model.

## Results: the benefit that did not appear

Both groups learned — time was a significant predictor (p < .001) in every model — but the ChatGPT group learned less. The confirmatory RM-HLM on the null model found the hypothesized interaction, F(70) = 5.889, p = .018, and then contradicted it: with the control group at posttest as the reference, b(group 1 | posttest) = −1.450, so the steeper slope belonged to the participants who wrote unaided. Descriptively, the control group rose from M = 0.79 (SD = 0.90) to M = 8.29 (SD = 1.95) on the knowledge-test sum, the experimental group from M = 1.50 (SD = 1.85) to M = 6.94 (SD = 2.34). The exploratory models added one covariable each; none produced the three-way group × time × covariable interaction that would indicate moderation, but two produced a pattern the authors consider the paper's most interesting secondary result.

For motivation, the three-way term was not significant (F(70) = .194, p = .661), yet adding motivation to the model made the group × time interaction non-significant (F(70) = 1.187, p = .280) while group (F(70) = 9.352, p = .003), motivation (F(70) = 7.451, p = .008) and their interaction (F(70) = 7.183, p = .009) became significant, with b(group 1 | motivation) = .618, t = 2.207, p = .031. Motivation was also higher in the ChatGPT group to begin with (M = 8.56, SD = 1.82 vs M = 7.26, SD = 2.18; t(68) = 2.886, p = .005, d = .697). Interest followed the same attenuated shape: the three-way term was non-significant (F(70) = 1.958, p = .166), the group × time interaction survived at F(70) = 5.064, p = .028, group × interest reached F(70) = 6.027, p = .017 with b = .665, t = 2.725, p = .008, and interest itself only approached significance (F(70) = 2.780, p = .100) with a non-significant group difference (t(68) = 1.718, p = .090, d = .415). Motivation and interest correlated at r = .805, which the authors flag as evidence that neither single-item measure cleanly separates the two constructs. Read together, the pattern says that motivation and interest were protective precisely for the group whose task carried the extra demands of using the tool — the authors invoke volition research (Heckhausen, 1991) to suggest motivation shielded ChatGPT users from the additional cognitive demands of the assisted condition, so low-motivation or low-interest students were the ones for whom ChatGPT "at least aggravated" learning.

The paper's own explanations for the reversal are behavioral. Participants later reported that time pressure shifted their focus to quantity — producing the required words — rather than depth of engagement with the content; the authors suggest ChatGPT users asked the same questions repeatedly and pasted answers instead of actively seeking their own examples and connections to [[prior-knowledge|prior knowledge]], and they read the low elaboration counts (2.94 examples and 1.81 connections generated in total, of which only 2.25 examples and 0.88 connections involved ChatGPT) as a utilization deficiency, the lag between having a new instrument and knowing how to deploy it. They also raise cognitive overload: negotiating the procedural integration of generated content into a coherent essay may have displaced processing of the content itself. The findings converge with Gayed et al.'s (2022) null result for an AI writing assistant and with Kosmyna et al.'s (2025) account of reduced mental effort and increased copy-paste among essay writers using ChatGPT. The authors immediately qualify the result: the effect is small, residuals were non-normal, and the standard error may therefore be larger than estimated.

## Effort, ownership, and what this means for cognitive offloading

The study is a causal test of an [[cognitive-offloading|offloading]] prediction inside a single writing task, and its negative result is specific rather than sweeping. What leaked away in the ChatGPT condition was elaborative effort — generating one's own [[metacognition|examples and connections]] — which is the activity the writing-to-learn literature identifies as the source of the learning, so the displacement lands on the graded target skill rather than on incidental work. The authors' utilization-deficiency reading cuts the other way, though: they do not claim ChatGPT cannot support [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]] or knowledge construction, only that it did not here, without training and under a 20-minute limit, which leaves open whether the tool is inert or simply mis-deployed. Their recommendation is therefore [[scaffolding]] in the form of training sessions and clear deployment instruction rather than prohibition, alongside caution about assigning ChatGPT tasks in large lectures where [[motivation|intrinsic motivation]] and topic interest vary widely.

Two features of the design limit how far the offloading reading can be pushed. First, the group that used ChatGPT was the *more* motivated one, and its disadvantage survived that advantage only partially once motivation entered the model — the group × time effect collapsed, and the moderation evidence sits in interactions rather than in a clean three-way term. Second, participants were explicitly forbidden from outsourcing the whole text, and the excluded participant who tried to do exactly that shows the rule was enforced; the harm occurred in a configuration where the tool was meant to assist elaboration, which is the configuration most writing [[pedagogy]] recommends. The paper's own conclusion is correspondingly narrow: "under time restrictions and with no training in how to leverage ChatGPT effectively, the use of ChatGPT as an elaboration assistant does not benefit learning or may even harm learning if the motivation or interest in the topic is low."

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Teach the tool before you assign it: the experimental group generated only 2.25 examples and 0.88 connections in dialogue with ChatGPT out of 2.94 and 1.81 overall, which the authors read as a utilization deficiency — the tool was available but not deployed to elaborate.
- **Instructors.** Protect elaboration time explicitly, because students under pressure trade depth for completion: participants reported focusing on hitting the word requirement in the 20-minute task, and ChatGPT users asked repeated questions and pasted answers instead of seeking their own examples.
- **Learning designers.** Require a visible product of student-generated elaboration — the examples and connections students produce themselves — since that is the activity the writing-to-learn literature credits with the learning and the one the assisted condition displaced.
- **Instructors.** Front-load motivation and topic-interest support whenever a task carries an AI tool: higher motivation or interest predicted a steeper gain only inside the ChatGPT group (p = .009 and p = .017), and the ChatGPT group started higher on motivation (M = 8.56 vs 7.26) yet gained less.
- **Instructors.** Avoid ChatGPT tasks in large lectures unless you can check how students used the tool, because the disadvantage concentrated in students low in motivation or topic interest and the authors recommend caution where intrinsic motivation varies widely.

## Limitations

The authors list their limitations in unusual detail. The sample of 35 is small, psychology-only, and demographically uneven across conditions despite session-level alternating; the authors call the randomization suboptimal because there was no counterbalancing or participant matching, which raises internal-validity questions. Several RM-HLM assumptions were violated — non-normal residuals, heterogeneity of variance in two models, and a level-two N below the recommended 50 — which they note leads to underestimated standard errors even though hierarchical linear models are relatively robust; floor effects at pretest (means of 0.79 and 1.50 out of a 12-point range) plausibly explain part of that pattern. Ecological validity is questionable: an artificial exam-like setting, unfamiliar desktops, and a single 20-minute writing episode do not resemble real study conditions, and the literature the authors cite finds ChatGPT's positive effects on academic performance only in traditional classroom settings and over durations longer than a week. The manipulation itself was imperfect, since 3 of 20 ChatGPT-group participants never used the tool at all, hinting at attrition related to high writing self-efficacy or unfamiliarity with the platform, and the low number of ChatGPT-generated examples and connections indicates that training could have changed the result. Materials are also thin relative to semester-long learning-protocol studies, the input text may have been too little, and the writing genre differs from the genres used in writing-to-learn research. Finally, the knowledge test was constructed for the study and never validated, the content analysis was completed largely by one rater, and the test mainly captures lower levels of Bloom's taxonomy, so the authors cannot rule out that the ChatGPT group processed the content more deeply but could not express it on an abstract, text-bound test; Graham et al. (2020) find larger writing-to-learn effects when learning is assessed with higher-order skills. Their recommendations are a larger and more diverse sample, an input text usable across disciplines, a validated knowledge test, robust motivation and interest instruments such as SELLMO and the full Writing Belief Inventory, engagement rather than motivation as the candidate mediator, a peer-feedback control group, and explicit attention to which layer of the writing process AI attaches to.
