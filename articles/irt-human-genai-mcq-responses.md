> **Synthesis:** Strugatski and Alexandron (2026) established a theoretical and empirical foundation for using [[item-response-theory|Item Response Theory]] to separate [[generative-ai|generative AI]] and human responses to multiple-choice assessments. They investigated whether person-fit statistics (PFS), which evaluate how well an examinee's response pattern fits IRT model expectations, can distinguish between GenAI and human responses. Using data from two [[authentic-assessment|authentic assessment]] contexts—a high-school chemistry test and a national university entrance exam, each with roughly 1000 human respondents—they demonstrated that PFS reveal significant differences between human responses and those generated by advanced chatbots, which appear as 'aberrant' responders. The study positions IRT as a robust framework for characterizing and separating human and GenAI response patterns, supporting [[academic-integrity]] in high-stakes testing.

## Key Findings

- Person-fit statistics reveal significant differences between human responses and those generated by advanced chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini), which appear as 'aberrant' responders.
- Different chatbots present significantly different response patterns, so they should be treated as a heterogeneous group of 'intelligences' rather than a single one.
- Newer GenAI versions not only improve in performance but also become more 'human-like' in their response patterns, complicating detection.
- The work positions IRT as a robust framework for characterizing and separating human and GenAI response patterns in MCQ assessments.
