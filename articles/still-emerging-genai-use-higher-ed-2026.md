> **Synthesis:** Kahn and colleagues (2026) report a cross-sectional survey within one UK research-intensive university school exploring how staff and students perceive their own responses to [[llm|Large Language Model]] (LLM) tools. Fielded April–June 2024, the study found that appropriate uses of GAI tools were not straightforwardly obvious to participants, with staff showing low subscription uptake and limited knowledge of student use, while students were more positive about likely gains and perceived stronger [[framing-ai-use-for-students|facilitating conditions]]. The authors argue the challenges posed by GAI for [[higher-ed|higher education]] cannot be reduced to a technical challenge for [[learning-design|learning design]] or [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] training, since effective use hinges on how both staff and students *imagine* the [[generative-ai|affordances]] of the tools.

## Key Findings

- **Modest, uneven adoption with a staff–student subscription gap.** 63% of staff respondents had used at least one GAI tool in the six months to May 2024, yet only 14% had taken out a paid subscription; by contrast, student uptake of paid subscriptions was higher (21%). The authors treat a subscription as a proxy for sustained engagement, suggesting most staff were working with only partially imagined affordances.
- **Students perceived stronger facilitating conditions.** A validated Facilitating Conditions scale showed students perceiving greater support than staff (mean 4.94 vs 4.15; t-test p = 0.001), consistent with student access to subscription tools.
- **Students were more positive about performance gains.** Student performance expectancy (mean 5.0) exceeded staff (mean 3.9), with student responses skewed positively; the items suggest [[personalized-learning|workload reduction]] and productivity were not straightforwardly in view for many staff.
- **Staff held more negative attitudes than students.** Staff scored higher on the Negative Attitude scale than students (4.93 vs 4.10; p < 0.001), reflecting staff concerns about student use of [[generative-ai|GAI]] for academic purposes.
- **A knowledge gap about student use.** Staff believed students used GAI for academic purposes substantially more than students reported (means 5.23 vs 3.11; p = 0.000) — highlighting the restricted social relations staff maintain with students, whose use often occurs during independent [[self-regulated-learning|learning]] outside staff view.
- **Students' consistent use was language and text production.** The items loading onto validated scales converged on using LLMs to find answers for assignments, generate written assignments, and translate or improve grammar — i.e., supporting language-usage and text production, the most immediately visible affordance of current LLMs.
- **Staff intention to use GAI was split.** Some staff intended to use GAI in research, teaching, and other work, others did not; the authors argue that disengaged staff are poorly placed to scaffold educationally appropriate student use.

## Study Design & Method

A [[qualitative-research|case study]] drawing on survey research method within a School of Social Science disciplines at a research-intensive UK university ([[higher-ed|University of Manchester]]). Two original online questionnaires were designed — one for students, one for academic staff — distributed via Qualtrics between April and June 2024 through social media, e-newsletters, email, and flyers. The instruments were built primarily on the [[technology-acceptance-model|Technology Acceptance Model]] and UTAUT, adapted for GAI with elements from the AIDUA and IAAAM models, and validated by a panel of seven experts (Aiken's V > 0.8). The staff questionnaire ran 11 dimensions and 48 items; the student questionnaire 9 dimensions and 40 items, both on 7-point Likert scales.

The study collected 45 complete staff responses from a population of 318 academics (plus 26 partial responses), with 64% of staff respondents having over ten years' experience in higher education; it collected 86 complete student responses (some items up to 116) from a population of around 3,400, 66% from postgraduate taught programmes. Analysis included internal consistency checks (Cronbach's alpha and indicator reliability ≥ 0.70) and t-tests comparing shared staff/student scales. [[ethics]] approval was granted by the University of Manchester Research Ethics Committee.

## Implications for AI in Education

- **Frame GAI challenges relationally, not as a technical fix.** The authors argue — echoing Bearman and Ajjawi — that [[pedagogy]] for an AI-mediated world involves working with [[explainable-ai|opaque, partial, and ambiguous]] situations, rather than treating the "assessment panic" as a problem solved by learning-design tweaks or more [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] training.
- **Staff must imagine how students imagine affordances.** Effective educational use depends on interrelationships between staff and students; educators' [[agency|agency]] and understanding of students' imagined affordances are central to [[scaffolding]] appropriate use.
- **Do not start from distrust.** Assuming students use GAI to circumvent assessment integrity begins from a position of distrust; the authors instead urge redesigning learning and assessment to account for GAI affordances, since the technology has already disseminated.
- **Reduce the staff knowledge gap.** Because staff cannot easily tell whether a student used a GAI tool from final text alone, institutions should strengthen the social relations through which staff understand student use, including independent learning.
- **Deepen TAM-style acceptance thinking.** The authors critique [[technology-acceptance-model|TAM]]'s "acceptance" framing as passive and thin for emerging [[ai-technologies|technologies]], since it cannot capture relational dynamics or the varied ways users imagine affordances.

## Limitations

- Small, single-institution sample with a low response rate; likely [[self-report-measures|self-selection]] bias in favour of staff already interested in GAI (some staff reportedly did not complete the survey because they felt they lacked knowledge).
- Skew toward highly experienced staff (64% with over ten years in higher education) and toward postgraduate students.
- Cross-sectional design cannot track adoption over time, and perceptions are by nature impressionistic, risking conflation of personal and technological factors in a rapidly changing tool landscape.
- Several scales failed validation and were excluded from findings; datasets were not released due to ethics constraints.
- The authors recommend future larger and more balanced samples, and qualitative research that richly describes students' perspectives, purpose, trust, and affectivity in LLM use.
