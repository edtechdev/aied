> **Synthesis:** Ismael, Luo, and Li (2026) test a 10-week AI-supported e-mentoring model with 50 second-year EFL ([[language-learning|English as a foreign language]]) pre-service teachers during their practicum in Egypt, combining structured human mentoring, collaborative digital platforms (Google Classroom, Nearpod, Facebook, Padlet) and AI-driven feedback via Gemini. The experimental group (n = 25) outperformed a conventional-practicum control group on both [[self-efficacy]] and trait [[social-emotional-learning|emotional intelligence]], with large effects, while interviews and weekly reflective logs show a move from control-oriented to student-centred, reflective practice. The authors frame AI as an augmentation of human mentoring rather than a replacement, and attribute the gains to an integrated ecosystem of reflection, peer collaboration and feedback.

## Key Findings

- **Self-efficacy improved far more in the AI-supported group.** TSES totals rose from 89.76 (SD = 10.12) to 112.40 (SD = 8.23) in the experimental group versus 88.92 (SD = 9.84) to 102.16 (SD = 9.12) in the control group, with a significant Time x Group interaction, F(1, 48) = 32.7, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .41 (between-group d = 1.04; within-group d = 1.84).
- **Gains spanned all three TSES subdomains.** The largest interaction effect was [[student-engagement|student engagement]] (partial eta-squared = .37), followed by classroom management (.33) and instructional practices (.29); classroom management, for instance, rose from 29.80 to 37.90 in the experimental group against 29.50 to 31.20 in the control group.
- **Trait emotional intelligence rose substantially.** TEIQue scores increased from 121.84 (SD = 11.36) to 135.60 (SD = 10.45) in the experimental group versus 120.96 (SD = 10.91) to 124.32 (SD = 11.08) in the control group (Time x Group F(1, 48) = 22.4, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .32; between-group d = 0.98, within-group d = 1.50).
- **Practical shift from moderate to high perceived efficacy.** Against the TSES total range of 24-120, the experimental group moved from a moderate to a high-efficacy profile, reported as greater confidence in managing disruptive behaviour, promoting engagement and applying [[pedagogy|instructional strategies]] independently.
- **[[qualitative-research|Qualitative]] data show changed pedagogy.** Four themes emerged: development of classroom management beliefs (from "making students silent" toward rapport-building), a shift toward student-centred engagement, growth in communicative and inductive instructional practices, and the combined development of self-efficacy and emotional intelligence.
- **Three mechanisms linked the outcomes** — continuous reflective practice, collaborative peer learning, and AI-supported instructional and emotional feedback — which the authors argue produced a mutually reinforcing effect on confidence and emotional [[regulation]] rather than two separate developmental tracks.

## Study Design & Method

An embedded [[mixed-methods-research|mixed-methods]] design within a quasi-experimental framework anchors the study to a [[higher-ed|higher-education]] [[teacher-education]] context. Participants were 50 second-year [[english-education|EFL]] pre-service teachers at the Faculty of Education, Ain Shams University (Egypt), in the first semester of 2025-2026, placed across 10 public schools with roughly five students each. Randomization was conducted at the **school** level using a simple lottery method: five schools (n = 25) to the experimental condition and five (n = 25) to the control condition. All participants were female, reflecting gender-segregated practicum placement; the authors report that female students made up about 80% of the English language cohort.

The experimental group received the AI-supported e-mentoring model across pre-practicum, during-practicum and post-practicum phases: Google Classroom for weekly tasks and resources, Nearpod for interactive content and lesson modelling, Facebook as a [[collaborative-learning|collaborative]] mentoring space for sharing teaching videos and peer [[feedback]], Padlet for anonymous reflection and scenario analysis, and Gemini for personalized, context-sensitive feedback. The control group followed standard practicum procedures — school-based supervision by the English department head and cooperating [[teacher-role|teacher]], informal oral feedback delivered as needed, and no structured online platforms or AI tools. The intervention ran 10 weeks for approximately 35 hours, aligned to the practicum schedule.

Outcome measures were the 24-item Teachers' Sense of Efficacy Scale (TSES; three subscales, 5-point Likert) and the 30-item Trait Emotional Intelligence [[self-report-measures|Questionnaire]] (TEIQue; 15 facets across four factors, 7-point Likert), administered pre- and post-intervention. Internal consistency was good to excellent (total TSES alpha .94 pre / .96 post; TEIQue alpha = .76, test-retest r = .71). Baseline equivalence was established: t(48) = 0.30, p = .76 for self-efficacy and t(48) = 0.28, p = .78 for emotional intelligence. Effects were tested with 2 (Group) x 2 (Time) mixed ANOVAs, and the qualitative strand (pre- and post-intervention semi-structured interviews plus weekly reflective inputs) was analysed thematically with a hybrid coding approach and inter-coder agreement of kappa = .84. Because the sample was determined by availability rather than a priori power analysis, a sensitivity analysis (G*Power 3.1) indicated a minimum detectable effect of d = 0.81 for N = 50.

Interview data (pre- and post-intervention) include statements such as not wanting to become a teacher out of fear of classroom challenges, alongside later reports of reduced [[ai-anxiety-and-stress|anxiety]] — a shift the authors read as emerging emotional regulation supported by guided reflection.

The theoretical [[scaffolding]] combines Bandura's Social Cognitive Theory (mastery and vicarious experiences, social persuasion, emotional states), a Hattie and Timperley [[feedback]] division of labour in which AI works at the task and process levels while human mentors address [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulation]] and self-level concerns, and a trait-based rather than ability-based conception of emotional intelligence. AI is explicitly positioned as augmenting, not replacing, the human mentor.

## Implications for AI in Education

- **[[teacher-education|Teacher education]] can treat AI-supported mentoring as continuous infrastructure, not an add-on tool.** The authors stress that effectiveness came from combining structured mentoring, collaborative digital interaction, reflective practice and AI-assisted support into a single ecosystem, which is what bridged the theory-practice gap during the practicum.
- **Confidence and emotional competence develop together.** The reciprocal pattern — increased teaching confidence enabling better stress management, and greater emotional awareness sustaining persistence — suggests teacher preparation should address cognitive and affective dimensions jointly rather than in separate tracks.
- **Feedback roles can be deliberately divided.** Assigning AI task- and process-level feedback while reserving self-regulation and identity-level work for human mentors is a concrete design rule for [[human-in-the-loop-ai|human-in-the-loop]] mentoring at scale.
- **Scalable support where mentoring is thin.** The paper targets under-resourced contexts with irregular, evaluation-focused supervision, and argues the model offers scalable, context-sensitive [[educational-development|professional development]]; it also highlights the [[equity-in-ai-education|equity]] issue of limited mentoring resources, including in [[global-south|Global South]] settings.
- **AI literacy is entangled with emotional literacy.** Because generative tools such as [[generative-ai|Gemini]] were used to reframe emotionally charged classroom situations and design communicative activities, [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] here includes the judgment to use AI suggestions reflectively rather than as verdicts.

## Limitations

- **Small, single-institution sample** of 50 pre-service teachers, limiting generalizability; the size did not permit a robust [[summative-assessment|examination]] of instrument structural validity (e.g. confirmatory factor analysis).
- **Adequately powered only for large effects.** The sensitivity analysis put the minimum detectable effect at d = 0.81, so smaller but meaningful effects may have gone undetected.
- **All participants were female**, a consequence of gender-segregated school placements and the ~80% female English cohort; gender representation and cross-context generalizability are limited.
- **Clustering not modelled.** Randomization at the school level means participants within a school are not fully independent; this clustering was not modelled statistically, and multilevel modelling is recommended for future work with more schools.
- **Short, single-semester intervention** restricts claims about long-term [[sustainability]] of gains past the practicum.
- **Causal attribution is bounded.** The intervention was multi-component, so the authors caution that improvements cannot be attributed solely to AI tools; the control condition also differed in being face-to-face, less structured, and without reflective cycles.
- **Data richness constraints.** Some participants were initially hesitant to record or document classroom activities, slightly limiting the observational and reflective data.
- **Stage-specific population.** Findings concern second-year pre-service teachers at the preparatory stage and may not transfer to more advanced or in-service teachers.
