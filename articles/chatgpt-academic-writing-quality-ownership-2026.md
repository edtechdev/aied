> **Synthesis:** Bauer, Fütterer, Brucker, and Gerjets (2026) test whether ChatGPT promotes students' academic writing skills — especially when writing without ChatGPT after having used it. In an experimental setup, first-year fashion design students engaged in three in-class writing exercises: composing introductions to scientific papers first without AI, second with ChatGPT, and third again without assistance. ChatGPT's use produced statistically significant improvements in writing quality both during and immediately after its use, improving text quality (examiner ratings, readability, cohesion) and subjective writing experience (ease, perceived control, cognitive load). Notably, while students' sense of authorship was reduced during ChatGPT use, their perceived ownership over their work increased in the third exercise, suggesting a nuanced relationship between authorship and ownership.

## Key Findings

**Writing quality gains.** ChatGPT statistically significantly improved students' writing quality both during use and immediately after, including examiner ratings, readability, and cohesion.

**Transfer to writing without AI.** Benefits persisted when students wrote again without AI assistance, suggesting skill acquisition rather than mere output dependence.

**Improved writing experience.** ChatGPT improved subjective writing experience — ease, perceived control, and reduced cognitive load.

**Authorship vs. ownership.** While students' sense of authorship was reduced during ChatGPT use, their perceived ownership over their work increased afterward — a nuanced distinction relevant to the wiki's [[agency]] and [[cognitive-offloading]] concepts.

**Implication.** In-class AI writing support can enhance learning and skill acquisition in [[higher-ed]], but the authorship–ownership tension requires careful pedagogical design.
