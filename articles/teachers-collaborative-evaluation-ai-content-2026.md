> **Synthesis:** This study investigates how [[k-12|middle-school]] science teachers evaluate [[generative-ai|AI-generated]] assessment questions and how they perceive the role of collaborative evaluation in [[educational-development|professional development]]. Sixty science teachers reviewed open-ended questions on lower- and higher-order thinking skills produced by ChatGPT, rating them through their disciplinary, [[pedagogy|pedagogical]], and [[curriculum-design|curricular]] judgment. The [[mixed-methods-research|mixed-methods]] findings show that collaborative evaluation helps [[teacher-role|teachers]] apply conceptual-precision judgment to AI content and surface risks such as reinforcing [[misconceptions]]. The paper underscores the value of [[teacher-education|professional development]] that positions teachers as critical evaluators of AI-generated material rather than passive consumers.

## Background

As [[generative-ai|GenAI]] becomes integrated into education, AI-generated content risks reinforcing misconceptions and propagating imprecise reasoning — particularly in disciplines that demand conceptual precision. [[formative-assessment|Question writing]] for assessment therefore requires critical evaluation by educators, but little is known about how teachers exercise that judgment collectively.

## Key Findings

1. Sixty middle-school science teachers reviewed ChatGPT-produced questions spanning lower-order and higher-order thinking skills.
2. Teachers drew on disciplinary, pedagogical, and curricular judgment to rate AI-generated content, applying conceptual-precision criteria.
3. Collaborative evaluation emerged as valuable [[teacher-education|professional development]], helping teachers surface and address the risk that AI content reinforces misconceptions.
4. A mixed-methods design combined [[quantitative-research|quantitative]] ratings with [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] insights on teachers' perceptions.
5. The findings position teachers as critical evaluators of AI material, supporting [[critical-thinking|critical]] [[student-engagement|engagement]] with [[generative-ai|GenAI]] in the classroom.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Have discipline-specific groups review AI-generated questions against stated criteria — scientific accuracy, linguistic clarity, and curriculum alignment — before they reach students. Teachers in this study caught subtle errors in items that read as scientifically correct, such as attributing a swing's behavior on the International Space Station to air resistance rather than microgravity.
- **Instructors.** Treat AI output as a draft for revision: teachers rated higher-order questions lowest on curriculum alignment (M = 3.47, SD = 0.92) and described them as conceptually ambiguous, so plan to rewrite or replace those items rather than assigning them as generated.
- **Instructors.** Screen for syntactic accessibility separately from factual accuracy: teachers rated a question 4 out of 5 for clarity while still warning that embedded clauses and abstract phrasing would challenge middle-school readers.
- **Faculty developers.** Make the evaluation a structured workshop task rather than a demonstration: mixed groups of three to four novice and veteran teachers, working from a disciplinary text and a fixed prompt with a shared rating form, produced specific and shared critiques.
- **Faculty developers.** Withhold AI tools during the critique phase so judgments rest on disciplinary, pedagogical, and curricular knowledge; this workshop barred external resources and GenAI while teachers evaluated the items.

## Limitations

- Sixty middle-school science teachers from eight schools in one district, all taking part in a single 90-minute workshop within one year-long PD seminar; the authors caution that the lower-order versus higher-order differences should be interpreted cautiously given the exploratory sample.
- Each discipline-specific group of three to four teachers evaluated only one lower-order and one higher-order question, so the observed rating pattern rests on a very small stimulus set.
- All items were generated by a single model — the free browser version of ChatGPT-4o — under one standardized prompt, tying the findings to that model's behavior at that moment.
- Ratings and reflections were collected at group level, teachers knew the content was AI-generated, inviting evaluation bias and group dynamics, and no pre/post measure of evaluative skill was taken, so the study documents perceptions rather than demonstrated evaluation ability.
