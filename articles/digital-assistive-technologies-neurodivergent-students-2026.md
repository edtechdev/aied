> **Synthesis:** This [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|scoping review]] maps a decade of research on digital [[assistive-technology|assistive technologies]] for [[neurodiversity|neurodivergent]] students in [[higher-ed|higher education]], screening 766 records from five databases down to 40 included studies published between 2015 and 2025. The field has visibly reorganised around [[generative-ai]], which appears in 15 of the 40 studies, with [[virtual-and-augmented-reality|virtual reality]] forming a second dominant strand (n = 11). The authors' central finding is a design critique rather than a technology inventory: the literature is dominated by individual accommodations and neurotype-specific tools, organised around formal diagnosis, when the functional barriers it actually addresses — reading and writing, study management, attention, social communication — cut across neurotypes. They argue for [[universal-design-for-learning|universal design]] and participatory development, and flag the [[equity-in-ai-education|equity]] cost of a field concentrated in the Global North whose most immersive tools remain the least scalable.

## Key Findings

1. Searches across Scopus, Web of Science, ERIC, PubMed and PsycInfo in October 2025, plus forward and backward snowballing in November 2025, identified 766 studies; 239 automatic and 29 manual duplicates were removed, 498 records were title-and-abstract screened, 113 went to full text, and 40 studies met the inclusion criteria.
2. Screening reliability was high: intercoder agreement reached 88.25% at the title-and-abstract stage and 89.53% at full text, with all discrepancies resolved by discussion among the three reviewers.
3. AI-based tools featured in 15 of the 40 included studies, and the review reports that the field gained considerable momentum particularly after the advent of generative AI; virtual reality was the second dominant technological strand with 11 studies.
4. The [[ai-technologies|technologies]] clustered into three purposes: supporting neurodivergent students' learning (n = 27), improving the selection of assistive technologies (n = 10), and addressing bias by educating neurotypical peers (n = 3).
5. Barrier coverage was uneven: reading and writing dominated (n = 13), followed by study management (n = 12), while attention challenges (n = 4) and social communication and interaction (n = 5) were comparatively neglected, and only 6 studies addressed multiple barriers at once.
6. Most studies focused on individual accommodations or on a single neurotype, which the authors identify as the field's structural weakness because overlapping functional differences across neurotypes are obscured by diagnostic categorisation.
7. Gaps named explicitly: research concentrated in the Global North, limited attention to postgraduate students, scarcity of (quasi-)experimental and longitudinal designs, and access and scalability problems for resource-intensive technologies such as virtual reality.
8. The review excludes studies of diagnostic tools, AI screening and early-diagnosis prediction, on the grounds that these sit outside intervention and support, and excludes [[k-12]] and specialised-institution settings to keep the higher-education focus.
9. Where technologies only replicate or supplement existing teaching in digital form, the review's framing (following Kirkwood and Price) expects limited [[learning-gains|learning gains]]; transformative potential is reserved for tools that change the learning experience itself.
10. The review incorporates a caution the included literature does not always carry: [[cognitive-offloading|over-reliance]] on [[conversational-ai|chatbots]] and the cognitive overload that immersive environments can induce are documented as "inversion effects", in which adding the technology can reduce learning.

## What the Decade of Evidence Contains

The review's organising decision is to categorise by functional barrier rather than by diagnosis — reading and writing, study management, attention, social communication and interaction — because a diagnostic lens hides the shared functional differences between neurotypes. Two examples the authors give make the case: dyslexic students tend to benefit from reduced reliance on written-only material, while both autistic students and students with ADHD can experience frequent fluctuation in their ability to focus, making performance sensitive to external conditions such as consistent structure and sufficient time for task switching. Higher education sharpens the stakes, since fewer contact hours push neurodivergent students towards independent study, precisely where access to effective digital support matters most and where standardised instruction and [[assessment]] can actively constrain rather than enable their learning.

The technology strand that has moved fastest is generative AI. Its arrival is credited with accelerating the field towards tools that can be personalised and deployed at scale, which is why AI appears in 15 of the 40 studies despite the review window opening in 2015. The virtual-reality strand, second at 11 studies, illustrates the opposite tension: immersive environments can be tailored and can offset the attentional costs of sensorially demanding rooms, but they are the most resource-intensive option in the set and the least plausibly universal.

## From Accommodation to Universal Design

The paper's argumentative core is a distinction between assistive technology as corrective measure — helping individual students adapt to environments not designed for them — and assistive technology as a driver of structural change that embodies universal design principles from the outset. The difference is not merely [[philosophy-of-ai-in-education|philosophical]]. Accommodation requires students to identify themselves as in need of special provision, which carries stigma; universal design dissolves the boundary by making the environment accessible to the widest possible range of students without a self-identification step. The review's own evidence points the same way, since the dominant pattern of neurotype-specific, accommodation-oriented tools cannot cover the functional differences that recur across neurotypes.

That leads to the review's second recommendation: participatory design, developing tools with neurodivergent students rather than for them. It also explains why the three studies aimed at educating neurotypical peers matter disproportionately in a small set — they are the only ones in the corpus that treat the environment and the people in it as the object of intervention rather than the student.

## What the Review Cannot Settle

The [[research-methods-aied|methodological]] limits are stated rather than glossed. The evidence base is thin where it matters most: the authors note the scarcity of (quasi-)experimental and longitudinal studies, which means much of the corpus describes deployments and perceptions rather than measured learning outcomes, and the heterogeneity of tools and outcome measures is the reason the design is a scoping review with [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] synthesis rather than a meta-analysis. Coverage is skewed towards undergraduate and Global North contexts, with postgraduate students and institutions outside those regions under-represented, and the search was limited to English-language peer-reviewed work, which will undercount regional and practitioner literature. Reviewers also had to bound the scope artificially, excluding acquired brain differences, [[well-being|mental health]] conditions, genetic syndromes, epilepsy and hearing impairments even while acknowledging some of these as neurodivergences.

What the review does establish is a baseline for the field that is genuinely useful to [[stakeholders]]: how many studies exist, what they build, which barriers they address, and where the distribution is lopsided. For institutions making procurement and design decisions, the practical implication is that the current market-shaped trajectory of assistive tools will keep producing narrow, diagnosis-gated and often resource-intensive products unless universal design and participation are stated as requirements. For researchers, the gap list is a research agenda: experimental and longitudinal designs, cross-neurotype functional targets, attention and social communication, postgraduate populations, and scalability of immersive tools.
