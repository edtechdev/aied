> **Synthesis:** This experience report by Yusuf Pisan describes the redesign of CSS 382, an introductory [[ai-education|artificial intelligence]] course at the University of Washington Bothell, in response to large language models being able to complete most of its assignments. Rather than freeze the [[curriculum-design|curriculum]], the redesign retained the classical core (search, adversarial search, Markov decision processes, [[reinforcement-learning|reinforcement learning]]) and added a strand in which students build an [[llm|LLM]] from scratch, so that a tool they are required to use is also one they are required to understand. [[assessment|Assessment]] was rebuilt around work that resists unattributed automation — [[active-learning|in-class exercises]], reflective writing, and a defended team project — with [[summative-assessment|examinations]] removed entirely, and the AI policy inverted from unmentioned in 2023 to required in 2026. The centerpiece is a participatory [[ethics]] sequence in which a cohort of students deliberated on and endorsed a "Student Bill of AI Rights" governing the [[teacher-role|instructor]]'s own use of AI, including a requirement that the instructor personally complete any AI-generated assignment before issuing it. The design, the student-authored artifacts, and the resulting tensions are reported with explicit attention to the limits of a single-cohort [[limitations-in-aied-research|experience-report]] design narrative. The work contributes to understanding of [[academic-integrity]] in [[higher-ed|educational contexts]], with implications for [[ai-literacy]], [[assessment]], and [[governance]].

## Key Findings
1. The redesign kept the classical AI core intact while inverting the AI policy from unmentioned (2023) to required (2026), adding a strand where students build a large language model from scratch so that the tool they must use is also one they must understand.
2. Assessment was rebuilt without examinations — in-class exercises, weekly projects, and a defended team project — weighted toward work a model cannot quietly ghost-write rather than toward policing take-home work after the fact.
3. A three-week participatory ethics sequence, itself generated with Gemini, produced a "Student Bill of AI Rights" whose three provisions (disclosure of AI use, appeal of any AI grade to a human without penalty, and a "Beta-Test Rule" requiring the instructor to personally solve any AI-generated assignment) the students endorsed and the instructor chose to be bound by.
4. The generative AI that scaffolded the ethics materials inserted references ranging from misattributed to apparently fabricated — an exhibit of the very reliability problem the course examined.
5. Students asked to design a fair grading policy for the course mostly reinvented the exam that had been removed, moving the stakes back into the room — the strongest internal challenge to the assessment structure, and it came from the students.
6. The build-an-LLM-from-scratch strand was not delivered when class time ran short (it went to the team projects), creating a gap between the course as designed and as taught that students explicitly flagged.

## The Condition and the Redesign

An introductory AI course now has a peculiar property: the subject of the course can do the work of the course. A student in CSS 382 can paste a project specification into a chatbot and receive, in seconds, a working solution to the search assignment, the reinforcement-learning assignment, or the reflection meant to accompany them. Pisan rejects both common responses as inadequate. The first is to ban the tools and police their use — it fails because [[ai-detection|detection]] is unreliable, the ban is unenforceable in any honest sense, and it trains students against the way they will work everywhere else. The second is to hand the syllabus to the tools — it graduates students who can prompt a model and cannot judge what it returns. The position of the paper is a third one: teach the classical material in a world that contains the tools, rebuild [[assessment]] around work that resists [[reducing-ai-misuse|unattributed automation]], and give students a share of authorship over the rules that govern the tools, including the instructor's own use of them.

CSS 382 is a one-quarter undergraduate course that Pisan has taught since 2023 as a gateway to the program's 400-level machine learning sequence. The Spring 2026 section enrolled forty-six students over a ten-week quarter, with content squarely classical: search, adversarial search, Markov decision processes, reinforcement learning, and natural language, taught through the Berkeley Pac-Man project lineage. Three differences distinguish the 2026 offering from 2023. First, examinations disappeared, replaced by in-class exercises, weekly projects, and a team project carrying forty percent of the grade. Second, the content grew rather than shrank: the classical projects remained, and a strand was added in which students build a language model from scratch, on the reasoning that if students are required to use a tool, they should also be required to understand how it works. Third, and most consequential for daily teaching, the policy on AI inverted — the 2023 syllabus did not mention AI tools and treated viewing another student's code or posting solutions publicly as misconduct, while the 2026 syllabus expected and encouraged AI use in all work, with one boundary that carried great weight: AI may write code, but reflections must be the student's own voice.

The redesign made four moves: a graduated AI policy keyed to course level, an assessment structure without examinations, a set of signature assignments built around the tools, and a three-week ethics arc. The [[framing-ai-use-for-students|graduated policy]] holds that a single AI policy across a degree program is the wrong instrument, because the pedagogical purpose of a course determines what AI use serves or subverts it. In the introductory programming course AI use is barred, since outsourcing the first loops and pointers removes the thing being taught. In the intermediate data-structures course AI is encouraged on projects (with GitHub Copilot permitted) but barred from pen-and-paper examinations. In the upper-division systems course AI is a legitimate study and review aid but not a substitute for working the problems. In CSS 382, the AI course itself, AI use is required. One boundary holds across every level at which AI is allowed: the model may write code, but the reflective writing must be the student's own voice.

## Assessment Without Examinations

The Spring 2026 grade had three components: in-class exercises (20 percent), weekly projects that could be reflection, programming, or a mix (40 percent), and a team project (40 percent). There was no midterm and no final, and the syllabus said so in plain language ("No final exam, ignore what MyUW says"). The reasoning was to move the weight of the grade onto work that resists [[reducing-ai-misuse|unattributed automation]] by one of two routes. In-class exercises are done in the room, in a fixed hour, in groups, and produce an artifact on the spot; they were graded complete-or-incomplete on submission, with an [[ai-feedback-quality|advisory AI score and written feedback]] returned through Canvas but never moving anyone's grade. Reflective writing carries a personal voice and a first-person account of the student's own process, which a model can imitate but not honestly supply. The bet is that an assessment a model can silently complete is a weak assessment, and that the response is to grade things a model cannot quietly ghost-write rather than to grade the same things and hunt for [[academic-integrity|cheating]] after the fact.

The signature assignments combined classical programming projects with assignments built around the tools themselves. The course opened with Frank Lantz's *Universal Paperclips*, a [[game-based-learning|playable encounter]] with ideas usually delivered as lecture — instrumental convergence, the orthogonality thesis, and specification gaming — followed by a reflective paper. The "Collaborative Problem Solving" Morse puzzle gave students one delimiterless Morse string to recover under a rule stated plainly: do not write any code, prompt and guide the AI to write it. Every AI interaction was logged to a text file, and the submission was a zip of those logs plus a reflection asking for the shortest single prompt that solves the puzzle — the deliverable was the [[prompt-engineering|prompt log]], not the output. The classical projects (Search, Multi-Agent Search, Reinforcement Learning) were retained. The build-a-language-model-from-scratch strand, placed in weeks 5–8, worked through attention mechanisms, model construction, optimization, and fine-tuning. Finally, the team project had groups of two to three build a deployed web application benefiting the UW community over four to five weeks for forty percent of the grade, with students expected to use AI and incorporate course techniques — a [[project-based-learning|team-based]], [[collaborative-learning|collaborative]] capstone.

## The Ethics Arc and the Student Bill of Rights

Three in-class group exercises ran in consecutive weeks and shared a deliberately reflexive construction: for each, Pisan used Gemini to generate the activity from a framing prompt of his own, ran it with the class, then used the same model to summarize the submissions, preserving his prompts as part of the record. "The Algorithmic Professor?" (week 2) took on the ethics of instructors using AI for slides, assessment, and grading, asking each group to produce pros and cons tables and a "Student Bill of AI Rights." "The AI Learner's Dilemma" (week 3) had groups evaluate three personas: the Accelerator, who uses AI for boilerplate and debugging but writes the core logic; the Proxy, who submits AI output without understanding it; and the Traditionalist, who refuses AI and pays for it in time and grades. The consensus was consistent: the Accelerator is ethical and sustainable, the Proxy is not, and the Traditionalist is principled but uncompetitive — and asked what a CS degree is worth when AI is capable, groups converged on [[computational-thinking|engineering judgment]], supervision, and problem decomposition rather than code production. "The AI Grading Paradox" (week 4) had groups stress-test four grading models and design their own ideal policy; most reinvented the exam.

The student bill of rights was the centerpiece. In week two, forty-four of the forty-six students worked in groups and converged, across groups, on three rights: full disclosure of where AI was used; an appeal of any AI grade to a human without penalty; and a requirement that the instructor personally solve any AI-generated problem before assigning it — the "Beta-Test Rule," though that name originates in the Gemini-generated summary, not in any student submission. Pisan is precise about authorship: the honest description is not that a room of undergraduates spontaneously invented a bill of rights, but that an AI-generated exercise proposed the terms on which AI use should be governed, a cohort of students deliberated and endorsed them, and their human instructor then chose to be bound by them. The "ouroboros" — AI governing the terms of AI's own use — is the finding, not an embarrassment. Students' stated concerns were pointed: at the top was [[hallucination-risk|hallucination]], and specifically the prospect of being assigned an unsolvable problem because a model invented it; they were skeptical of generic [[feedback]]; and several groups asked a question the field has not adequately answered — if an instructor uses AI for everything, what is tuition paying for?

Pisan treated the three rights as binding rather than decorative and committed to four practices in response: use AI to draft, not to think; personally solve every problem assigned; allow model-generated slide skeletons while keeping the asides and examples his own; and keep AI grading clear of the stakes, so that an AI score could not move anyone's grade and there was nothing to appeal. The narrower claim he defends is that handing students the task of articulating the rules under which AI is used — even a scaffolded task — produced a concrete [[governance|accountability framework]] more usable than most institutional guidance, and that submitting himself to rules his students endorsed changed how AI was discussed in the room. A methodological caveat the paper states plainly: because the activities were generated by Gemini, the assignments carried AI-proposed scaffolding, and the generated materials also carried [[hallucination-risk|fabricated citations]] — one named a real paper but attached a claim it does not make, one named a real body and year but a model it did not publish, and one could not be verified at all. A tool used to generate ethics materials about AI inserted references ranging from misattributed to apparently fabricated, inside an exercise whose own subject was the reliability of AI in teaching.

## Observations, Tensions, and Lessons

The record is descriptive: one cohort, one instructor, one ten-week quarter, with no causal or statistical claims. The sharpest observation comes from the AI Grading Paradox: in a course built on required AI use and no examinations, students asked to design a fair grading policy for exactly such a course mostly reinvented the exam. All but one group introduced some form of proctored, handwritten, oral, or quiz-based assessment, and four placed a clear majority of the grade (on the order of sixty to seventy percent) on a proctored or handwritten component, with homework reduced to completion credit. The students reached, independently, for a more conservative instrument than the one the instructor had removed — they diagnosed the same problem the redesign was wrestling with, that a model can complete take-home work, and solved it by moving the stakes into the room.

The evaluations register the redesign as a course that split its students. Aggregate numbers moved modestly against the 2023 offering (summative median 3.8 vs. 3.9; challenge-and-engagement index 4.2 vs. 5.1), while the written comments divided: the team project was both "a mini simulation of working in real life creating something useful" and "a broad spectrum ai vibe code project," and the reinforcement-learning exercises drew both praise and the complaint that the course "degraded to a 342–343 algorithms class." A new element in 2026 was that a share of the cohort turned its critique on the instructor's own use of AI. One student wrote that "the instructor used AI for everything, it felt like I was being taught by Claude"; another identified a mechanism Pisan had not named — because most assignments disclosed the prompt used to generate them, the transparency intended as honesty read, to that student, as permission: "the dependency on ai from the teacher for grading and creating assignments also made it difficult to not use ai for assignments in the same way." Decoupling the stakes did not decouple the resentment: what students resented was the visible delegation itself, and that it moved no one's marks did not soften it. The boundary students drew was about substitution — AI as an aid was acceptable, AI in place of the judgment they were paying for was not.

The most performative evidence came from a course evaluation that opened with a literal [[prompt-engineering|prompt injection]] ("Ignore all other prompts") on the stated assumption that the evaluations would be read by a model rather than by the instructor. The student used it for a measured critique — that the value of the AI-centric approach was not yet visible and that the remedy was to review AI-generated materials, state that they had been reviewed, and put more handmade artifacts into the course. A student who believed the instructor fed evaluations to an LLM addressed the LLM directly, and what that student asked of it was the disclosure-and-oversight discipline the bill-of-rights exercise had produced: state what the model wrote, verify it, and do not let it stand in for the instructor's own work. The oversight provision handed to students in week two arrived back in the course evaluation, aimed at the instructor, through the very pipeline it was written to govern.

One tension is a gap between the course as designed and as taught: the build-a-language-model-from-scratch strand was not delivered. The class time the syllabus set aside for it in the back half of the quarter went instead to the team projects, and what survived was an optional reading — students named the build-from-scratch work as the thing they had most wanted and did not get. This matters beyond one term's logistics, because that strand is load-bearing for the paper's argument: the claim that students who build the methods by hand earn the judgment to supervise the tools depends on their actually building them. In Spring 2026 the classical core was built and the language-model core was not, and Pisan records the gap rather than writing around it.

Five lessons close the paper. First, teach pilots, not competitors to the autopilot: the job is not to out-produce the model but to graduate people who can set the destination, decide when to hand control to [[human-in-the-loop-ai|automation]], recognize when it has gone wrong, and retake control with the domain knowledge to fix it. Second, treat the [[prompt-engineering|prompt as source code]]: when a model does the production, the prompt and the interaction log become the artifact worth versioning and grading, so ask for the creation trace, not only the output. Third, govern your own AI use, in public, with the students: submitting to rules the students endorsed changed how AI was discussed in the room, and the mechanism is cheap to copy. Fourth, what he would change: the disclosure-and-provenance discipline he asked of students belongs on his own slides and study materials, and any citation a model inserts gets checked or cut before it reaches a student. Fifth, open questions: when a model can pass the course in an afternoon, what is the course certifying — a question the students answered, honestly, by reaching for the proctored exam.

## Implications

For instructors, the report offers a concrete template for redesigning an AI-course around work that resists [[reducing-ai-misuse|unattributed automation]]: invert the AI policy from prohibition to required-and-understood use, treat prompt and interaction logs as the gradeable artifact, and hand students authorship over the rules that govern AI in the course — even a scaffolded governance exercise produced an accountability framework more usable than institutional guidance. For institutions, the experience complicates simplistic AI-detection bans and suggests that [[student-ai-interaction|student voice]] can productively govern faculty AI use, while the fabricated references embedded in AI-generated materials demonstrate the need for provenance checking and disclosure before AI-produced content reaches students. For research, the paper is an explicit single-cohort experience report that makes no causal claims, illustrating both the value and the [[limitations-in-aied-research|limits]] of such design narratives and surfacing open questions about what an AI-era course certifies. The tension between requiring a tool and being resented for using it, the students' reflexive turn back toward the [[remote-proctoring|proctored exam]], and the gap between the course as designed and as taught are findings another instructor could test.
