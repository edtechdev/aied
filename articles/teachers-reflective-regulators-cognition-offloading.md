> **Synthesis:** Ho and Chen (2026) investigated how in-service teachers perceive, manage, and reflect on the cognitive implications of integrating [[generative-ai|generative AI]] into their professional practice. Using a collective case-study design, they interviewed 18 [[teacher-role|teachers]] in mainland Chinese and Hong Kong settings and applied the [[cognitive-offloading]] framework to guide data collection and thematic coding. Teachers generally framed GenAI not as a substitute for thinking but as a partner in cognitive regulation shaped by institutional and [[ethics|ethical]] contexts. Building on Risko and Gilbert's framework, the study proposes a *[[metacognition|metacognitive ecology]]* lens for understanding how cognitive monitoring, [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] interpretation, ethical boundary-setting, and institutional conditions shape GenAI-mediated cognitive redistribution.

## Key Findings

- Three interrelated processes emerged: recognising and evaluating the need to use GenAI, redistributing cognition between human and machine systems, and reflectively re-engaging after use.
- Teachers treated GenAI as a reflective partner in cognition rather than a replacement, emphasising the evolving interplay between human judgment and automation.
- The study extends cognitive offloading theory to professional AI judgment, identifying 'professional drift' as a risk in AI-mediated teaching.
- [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] was reframed as the reflective and ethical regulation of cognition, linking cognition, ethics, and identity in what the authors call a metacognitive ecology.
