> **Synthesis:** Payan, Gyevnár, Kasirzadeh & Shah (2026) tackle the institutional end of the [[academic-integrity]] problem: when a submitted manuscript may be substantially [[generative-ai|AI-generated]], crediting expertise from authorship alone stops working. Their proposal, **greCAPTCHA**, is a proctored assessment that measures an author's *capacity to verify* — the knowledge and reasoning needed to critically assess the contributions they claim. In a user study and interviews with 31 researchers, automated scores separated participants' own papers from unfamiliar control papers with an AUC of 0.90, and participants judged the construct appropriate while flagging wording, grading and time-pressure problems that must be fixed before deployment.

## Key Findings

1. **The construct is manuscript-specific understanding, not domain familiarity.** Participants performed significantly better on their own work than on unfamiliar papers, and there was no statistically significant difference between unfamiliar papers inside versus outside a participant's field — evidence the instrument isolates understanding of *this* manuscript.
2. **Four question levels operationalize the construct:** planted errors, unstated rationales, background knowledge and failure modes. Free-response questions on rationales and failure modes were rated the most diagnostic.
3. **Discrimination is real but not yet deployment-grade.** The prototype reached an AUC of 0.90 with a lowest false-positive rate of 20%; the authors argue that error rate must fall further.
4. **Participants saw a [[trust]] benefit for [[peer-assessment|peer review]]** — a well-designed greCAPTCHA would increase their confidence in review as a verification mechanism — while warning that ambiguous wording, unclear grading expectations, rigid rubrics and time constraints would corrupt results.
5. **Authorship evidence is reframed as assessable.** Rather than trying to detect AI text after the fact, the approach certifies that the named author can defend the work.

## Why this is a detection-avoidance design

The paper deliberately steps away from [[ai-detection]]. Detection asks whether text was machine-written, a question that is unreliable and increasingly contested; greCAPTCHA instead asks whether the human can answer for the content. This mirrors the broader shift in [[assessment]] design toward evidence of process and understanding — the same logic behind [[authentic-assessment]] reforms and oral defenses — but formalizes it as a proctored instrument with a score, which is what institutions need if they are to act on it.

## Validity limits the authors themselves raise

The reported false-positive rate means one in five participants who could verify their work would be misclassified, which for a gatekeeping instrument is a serious cost. The authors are candid that deployment requires reducing it, and they note the study's participants were researchers rather than the full range of people whose submissions institutions evaluate. The paper therefore lands as a strong construct-validity argument plus a prototype, not a ready policy instrument — and it sits alongside work on [[assessment-validity]] and [[remote-proctoring]] that has to answer the same fairness questions.
