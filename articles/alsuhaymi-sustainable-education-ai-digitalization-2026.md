> **Synthesis:** Alsuhaymi and Atallah (2026) offer a [[qualitative-research|qualitative]], value-[[critical-thinking|critical analysis]] of whether — and under what conditions — AI contributes to sustainable education in the age of digitalization. They argue AI does not exert a uniform, inherently positive or negative influence on educational sustainability; its effects are **conditional and governance-mediated**. Distinguishing *sustainable education* (a value-based, human-centered project), *sustainability in education*, and *education for sustainable development* (ESD), the authors show that AI contributes to sustainable education only when its adoption is subordinated to explicit educational values and human-centered purposes — and undermines it when technologization and commodification are allowed to erode those values.

## Key Findings
1. **AI's effect on educational sustainability is conditional, not automatic.** Whether AI supports or erodes sustainable education depends on how its adoption is governed and to what values it is subordinated — not on the technology itself.
2. **Three distinct framings must be distinguished.** *Sustainable education* (human-centered, value-based), *sustainability in education* (treating education as an instrument for sustainability), and *education for sustainable development* (ESD, the policy-oriented global agenda) are different projects that AI serves differently.
3. **Digitalization brings dual pressures.** The same developments that enable [[personalized-learning|personalization]] and efficiency also accelerate technologization and commodification, threatening the erosion of educational values and human-centered purposes.
4. **Value-critical analysis is the corrective lens.** A [[critical-pedagogy|value-critical]] approach surfaces the normative commitments embedded in AI-mediated education, arguing that sustainability must be reclaimed as a value-based project rather than reduced to technical efficiency.

## Relevance to AI and sustainability in education
This paper anchors the [[sustainability|sustainable AI in education]] concept page's normative dimension: it frames sustainability not as a technical or environmental metric but as a **value-based, human-centered educational project**. It connects directly to [[ethics]] in [[ai-education|AI education]], to [[governance]] (the conditions under which AI use is responsibly governed), and to the knowledge base's treatment of AI's [[critical-pedagogy|critical]] and [[philosophy-of-ai-in-education|philosophical]] dimensions.

## What this means for practice

- **Institutions.** Subordinate every AI adoption decision to explicit educational values and human-centered purposes; the paper's central claim is that AI's contribution to sustainable education is conditional and governance-mediated, not automatic.
- **Administrators.** Keep three framings distinct in strategy documents — *sustainable education* (value-based, human-centered), *sustainability in education* (education as an instrument for sustainability), and *education for sustainable development* — because AI serves each of them differently.
- **Policymakers.** Design regulation against the dual pressures of digitalization: the same developments that enable [[personalized-learning|personalization]] and efficiency accelerate the technologization and commodification that erode educational values.
- **Administrators.** Preserve reflective pedagogy, critical engagement with AI-generated outputs, and meaningful human relationships as the core of educational processes rather than treating them as residual to efficiency gains.
- **Policymakers.** Make sustainability a stated educational value in policy rather than a technical-efficiency metric, since the paper argues governance conditions — not the technology — determine whether AI supports or undermines sustainability.

## Limitations

- This is a qualitative, value-critical conceptual analysis with no primary data: it cannot measure AI's effects on sustainability in any particular institution or national system, so the conditional model is an interpretive framework rather than a tested explanation.
- The argument rests on secondary sources — peer-reviewed literature and policy-oriented normative documents — and the authors state that it does not adopt a systematic literature review or meta-analytic design; database, search-term, and inclusion descriptions serve a scoping function, not an analytical protocol.
- The analysis is positioned at a macro level of depth and scope, addressing normative and governance questions, so it cannot resolve institution-level or classroom-level variation in how AI is adopted.
- The authors identify culturally and religiously grounded contexts, where epistemic authority, moral formation, and technological mediation intersect, as unexamined; no empirical case analysis of enacted governance is included.
