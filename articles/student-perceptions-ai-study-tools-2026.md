> **Synthesis:** This exploratory EdArXiv survey examines how students perceive [[generative-ai|generative AI]] tools for study productivity and learning. From an anonymous online questionnaire (N=26, 23 current students), the study reports that 88.5% of respondents use AI tools for studying, with most reporting faster assignment completion (80.7% agree/strongly agree) and improved subject understanding (92.3%). At the same time, students voiced concerns about [[cognitive-offloading|overreliance]], reduced [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]], diminished independent effort, inaccurate information, and [[academic-integrity|academic dishonesty]]. The small, non-probability sample and self-reported measures make the findings exploratory, consistent with broader reviews of the benefits and risks of [[generative-ai|generative AI]] in students' study practices.

## Benefits: perceived productivity and understanding

Respondents overwhelmingly reported using AI tools for studying, with explanations, summaries, information retrieval, and feedback as common uses. Fourteen respondents (53.8%) strongly agreed and seven (26.9%) agreed that AI helped them complete assignments faster; seventeen (65.4%) strongly agreed and seven (26.9%) agreed that AI improved their understanding of subjects. These perceptions mirror a wider pattern in the [[generative-ai-education-productivity-gaps|education literature]] suggesting that students experience AI as a productivity aid.

## Risks: overreliance, critical thinking, and integrity

Alongside benefits, students described concerns about overreliance, reduced critical thinking, reduced independent effort, inaccurate information, and academic dishonesty. These echo the dual-edged findings of [[ai-education|AI in education]] research on [[cognitive-offloading|cognitive offloading]] and [[reducing-ai-misuse|AI misuse]], where AI assistance trades short-term efficiency against potential erosion of independent [[self-regulated-learning|learning behaviors]].

## Limitations

The study uses a small (N=26), non-probability sample with self-reported measures, so findings are exploratory and cannot establish causal effects. Its value lies in capturing authentic student voices about [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]] and perceived impacts, complementing larger-scale quantitative studies.
