> **Presumed Effective: The Manufacturing of an Evidence Base for AI-in-Education Through Flawed Meta-Analysis** — A forensic [[research-methods-aied|methodological]] audit by O'Neill (2026) of 14 high-impact [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|meta-analyses]] making broad claims about AI's impact on education. The audit finds that **none** of the 14 meta-analyses provided a valid basis for their claims: none had a coherent construct (a tool like "ChatGPT" treated as a single intervention, and multidimensional "learning outcomes" pooled as if interchangeable), none validly assessed publication bias, and all exhibited severe heterogeneity that moderator analyses never resolved. A majority (61%) of randomly vetted primary studies were problematic, most often because the outcome measured did not match the meta-analysis claim.

## Key Findings

- **No coherent construct.** All but two meta-analyses defined the treatment as a *tool* (ChatGPT, [[generative-ai|GenAI]]) without a [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] intervention — "a product name is not a pedagogy," equivalent to meta-analyzing the effects of "paper." Likewise, outcomes like "[[learning-gains|academic achievement]]" bundled test scores, motivation, [[self-efficacy]], attitudes, and [[student-engagement|engagement]] into a single pooled estimate — averaging unlike things into a mathematically calculable but meaningless number. Eleven of 14 had no population boundary, treating "students" as spanning children through [[adult-learning|adult learners]].
- **Problematic primary-study selection.** Of 59 vetted primary studies, 28 of 46 randomly selected (61%) and all 13 targeted ones presented validity concerns — most commonly dependent-variable mismatch (n=16) and independent-variable mismatch (n=11). One primary study with fabricated references and impossible statistics (Alneyadi & Wardat 2023) was included by six of the meta-analyses.
- **Dependence among effect sizes ignored.** Twelve of 14 meta-analyses treated multiple effect sizes drawn from the same primary study as independent — inflating apparent evidence (e.g., 35 studies became 134 effects; 22 publications became 86 "independent studies"). This understates standard errors, narrows confidence intervals, and makes moderator effects and bias tests spuriously significant.
- **Extreme heterogeneity never resolved.** Average I² = 87.5% (range 77.2–94.4%). All 14 used random-effects models but only 2 reported a prediction interval (both included zero). No meta-analysis met the minimum subgroup size of 10 for moderator analysis; more than half drew conclusions from subgroups as small as k = 1 or k = 2. None resolved heterogeneity enough to justify a single pooled effect — yet all 14 reported one authoritatively.
- **Publication-bias assessment was invalid across the board.** None provided a valid and sufficient accounting. Twelve used effect size as the unit of analysis despite dependent data (invalidating Egger/Begg tests); widely discredited fail-safe N metrics were used in 6 studies (3 as the *only* bias check); negative low-power tests were overinterpreted as proof of no bias (Egger's threshold should be p<.10, not p<.05). Only one meta-analysis (Liu et al. 2025) made valid bias calculations.
- **A cascade of errors produced a concrete (wrong) policy recommendation.** Two meta-analyses mis-coded a primary study (Ji et al. 2024) that treated student *groups* as the unit of analysis as if N=36 individuals — misplacing it in the "21–40 students" subgroup and then recommending ChatGPT/GenAI interventions be designed for that class size, layering miscalculation on miscategorization on tiny subgroups.
- **Flawed evidence circulates as authority.** The 14 meta-analyses (published in Springer Nature, Wiley, SAGE, Taylor & Francis, and MDPI journals) had amassed >2,000 citations within ~16 months. A citation-uptake analysis found the (now-retracted) Wang & Fan (2025) meta-analysis was cited as authoritative evidence of AI gains in 60% of post-retraction citing papers, none of which acknowledged the retraction; Lee & Kwon (2024) was mis-cited in 86% of sampled citing papers.
- **Root cause is systemic, not individual error.** The recurrence of elementary validity failures across peer-reviewed journals points to a failure of editorial and reviewer gatekeeping. The author recommends requiring full data transparency for meta-analyses and stronger editorial/reviewer standards.
