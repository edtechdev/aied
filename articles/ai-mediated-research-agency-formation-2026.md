> **Synthesis:** Han and Liu (2026) examined how [[generative-ai|generative AI]] reshapes the [[agency|agency]] of early-career researchers in doctoral and postdoctoral training. They define AI dependence as reliance on intelligent tools as the primary source of cognitive support in judgment-intensive research tasks, and read its educational risk as *epistemic delegation* — the transfer of problem framing, methodological choice, and interpretive authority from the researcher to the system. In a survey of 420 astronomy doctoral students and postdoctoral researchers in China, AI dependence was negatively associated with research autonomy and research [[self-efficacy|self-efficacy]], both of which were positively associated with innovative research behavior, and supervisory support weakened those negative pathways. The study shifts the debate from AI-enabled productivity to AI-mediated researcher formation.

## Key Findings
1. **AI dependence depresses research agency.** Higher reliance on intelligent tools was negatively associated with both research autonomy and research self-efficacy in the sample of 420 early-career researchers.
2. **Two mediating pathways link dependence to innovation.** Both research autonomy and research self-efficacy mediated the association between AI dependence and innovative research behavior, with both mediators positively related to innovation.
3. **The autonomy pathway is the stronger mechanism.** The indirect association through research autonomy was stronger than the indirect association through research self-efficacy, making perceived control over problem framing and method choice the more decisive lever.
4. **Supervisory support buffers the loss of agency.** Supervisory support was positively associated with autonomy, self-efficacy, and innovation, and it weakened the negative association between AI dependence and both research autonomy and innovative behavior.
5. **Instrument quality was established on split samples.** Split-sample factor analysis supported a broad one-factor AI-dependence scale, and item-level confirmatory factor analysis supported the five-factor measurement model.

## AI Dependence as Epistemic Delegation

The paper's central move is to treat AI dependence not as tool use but as the redistribution of epistemic authority. In judgment-intensive research tasks, decomposing a question, choosing a method, and interpreting results are traditionally the researcher's own cognitive work; the authors argue that when an intelligent system becomes the primary source of cognitive support, these operations migrate to the machine. They call this *epistemic delegation* and frame it as an educational risk precisely because [[higher-ed|doctoral education]] is meant to build, not outsource, the capacity for independent judgment. Grounding the construct in [[self-regulated-learning|regulatory]] terms, the study locates dependence alongside [[cognitive-offloading]] but stresses that here the stakes are identity-forming: how an early-career scholar learns to think is at stake, not just task efficiency.

## Mediation and Moderation of the Agency Pathway

Using the AI-mediated research agency formation framework, the authors modeled whether perceived autonomy and confidence explain the link between tool dependence and innovative research behavior, and whether the research environment conditions these links. Both mediators carried the negative association, but autonomy was the stronger channel; that is, the main psychological cost of heavy dependence runs through a felt loss of control over one's research choices rather than through eroded confidence. Supervisory support operated as a protective condition, weakening the negative link to autonomy and to innovation. The authors interpret this as the relational foundation for *epistemic supervision*: explaining, verifying, and [[prompt-engineering|prompting]] reflective AI use so that dependence does not quietly become [[human-in-the-loop-ai|delegation without oversight]].

## From Productivity to Researcher Formation

The contribution reframes a dominant concern. Rather than asking whether generative AI makes early-career research faster, Han and Liu ask how it shapes who the researcher becomes — a question closer to [[learner-identity]] and the sociology of scientific training. Their emphasis on supervisory [[scaffolding]] suggests that the supervisory environment, not individual tenacity, is the most actionable site for intervention in higher-education [[research-methods-aied|research training]].

## What this means for practice
- **Instructors and research supervisors.** Treat early-career researchers' heavy reliance on generative AI as an agency signal worth discussing explicitly: ask which framing, method, and interpretation decisions are staying with the student and which have migrated to the tool.
- **Instructors.** Build supervision around explanation and verification — require students to justify model choice and audit outputs — since the study identifies supervisory support as the buffer that weakens dependence's negative link to autonomy and innovation.
- **Faculty developers.** Equip doctoral supervisors with concrete protocols for epistemic supervision, because the finding that autonomy mediates more strongly than confidence points to preserving student control over problem framing as the highest-leverage target.

## Limitations
- Cross-sectional survey design: the mediation and moderation tests are modeled associations, so the direction from AI dependence to reduced autonomy and innovation is inferred, not confirmed.
- All measures are researcher [[self-report-measures|self-report]] collected through peer-based WeChat groups among astronomy doctoral students and postdoctoral researchers in China; the astronomy and Chinese higher-education context limits generalization to other disciplines and settings.
- The five-factor and one-factor scale structures were established within this single sample (split-sample factor analysis), without validation against observed task behavior or a comparison group.
