> **Synthesis:** Chick, Morello and Staffey document a six-week faculty institute at the University of Bridgeport in Summer 2025 that took ten instructors and staff from Education, Health Sciences, Business, Sport Management, Traditional Chinese Medicine and Social Sciences through [[universal-design-for-learning|Universal Design for Learning]], Bloom's Taxonomy and design thinking as lenses for [[generative-ai|generative AI]] integration. Pre and post surveys, session artefacts, reflections and ten capstone redesigns show a cohort that entered with low [[ai-literacy|AI fluency]] (mean self-rating 1.89 on a six-point scale, none rating themselves advanced) and left with 60% reporting high confidence in guiding student AI use and 90% reporting positive perceptions. The authors name four themes, fear shifting to curiosity, a desire for ethical clarity, inclusive design as an equity amplifier, and faculty moving from gatekeepers to guides, and propose "symbiotic pedagogy" as the framework their participants actually built. Their conclusion is that resistance was never the problem: the barriers surviving the institute are institutional, a policy vacuum, unequal tool access, unrecognised time, and assessment systems built for pre-AI work.

## Key Findings

1. **A deliberately mixed cohort, very small.** Ten faculty and staff completed UBRIDGE (Universal design, Bloom's taxonomy, Reflection, Integration, Design thinking, Growth mindset, Ethics), six weekly two-hour synchronous sessions on Zoom. Teaching experience ranged from 2 to 35 years (M = 12.3). Recruitment was purposive rather than enthusiast-only: five reported no prior AI use in teaching and four had tried rubrics or quiz generation.
2. **Confidence and perceptions moved sharply in one direction.** Before, 80% rated their confidence in guiding student AI use as nonexistent or low and 20% held negative views of AI in [[higher-ed|higher education]] (baseline mean 3.22 of 5). Afterwards no one reported no confidence, 60% placed themselves at the top of the scale, and 90% reported positive perceptions with negative views at 0%.
3. **Concerns were about integrity and over-reliance, not replacement.** Academic integrity led pre-survey concerns (80%), then student or faculty over-reliance (70%) and bias or inaccuracy (50%); equity and access were named by none. The most requested training was [[academic-integrity|AI ethics and academic integrity]] (70%), level with AI basics and course-design integration.
4. **Four themes structured the transformation.** Fear to curiosity; ethical clarity desired but lacking; inclusive design as an equity amplifier; and faculty moving from gatekeepers to guides. The authors stress the shift was partial: some kept "healthy scepticism" throughout, and the group benefited from those sceptics moving to nuanced critique.
5. **The capstone redesigns are the hardest evidence.** All ten redesigned a course component. Coded thematically, 8 of 10 positioned AI as a scaffold supporting rather than replacing thinking, 7 of 10 redefined their core teaching purpose toward inquiry, 6 of 10 built UDL-aligned designs, 6 of 10 embedded critique of AI bias, 6 of 10 connected AI use to professional practice, and 5 of 10 reflected explicitly on their instructional identity.
6. **Intentions were near universal; the enabling conditions were not.** All ten said they would keep integrating AI and would recommend the institute, and 70% had already begun using AI-enhanced assignments before the six weeks ended. The same participants described contradictory policy signals, personal subscriptions needed for continued tool access, and no course release or summer support for the redesigns they had planned.

## How the institute was designed and studied

The design mixed three frameworks rather than treating AI as tool training: UDL (CAST 2018) as the inclusion frame, Bloom's Taxonomy (Anderson and Krathwohl 2001) to locate where AI helps and where it displaces learning objectives, and design thinking (Brown 2009), with its empathise, define, ideate, prototype and test stages, as the process connecting theory to course design. The supporting literature includes TPACK as the basis for what the authors call "AIPACK", Rogers's diffusion categories, Mezirow's transformative learning and its disorienting dilemmas, and DigCompEdu and the EU Digital Education Action Plan as policy analogues.

The study is a qualitative case study in Stake's intrinsic tradition with survey components, framed as pragmatic constructivism and analysed with Braun and Clarke's six-phase thematic analysis in NVivo. Evidence came from pre and post surveys, Padlet boards, collaborative policy and assignment documents, design thinking worksheets, screenshots of AI experiments, asynchronous reflections, capstone submissions and facilitator field notes, with member checking, peer debriefing and thick description. The lead authors were also the facilitators and kept a reflexive journal about that dual role. The study received IRB exemption as normal educational practice research, research participation was voluntary and separable from the institute, and participants received modest stipends.

## What changed over six weeks

The vocabulary shift is the clearest marker. Early descriptions of AI as a "cheating machine", a "threat" or a "dehumanizing force" gave way to "creative partner", "curious assistant" or "design prompt". A mathematics professor's early comment shows the fear the institute had to work through: "I spent years developing expertise in my field. Now a machine can solve problems faster and explain solutions better than I can. What's my value anymore?" Hands-on use produced relief as much as surprise: "It's good, but it's not magic. It can give me a decent first draft, but it doesn't know my students, my context, my goals. There's still plenty of room for me." One participant described the arc directly: "Week 1, I wanted to ban it entirely. Week 3, I was intrigued but skeptical."

The authors read the confidence jump as a reorientation of professional identity, from reacting to AI to designing for it.

## Four themes of transformation

- **Fear to curiosity.** Achieved not through advocacy but through structured safe space for experimentation, which acknowledged the existential question of professional value before moving to practice.
- **Ethical clarity desired but lacking.** Participants arrived in binary terms, prohibited or permitted, and wanted principled reasoning rather than rule lists. "Our academic integrity policy just says 'unauthorized use of AI is prohibited,' but what's authorized? Who decides?" Working groups produced [[ai-use-disclosure|AI collaboration statements]], a decision tree weighing learning objectives, assessment validity and student agency, and student-facing syllabus language. The authors present this bottom-up work as a template for policy co-creation.
- **Inclusive design as equity amplifier.** Participants used AI for multiple representations, differentiated complexity, language support and low-stakes conversation practice. The theme carries its own correction: an experiment where AI gave stereotyped career advice based on names suggesting different ethnic backgrounds became a teaching case about [[equity-in-ai-education|bias]], and faculty concluded access depended on intentional design.
- **Gatekeepers to guides.** Assignments changed from "AI-proof" to requiring documented, defensible AI collaboration with oral defence and peer critique. Participants also named the cost: "It's exhausting. Every semester, AI capabilities change, and I have to redesign everything."

## Symbiotic pedagogy and the institutional framework

The authors' theoretical contribution is "symbiotic pedagogy", building on the "symbiotic intelligence" of their earlier work and offered as an alternative to techno-optimist and techno-pessimist framings. It has five principles: complementary design, using distinct human and AI strengths; transparent process, where AI collaboration is visible and reflected on; critical integration, where students judge when to trust, question or modify output; ethical reasoning embedded throughout; and iterative refinement, which treats [[prompt-engineering|experimentation]] and failure as the route to competence. One participant supplies the emblem: "I realized AI isn't trying to be me, it's trying to help me be better at being me."

For institutions the paper offers a five-domain support framework, each with challenges, actions and success indicators: policy development (faculty co-creation, against reactive prohibition and contradictory messaging); resource allocation (institutional subscriptions and time support, against individual burden); community building (communities of practice and peer networks, against post-institute isolation and departmental resistance); recognition systems (promotion criteria valuing pedagogical scholarship, against career disincentives); and infrastructure adaptation (LMS, assessment and integrity process change, against systems that flag legitimate AI collaboration as dishonesty). The authors warn that piecemeal responses leaving [[educational-policy-ai|policy]] and infrastructure untouched will fail even enthusiastic cohorts.

## Barriers, limitations, and what does not transfer

The limitations are specific. The sample is ten faculty from one institution described as an HSI, purposively sampled, so findings cannot be generalised to other institutional types, disciplines or regions. Participants volunteered for AI-focused development and received stipends and individual facilitator feedback, so self-selection bias is unavoidable, and the authors ask whether similar transformation is possible among faculty who are required rather than invited to engage. Six weeks cannot show whether practice becomes habitual; longitudinal follow-up is needed. Students were never consulted, even in the design thinking empathy phase, which drew on faculty assumptions rather than student data, so the paper cannot say whether the redesigns meet student needs. The dual researcher-facilitator role may have shaped participant narratives, and the specific tools described will date as capabilities move, though the authors argue the principles will not.

The closing claim is diagnostic rather than instructional: faculty resistance "stems not from technophobia or stubborn traditionalism but from legitimate concerns about educational quality, equity, and human agency". Its practical import is that six weeks can change what faculty believe and design, but the conditions that let that work continue, clear [[ethics|ethical guidance]], real time, institutional tool access, and [[authentic-assessment|assessment]] and integrity processes that can handle human-AI collaboration, are not deliverables of a faculty development programme.
