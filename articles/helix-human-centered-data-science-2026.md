> **Synthesis:** Chui et al. present the Human-Centered Education for Learning in Information and eXplainable Computing (HELIX) framework for embedding societal-impact and [[ethics]] content into computing courses, organized around three iterative pillars — knowledge building, decision-making, and empowerment — with concrete actions for instructors and students. In a graduate introductory programming course pilot (n=22), the framework used readings, algorithmic design activities, and scenario-based reflections to examine changes in students' knowledge acquisition, decision-making, and self-reflection regarding human-centered perspectives in data science. ## The HELIX Framework

As [[generative-ai|AI]] and data-driven systems pervade practice, there is an imperative to embed societal-impact and ethics content into computing courses. HELIX is organized around three iterative pillars:

1. **Knowledge building** — developing understanding of how data-driven systems operate and their societal implications.
2. **Decision-making** — practicing the human-centered judgments involved in designing and using such systems.
3. **Empowerment** — building the confidence and [[agency]] to shape systems responsibly.

The framework was applied in a graduate introductory programming course using readings, algorithmic design activities, and scenario-based reflections. The authors release an anonymized materials kit (survey, assignments, analysis code) to support adoption.

## Pilot Findings

The pilot (n=22) examined changes in students' knowledge acquisition, decision-making processes, and self-reflection on human-centered perspectives in data science. Findings suggest the framework is feasible in information science contexts, connecting to [[explainable-ai]] and [[human-in-the-loop-ai]] perspectives. The authors discuss design tensions — workload, assessment, and relevance to diverse information science learners — and provide guidelines for integrating human-centered content without overwhelming technical outcomes. They recommend future work using comparative survey assessment to strengthen causal inferences.
