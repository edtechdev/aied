> **Synthesis:** This [[meta-analysis-systematic-review|systematic literature review]] examines 28 empirical studies of AI **used as a learning tool** (not AI as a [[curriculum-design|curriculum]] topic) in authentic [[k-12]] settings, drawn from Scopus, Web of Science, ACM, IEEE Xplore, the *Journal of Learning Analytics* and *IJAIED* for 2010–2023, and coded with etic frameworks including the learning-theory taxonomies of Leonard (2002) and Zhou and Brown (2015), the Reference Model for Learning Analytics, the Detect–Diagnose–Act framework and the Six-Level Automation Model (Molenaar, 2022), and Henderson et al.'s Feedback Impact Taxonomy. Research concentrates in China (N = 6) and the United States (N = 6) and in primary education (N = 15). Its central finding is a two-part shortfall: **about half of the studies report no learning theory or [[pedagogy|pedagogical]] model to guide design**, and only 39.29% contextualise the tool to concrete course conditions. [[educational-robotics|Social robots]] are the most common technology (N = 11), most tools do student-facing work with partial automation (N = 15), and student performance remains the dominant outcome while [[teacher-role|teacher]] effects were examined in a single study. The authors translate this into four calls: localise [[ai-education|AIEd]] to regional contexts, ground it in theory and course design, protect [[agency|teacher agency]] and autonomy, and evaluate sustained pedagogical effects rather than end results alone.

## Key Findings

1. **A gap the prior reviews left open.** Earlier SLRs mapping AI in K-12 covered the main [[ai-technologies|technologies]] and their effects on performance, but the authors argue none addressed pedagogical grounding ([[learning-theories|learning theories]] plus course contextualisation), purposes, and effects together in authentic settings; only two SLRs had examined learning theories, and one of those (Oyelere et al., 2022) was confined to African schools, with both spanning only to 2021. The explicit target is AI as a tool for learning, not learning about AI.
2. **Research is recent, geographically concentrated, and roughly half non-journal.** Most manuscripts date from 2016 onward, with interest rising from 2018; China and the United States are the most prolific countries (N = 6 each), followed by Spain (3), Greece, Australia, Brazil and Taiwan (2 each), and single studies from Sri Lanka, Egypt, Italy, Sweden and the Netherlands. 46.43% of papers appeared in conference proceedings (N = 13) against 53.57% in journals (N = 15), and coverage of quality indexes was partial: 10 of 13 conference papers (76.92%) in GGS and/or CORE-indexed venues and 11 of 15 journal articles (73.33%) in WoS high-impact journals.
3. **Half the corpus is not pedagogically grounded.** Only 15 of 28 manuscripts are informed by concrete learning theories or models. Among those that are, constructivist theories or models dominate (N = 10, including [[game-based-learning|game-based learning]] at N = 6 and Piaget's cognitive development at N = 1), followed by cognitive learning models (N = 4, including Bloom's taxonomy and Zimmerman's [[self-regulated-learning|self-regulated learning]] model), [[sociocultural-learning|sociocultural]] theories (N = 3), [[motivation|motivational]] theories (N = 2) and one use of multiple-intelligence theory. The authors attribute [[constructivist|constructivism]]'s dominance to its fit with AI affordances — personalised feedback, tailored [[scaffolding]], autonomy and self-[[self-regulated-learning|regulation]] — and to the authentic-context emphasis shared with intelligent tutoring systems.
4. **Course contextualisation is thinner still.** Only 11 papers (39.29%) addressed further contextual aspects: 7 considered concrete learning objectives tied to curriculum skills such as arithmetic or [[computational-thinking|computational thinking]], and 3 considered [[learning-design]] aspects such as activity type and difficulty, resource types and critical learning moments during enactment. The authors' worked example of the cost: a teacher-facing dashboard that flags struggling students by quiz attempts alone will misfire, because a high attempt count on a difficult task can signal [[desirable-difficulties|productive struggle]] rather than difficulty, generating false alerts and eroding teacher [[trust-calibration|trust]].
5. **Pedagogical purposes are cognitive first, and technical objectives cluster around tutoring and monitoring.** 22 of 28 studies aimed at cognitive purposes (inquiry competences, mathematical or financial skills), 9 at students' affective state (notably AI robots for learners with ASD), and 2 at psychomotor skills. Technologically, 10 tools had tutoring or mentoring purposes, 6 monitoring and analysis for teachers' decision-making, 6 performance prediction, 3 promotion of reflection, 3 [[feedback|feedback]] provision, 2 adaptation and 2 recommendation.
6. **By function, most tools act rather than detect.** Under Detect–Diagnose–Act, 17 studies proposed tools providing pedagogical or didactical action (to students as recommendations, or to teachers as informing and alerting), 8 aimed to diagnose students' current state (profiling, clustering), and 3 to detect learner traces (early-warning dashboards, [[multimodal]] sensing of eye contact, vocal and facial expression and gesture). Social robots were the most common technology (N = 11), ahead of [[virtual-and-augmented-reality|augmented reality]] (3), [[conversational-ai|chatbots]] (3), [[intelligent-tutoring|intelligent tutoring systems]] (3), [[adaptive-learning|adaptive learning]] platforms (2) and one intelligent assessment system.
7. **The users are students, the design input is often absent, and teacher-facing tools are rare.** Students were the end users in 25 studies, teachers in 3 and parents in 1; only 9 studies involved [[stakeholders|stakeholders]] in design at all — 6 with teachers as co-designers, 2 with students, plus single cases consulting parents and administrators, using workshops, interviews, storyboards and co-design sessions. Most technologies were student-facing rather than teacher-facing, which the authors contrast with the equal need for tools that let teachers guide, monitor and adapt AI-generated insight.
8. **Automation sits low, and no study tested the extremes.** Classified against Molenaar's (2022) six-level model, 15 studies proposed partial automation (technology executes specific tasks while the teacher retains control), 7 conditional automation (incidental monitoring with the teacher able to resume control), 5 teacher assistance (technology supplies supportive information), 1 high automation, and **none** at teacher-only or full automation.
9. **Evaluation is [[quantitative-research|quantitative]] and performance-dominated.** 20 studies used quantitative methods, 6 [[qualitative-research|qualitative]] and 1 [[mixed-methods-research|mixed methods]]; experimental designs with randomised assignment were the most common (N = 11, 46.6%, ten with two conditions), alongside pre/post tests (5), statistical analysis (4) and survey-based descriptive analysis (2); qualitative work relied on observation (5), interviews and [[self-report-measures|questionnaires]] (3) and learning artefacts (2). Sample sizes were mostly small: 12 studies under 50 participants, 6 between 50 and 100, 6 between 100 and 500, and only 5 above 500.
10. **Effects are positive on the whole, but heterogeneous and narrowly measured.** Most studies reported significantly positive student effects, some with very large effect sizes (an ITS for argumentation, d = 2.14) and others moderate (immediate tutoring in assignments, d = 0.54); four studies reported moderated or weak effects, and one observed that students still needed the teacher's help to construct concept–representation connections even with technology support. Framed by Henderson et al.'s taxonomy, 14 studies reported learning-outcome effects, 10 affective, 10 [[metacognition|metacognitive]] and only 3 relational — and just **one study** (Holstein et al., 2019, on an AR orchestration tool for prioritising feedback) examined effects on teachers, despite most tools depending on teacher presence.
11. **Geographic skew carries an epistemic risk.** Reading the corpus through Hofstede's cultural dimensions, the authors classify 14 studies as situated in collectivist contexts (China, Sri Lanka, Brazil, Greece, Egypt, Taiwan), where [[collaborative-learning|collaborative]] features and [[group-work|group-based assessment]] norms are emphasised, and 14 in individualistic contexts (United States, Australia, Netherlands, Italy, Sweden), oriented to [[personalized-learning|personalisation]], competition and individual progress. Their concern is transfer: tools developed and tested under one ecocultural paradigm may be imposed on under-represented settings, producing a risk of epistemic bias, so research should include cultural-dimension mapping and participatory or co-design methods that let local educators and learners shape system goals and feedback.
12. **The review's four research implications.** (a) Promote AIEd research in local contexts, including [[culturally-relevant-pedagogy|culturally relevant]] scenarios, support for local and minority languages, and publishing venues that invite work from less-resourced settings; (b) restore the substance of pedagogical and contextual foundations, since theory-based designs make pedagogical decisions and outcomes interpretable where data-driven black boxes do not, and course elements such as activity type and difficulty let tools reason consistently with context; (c) support teachers' presence and autonomy, since AI tools with preset pathways and content can undermine professional expertise, and teachers receive almost no guidance in the reviewed studies on how to interpret data-driven tools or incorporate AI smoothly — a gap the authors link to limited [[ai-literacy|AI literacy]], weak data-interpretation competence and fear for the profession; (d) conduct rigorous evaluation in real-world settings that examines sustained pedagogical effects, because measuring performance alone leaves the emotional, psychomotor and social dimensions of the learning experience unrepresented.

## Study Design & Method

The review follows Kitchenham and Charters' guidelines for systematic literature reviews across search planning, conduct and reporting. Databases covered Scopus, Web of Science, the ACM Library, IEEE Xplore, the *Journal of Learning Analytics* and *IJAIED*; the search combined AI keywords (AI, artificial intelligence, deep learning, [[machine-learning|machine learning]], data mining, [[educational-nlp|natural language processing]]) with technology keywords (learning analytics, adaptive, intelligent, robot, virtual reality, augmented reality) and K-12 terms, restricted to title, abstract and keywords and to 2010 onward, yielding 603 candidate publications that were screened in three rounds down to 28 studies. Inclusion required empirical studies using AI as a tool, in peer-reviewed venues; secondary studies, papers under five pages, project summaries, non-English papers and duplicates were excluded. Coding combined etic categories derived from the five frameworks listed above with emic categories that emerged from the data (student-facing versus teacher-facing tools, course aspects, special-needs targeting, concrete subjects). Trustworthiness measures were peer debriefing on the coding scheme, researcher triangulation, two coders in the first and second screening with joint discussion of ambiguous cases, solo coding in the third, and double-coding of five sample papers to calibrate the codebook.

## Limitations

The review covers 2010–2023, so the post-2023 [[generative-ai]] wave is outside its window — a material constraint given how quickly the technology moves. Only English-language publications were included, which the authors acknowledge may introduce language and geographic bias, and they note that the specific [[research-methods-aied|methodological]] choices of the Kitchenham and Charters approach may have shaped the final pool. The review is descriptive rather than meta-analytic: findings are counts across a heterogeneous set of 28 studies, with no pooled effect estimate, and effect sizes are reported as individual study results rather than synthesised. Coding relied on what papers explicitly reported, so studies that applied pedagogy implicitly would be recorded as ungrounded. The corpus also inherits the field's own weaknesses — small samples, performance-dominated measures, near-absent teacher-outcome data — which bounds what the review can conclude about impact.
