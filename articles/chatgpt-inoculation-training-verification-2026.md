> **Synthesis:** Vu, Cummings, and Park (2026) investigate whether an inoculation message can enhance students' intentions to verify ChatGPT-provided information and encourage actual verification behavior. Using a mixed factorial design (2×2×2: domestic vs. international EFL student status × inoculated vs. non-inoculated × pre-test vs. post-test), they found that a generic inoculation message containing forewarnings prompted students to exhibit higher caution with ChatGPT, evidenced by verification of ChatGPT-generated answers in an academic-source-summary task. Notably, while inoculation did not significantly shift verification *intentions*, it did influence actual *verification behaviors* — which varied by student status and task type, highlighting the complexity of human-AI interaction.

## Key Findings

**Inoculation shifts behavior, not intention.** A generic inoculation message (forewarning) increased students' actual verification of ChatGPT-generated content, even though self-reported verification intentions were not significantly affected — a gap between stated intention and enacted behavior.

**International EFL students especially vulnerable.** Students with high demand for language and academic assistance (e.g., international EFL students) are particularly vulnerable to ChatGPT's risks, including hallucinations, misinformation, and limited advanced problem-solving.

**Task-dependent effects.** The effect of inoculation and student status on verification behavior depended on the type of task, underscoring the need for nuanced, context-aware approaches to promoting critical evaluation of AI output.

**Moderating factors.** Time constraints and trust in ChatGPT were indirect factors that shaped inoculation's effectiveness.

**Pedagogical implication.** The authors offer recommendations for designing inoculation messages and training to support more responsible and safe use of ChatGPT, aligning with the wiki's [[ai-literacy]] and [[reducing-ai-misuse]] concerns.
