> **Synthesis:** Zhu and colleagues asked whether [[generative-ai|ChatGPT]] benefits STEM and non-STEM undergraduates equally in an interdisciplinary course, and their exploratory quasi-experiment answers no. In a two-week digital literacy course at a Singapore university in March 2023, 130 of 183 first- and second-year students worked in deliberately mixed groups on AI and blockchain activities, with some classes using ChatGPT and some not and no training given. [[student-engagement|Physical engagement]] diverged by condition and discipline: STEM students engaged more with ChatGPT, non-STEM students more without it. Non-STEM students reported higher cognitive engagement than STEM students in both conditions. And the notes students wrote were more diverse and more integrated without ChatGPT than with it. The authors frame this as an equity problem rather than a verdict on the tool, and argue that [[digital-divide|disciplinary background]] should be treated as a divide variable that [[ai-literacy|AI integration]] must be designed around.

## Key Findings

1. **Physical engagement split by discipline and condition.** STEM students reported significantly higher physical engagement in the ChatGPT condition, while non-STEM students reported significantly higher physical engagement in the condition without it.
2. **Non-STEM students were more cognitively engaged overall.** The difference held across conditions, which the authors read as effort driven by the topics — AI and blockchain — being further from those students' disciplinary ground.
3. **Notes written without ChatGPT scored higher on interdisciplinary learning quality.** Both diversity of disciplinary perspectives and integration of those perspectives were higher in the non-ChatGPT condition than in the ChatGPT condition.
4. **Mixed groups were used in both conditions.** Each team combined STEM and non-STEM students by design, so the conditions differed in tool access rather than in composition, which is what makes the note-quality gap attributable to the condition.
5. **The study measured engagement by survey and learning quality by coding written posts.** Physical, emotional and cognitive engagement came from student reports, while interdisciplinary learning quality was quantified from their online notes.
6. **The authors recommend designing for the difference, not adopting or rejecting the tool.** Their conclusion is that tool features, instructional strategies and training should be differentiated for STEM and non-STEM students so that generative AI reduces rather than widens learning inequality.

## What the study did

The setting was a required digital literacy course at an autonomous Singapore university, run as two two-hour in-class activities on AI and blockchain, co-designed by the research team and the instructor around real-world tasks — for blockchain, students role-played members of a blockchain association, analyzed cases and negotiated a startup idea. Participants were drawn from four classes with the same instructor: 183 enrolled, 130 consenting and completing measures (mean age 20.67), of whom 72 were in [[stem-education|STEM]] programs (computer science, engineering, environment) and 58 in non-STEM programs (business, humanities and social sciences). Each group of five or six was deliberately mixed across disciplines to create the [[collaborative-learning|interdisciplinary conditions]] the study wanted. ChatGPT was new to students and no training was provided, so the study captured untrained, naturalistic use — and the authors are explicit that the tool in question was GPT-3.5.

## Why the two groups diverged

The discussion offers two readings of the [[student-engagement|engagement]] results. The first is effort compensation: topics like AI and blockchain may sit closer to STEM students' existing knowledge, so non-STEM students had to invest more cognitive work whatever the condition, which matches earlier evidence that interdisciplinary courses can advantage some disciplines while disadvantaging others. The second is resource competition: STEM students who were more absorbed in interacting with ChatGPT may have had fewer cognitive resources left for the task itself, which would explain their higher physical engagement alongside their lower cognitive engagement. The engagement split also speaks to [[digital-divide|digital divide]] research, which the authors note usually measures socio-demographic or literacy factors at the individual level — here the divide variable is the discipline a student studies, inside a single classroom, which local equity policy rarely captures. The note-quality result points the same way: students without the tool drew on and integrated more disciplinary perspectives than those with it.

## What this means for practice

- **Instructors.** Do not treat generative AI as an equal-opportunity scaffold in an interdisciplinary course; the students it helped engage were the ones already closer to the material, so check who the tool is actually serving before making it mandatory.
- **Curriculum designers.** Design activity-level supports by disciplinary background — additional grounding for non-STEM students in technical topics, and prompts that keep STEM students on the interdisciplinary task rather than inside the tool.
- **Administrators.** If AI is adopted for equity reasons, this study is evidence to measure rather than assume: engagement and work quality moved in opposite directions by discipline here, which no institution-level policy review would have predicted.
- **Researchers.** Disciplinary background deserves a place in the [[equity-in-ai-education|equity]] variables of AI-in-education research, next to gender, geography and literacy; the paper also calls for logging how students actually interact with the AI in mixed groups, which this design did not capture.

## Limitations

- A strictly controlled design was not possible: the instructor wanted every class to experience ChatGPT, so condition sizes were uneven and individual students' condition was not stable across the two weeks, with robust ANOVA used to compensate.
- 130 first- and second-year students in one course at one Singapore university, across two activities on two topics, so the contextual features limit generalization.
- [[self-report-measures|Self-reported]] engagement plus coded notes, with no test of learning gains and no logs of how students used the tool, so the mechanism behind the engagement split remains inferred.
- The technology was GPT-3.5, the version current in March 2023, and the authors caution that later models are substantially more capable, which dates the negative note-quality result in particular.
