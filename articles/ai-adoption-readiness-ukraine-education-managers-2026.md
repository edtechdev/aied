> **Synthesis:** Kremen and colleagues (2026) surveyed 395 education managers across 23 Ukrainian regions in September 2025 to measure AI adoption [[governance|readiness]] in a post-Soviet transitional context. They found a significant gap between personal readiness (M=3.83, SD=0.96) and institutional readiness (M=3.15, SD=0.93) — a 0.68-point gap (d=0.73) — with [[regulation|regulatory]] absence and [[digital-divide|digital]] competency gaps as the most prevalent barriers. Latent Class Analysis identified six provisional manager typologies, and fuzzy-set [[qualitative-research|Qualitative]] Comparative Analysis found that no single barrier was necessary or sufficient for low readiness. The authors argue for differentiated, typology-based training and stronger regulatory frameworks so that managers — the decision-makers who control how AI enters practice — can lead evidence-based adoption.

## Key Findings
1. **A 0.68-point personal–institutional readiness gap.** Personal readiness (M=3.83) significantly exceeded institutional readiness (M=3.15) on the same scale, confirmed by a Wilcoxon signed-rank test (p<.001, d=0.73).
2. **Regulatory absence and competency gaps are the top barriers.** Lack of a clear regulatory framework and gaps in digital competency were the most prevalent obstacles reported by the 395 managers across 23 regions.
3. **Managers split into six distinct typologies.** Latent Class Analysis yielded six provisional manager profiles, with a "Competency-constrained" class as the largest, revealing heterogeneous readiness rather than a single population.
4. **Low readiness is not driven by any one barrier.** fsQCA found no barrier necessary and no configuration sufficient for low AI readiness, so weak adoption has diffuse rather than configurational causes.
5. **[[generative-ai|ChatGPT]] was the most widely adopted tool**, and digital competency showed the largest training gap, pointing policy at capacity building as much as at regulation.
6. **Managers are more personally than institutionally ready.** Higher personal than system readiness signals willing leaders constrained by their institutions.

## Managers as the Gatekeepers of AI Adoption

The paper starts from a premise the [[ai-education|AI-in-education]] governance literature often underplays: education managers are the decision-makers who decide whether and how AI enters daily educational practice, so their readiness is not a peripheral staff concern but a precondition for evidence-based policy. Studying them in Ukraine matters because the country sits in a post-Soviet transitional setting where regulatory infrastructure for AI is still embryonic, and where [[educational-policy-ai|policy]] must be built from the ground up. The authors treat readiness as two layered constructs — personal willingness to engage with AI and the system-level capacity of institutions to support it — and their central measurement finding is that these layers diverge sharply.

## Six Typologies and Diffuse Barriers

Rather than assuming a single adoption profile, the study used person-centered Latent Class Analysis and identified six provisional manager typologies, with the Competency-constrained class the largest — managers who want AI but lack the skills to operationalize it. The configurational follow-up (fsQCA) is notable for a null result with policy teeth: because no single barrier was necessary or sufficient for low readiness, there is no lone "fix" to administer. The pattern suggests that [[technology-acceptance-model|adoption]] is held back by a diffuse combination of missing regulation, shallow skills, and institutional constraints, which is why the authors argue for simultaneous action on regulatory frameworks, institutional capacity, and differentiated training timed to each typology.

## What this means for practice
- **Administrators.** Measure personal and institutional AI readiness as separate constructs — the personal–institutional gap is the actionable signal, and leaders who are personally ready may still be institutionally blocked.
- **Administrators and policymakers.** Treat regulatory absence and digital-competency gaps as the binding constraints and build [[governance]] frameworks alongside capacity-building, since no single barrier explains low readiness.
- **Policymakers.** Differentiate [[educational-development|professional development]] by manager typology rather than offering one generic AI training, because the six LCA profiles expose heterogeneous needs.

## Limitations
- The study relies on a [[self-report-measures|self-report]] survey of 395 managers, so readiness is measured as perceived rather than observed capacity or actual deployment behavior.
- Data were collected in September 2025 across 23 Ukrainian regions amid an ongoing war, which constrains both sample balance and generalization to non-transitional or non-conflict settings.
- The six LCA profiles are described as provisional and were not validated against external outcomes such as implemented AI initiatives or student learning results.
