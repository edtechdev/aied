> **Synthesis:** Using survey data from 327 Chinese EFL undergraduates and partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM), this study tests whether [[ai-literacy|AI prompting literacy]] or top-down external mandates better drive deep-revision engagement in AI-assisted [[writing-education|academic writing]]. It finds that prompting literacy predicts deep revision through the parallel psychological mediators of perceived competence, intrinsic motivation, and psychological safety — while external mandates show no direct effect — reframing AI adoption from enforcement to [[motivation|internal need satisfaction]].

## Key Findings

- **Prompting literacy is the core driver.** AI Prompting Literacy (APL) significantly predicted Perceived Competence (β = 0.636, t = 24.387, p < 0.001), Intrinsic Motivation (β = 0.439, t = 7.050, p < 0.001), and Psychological Safety (β = 0.657, t = 17.093, p < 0.001), supporting H1–H3.
- **External mandates have no direct effect.** The direct path from External Mandate (EM) to Deep Revision Engagement was non-significant (β = −0.013, t = 0.165, p = 0.869, f² = 0.000), failing to support H7.
- **All three psychological needs partially mediate the APL–engagement link.** Significant indirect effects ran through Perceived Competence (β = 0.139, p = 0.003), Intrinsic Motivation (β = 0.197, p < 0.001), and Psychological Safety (β = 0.119, p = 0.009) — supporting the full parallel mediation model.
- **Intrinsic Motivation is the strongest direct driver of deep revision.** It predicted Deep Revision Engagement (β = 0.448, t = 6.765, p < 0.001) with a moderate effect size (f² = 0.251), compared with weaker effects for Perceived Competence (β = 0.218, p = 0.003) and Psychological Safety (β = 0.181, p = 0.008).
- **The model explains substantial variance.** The structural model accounted for 50.0% of the variance in Deep Revision Engagement (R² = 0.500), with APL exerting strong effects on Psychological Safety (f² = 0.761) and Perceived Competence (f² = 0.680).

## Study Design & Method

The study employed a cross-sectional, [[quantitative-research|quantitative]] survey design with purposive sampling of 327 EFL undergraduates recruited from two Chinese [[higher-ed|higher education]] institutions (an application-oriented institute of technology and a regional normal university). The sample was 45.9% male and 54.1% female, with a mean age of 19.62 years (SD = 0.84); 380 questionnaires were collected and 53 purged (attention-check failures, sub-90-second completions, and response patterning), leaving an 86.05% effective recovery rate that exceeded the G-Power minimum of 129.

Data were analyzed in SmartPLS 4 with partial least squares structural equation modeling, justified as exploratory and appropriate for the complex parallel-mediation structure and non-normal data; significance was assessed with 10,000-resample bootstrapping, and the fitted model met PLS-SEM thresholds (SRMR = 0.096; all inner VIF values 1.000–2.230; highest HTMT = 0.742; CR and AVE above 0.70 and 0.50). Instruments were adapted from established scales — APL from Su et al. (2023), DRE from Yuan et al. (2024), and SDT constructs from classic motivation and educational-technology research — while a new three-item EM scale was developed to capture mandated AI use in grading; all scales were piloted on 105 EFL students and showed strong reliability (Cronbach's α: APL 0.859, PC 0.799, IM 0.960, PS 0.879, DRE 0.844, EM 0.880).

## Implications

- **Shift policy focus from enforcement to capability-building.** Because compliance-driven mandates alone did not elicit deep cognitive engagement, institutions should complement [[governance]] with dedicated AI-prompting workshops and [[agency|autonomy]]-supportive environments rather than monitoring-centric usage metrics or [[ai-detection]] software.
- **Teach prompting as a cognitive support strategy, not a shortcut.** The proposed "Collaborative Human-AI Revision Design" explicitly embeds [[prompt-engineering|prompt engineering]] across drafting, feedback, and rewriting phases — e.g., constraint-based prompts and criteria-specific diagnostic prompts positioned to treat AI as a [[formative-assessment|formative]] peer reviewer rather than an authoritative oracle.
- **Move assessment toward process and [[metacognition]].** [[summative-assessment|Summative]] rubrics that heavily penalize linguistic inaccuracy may nudge students toward "algorithmic dependence"; process-oriented tools such as "Prompt Reflection [[eportfolio|Portfolios]]" that reward [[productive-failure|productive failure]] could sustain intrinsic motivation and [[critical-thinking|critical thinking]].
- **Curricula should treat human–AI collaboration as a core literacy.** Evaluating the quality of students' iterative dialogue with AI, rather than only the final product, aligns grading with the psychological drivers the model identified.

## Limitations

- **Cross-sectional, single-source [[self-report-measures|self-report data]]** make the findings susceptible to Common Method Bias; the authors interpret all mediation paths as model-dependent indirect associations rather than causal or chronological links.
- **No demographic moderators modeled** — the study does not test moderating paths or multi-group variance (e.g., gender differences) within the structural model.
- **Quantitative scales may miss dynamic nuance** — deep revision is a complex, iterative cognitive process, and self-report instruments could not capture the full dynamics of human–computer interaction.
- **Single-context generalization** — findings are based on Chinese EFL freshmen and sophomores, limiting cross-cultural applicability.
