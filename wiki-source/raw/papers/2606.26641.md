Current dialogue systems, powered by large language models, often treat empathy as essential without assessing its true impact,
especially in behavior change, where motivation and adherence often depend on subtle user-chatbot dynamics. We examine this
assumption by building three WhatsApp physical-activity (PA) coaching chatbots that differ only in empathy level and evaluating
them in a six-week within-subject study (N = 13). Participants struggled to distinguish between the empathy conditions, and the
non-empathetic version was often rated as more engaging and useful. However, higher-empathy variants were still associated with a
larger overall average increase in step counts and faster improvement in intention to follow advice. These results suggest empathy’s
role is nuanced: it may be hard for lay users to identify explicitly, but it can still shape motivation and trust that support sustained
change. We interpret this pattern through the Elaboration Likelihood Model’s peripheral route. We highlight design implications for
building next-generation PA coaching chatbots that balance effectiveness with human-like connection.

Introduction

Incorporating empathy [11] into conversational systems has been a heavily studied topic [37, 52, 67]. Empathy can
enhance trust and long-term engagement [3], reduce depression symptoms in mental health support [18], and facilitate
the resilience process and enhance well-being [22]. As a result, many applications of interactive systems emphasize
empathy as a core design principle. One of these applications is Physical Activity (PA) coaching chatbots. These digital
systems, developed by researchers to promote behavioral change [31, 48], demonstrate promising potential in improving
physical activity and diet adherence [57] and combat the inaccessibility of in-person PA counseling.
Most existing digital PA coaching systems utilize older natural language processing technologies. Nowadays, as
LLMs shift the interaction paradigm, current research still focuses only on the initial consultation session in their
design, neglecting sustained engagement and adherence [24]. Furthermore, many existing coaching systems utilize
motivational interviewing techniques [41], often emphasizing empathetic responses, as guidelines for chatbot behaviors.
From a theoretical perspective, higher degrees of empathy may enhance the social presence of the chatbot [5], thereby
promoting self-motivation through improved relatedness (self-determination theory [14]), which in turn drives sustained
behavioral change. The effect of empathy alone as an isolated independent variable has not been sufficiently studied in
prior literature under longer-term digital PA counseling system settings. Understanding this effect is crucial because
it can inform the design of more effective coaching systems, clarifying whether improvements in engagement and
adherence rely on empathetic responses themselves or other counseling strategies.
To isolate the effect of chatbot empathy on participant outcomes, we construct versions of a personalized, LLM-based
physical activity coaching chatbot with varying degrees of empathy. Our chatbots engage with users through an initial
consultation session and then a series of maintenance sessions. The initial session aims to acquaint the user with
the chatbot, get to understand the user’s situation, and provide tailored action plans. The maintenance sessions are
designed to be more effective in engaging users through employing personalization, which has been shown to enhance
engagement and satisfaction [19]. A total of three chatbot versions were designed for our study: Non-Empathetic,
Standard, and Empathetic. The Non-Empathetic version is prompted not to respond empathetically; the Standard
version leverages the emergent capabilities of LLMs to express empathy [38]; the Empathetic version identifies and
responds empathetically to Empathy Opportunities in user responses in maintenance sessions (all sessions after the
initial consultation session). Here, the notion of Empathy Opportunities (EOs) refers to cues that the user would provide
for others to respond empathetically, such as negative self-judgment (e.g. I could have done better) [50]. Upon identifying

these opportunities, the Empathetic chatbot would respond empathetically in a manner similar to how healthcare
professionals would respond to these messages, guided by study data from Rey Velasco et al. [54]. Our Empathetic
chatbot therefore employs clinical empathy, contrasting with the general empathy embodied by the Standard chatbot.
We hypothesize that this theory-grounded mechanism for conveying empathy may strengthen the relationship
between the user and the chatbot, making the users more engaged and more likely to heed the chatbot’s suggestions.
Specifically, receiving empathy from the chatbot may boost the user’s self-efficacy and enhance the user’s trust in the
chatbot, resulting in overall increased PA levels and better engagement through improved rapport [43] over multiple
interactions. We propose the following research question:
RQ: Does adopting a clinically-informed empathetic framework in a PA coaching chatbot positively
influence user outcomes (including overall PA level and self-efficacy) and chatbot perception under a
multi-session setting?
To investigate this research question, we conducted a within-subject user study (N = 13) over six weeks. We measure
self-efficacy, the user’s relationship quality with the chatbot, and physical activity in terms of step counts, along with
other metrics, using self-report questionnaires.
Our results indicate that, even when participants often struggle to differentiate between the chatbot versions
throughout the weeks, their behavior is still influenced by the presence of empathy. While the Non-Empathetic chatbot
can be perceived as more engaging and useful than the Standard and Empathetic variants, the Empathetic chatbot
increases participant intention to follow chatbot advice and self-efficacy significantly faster, and is associated with
higher trustworthiness compared to the Non-Empathetic version. Additionally, initiating the six-week interaction with
the Empathetic chatbot results in the least attrition. This work underscores the nuanced and positive effect of empathy
on behavioral change. The contribution of this work is as follows:
(1) We incorporate a clinical empathy framework informed by Rey Velasco et al. [54] and Pounds [50] into a PA
coaching chatbot.
(2) We systematically examine the isolated effect of empathy using three PA chatbot variants using a within-subject,
six-week-long user study.
(3) We reveal that focusing solely on reported perceived empathy from non-expert participants for chatbot assessment
may not be a reliable method.
(4) We present design considerations for future LLM-powered PA coaching chatbots.

Related Work

2.1

Chatbots for Physical Activity Coaching

Prior to modern-day transformer-based LLMs, chatbots designed for Physical Activity (PA) coaching relied on fixed and
templated responses and workflows through intent recognition [34, 60], which can be overly rigid and generalized.
Process evaluations and component analyses further highlight engagement challenges, such as participant frustration
from communication failures [13, 34] and attrition [32]. Even with these limitations, systematic reviews on these
pre-LLM chatbots reveal that they are still efficacious in elevating PA levels, with an average effect of +735 steps a day
across 19 trials [57]. It is plausible that LLM-powered chatbots, now with potential for higher flexibility and personalized
support [66], would be able to better enhance the positive outcomes from interacting with these chatbots.
More recently, PA coaching chatbot designs have been incorporating modern LLMs to varying degrees. Vandelanotte
et al. [62] discusses the implementation of MoveMentor, a chatbot capable of providing just-in-time interventions.

Despite utilizing LLMs to generate question-answer pairs relevant to their use case, the authors do not directly leverage
LLMs for handling all interactions, but instead an intent-based retriever. This prevents MoveMentor from being able to
utilize the full range of LLM capabilities, especially since LLM generations may lack overall diversity [64].
One of the first fully LLM-based PA chatbots is GPTCoach [25]. This system implements the onboarding session
using Motivational Interviewing (MI), a core empathetic technique leveraged for behavioral change coaching [6, 41].
GPTCoach additionally provides visualizations of historical PA data to enrich its advice. The authors highlight the need
to cater LLM-based coaching chatbot advice to each individual participant, which we address in our design through
personalized action planning. A limitation of this work is that GPTCoach only supports the initial consultation session,
leaving the longer-term efficacy of LLM-based chatbots underexplored.
Providing data-driven guidelines for designing PA coaching chatbots, Liang et al. [35] examines a set of human PA
counseling dialogues. The authors categorized utterances into counseling strategies and topics. They discover that
barriers and motivation are heavily discussed during these counseling sessions, and that more barrier-relevant strategies
from the counselor is correlated with improvements in the barrier survey.
Generally, we see that most chatbots for positive behavioral change are yet to embrace state-of-the-art NLP technologies. There is additionally a lack of longer-term evaluations for such chatbots employing modern LLMs. Our work
bridges this important gap by studying the longer-term impact of an LLM-based physical activity coaching chatbot.
2.2

Role of Empathy in Behavioral Change

Empathy has been a robust relational factor in counseling. Therapist empathy exhibits a moderately strong correlation
with better client outcomes [16]. In the behavioral change context, Motivational Interviewing (MI) is a common approach
that underlines empathy as its core principle. MI has been shown to significantly increase the self-efficacy of type 2
diabetes patients [20] and promote weight loss and self-efficacy improvement among adolescents [63].
In digital counseling systems, empathy is frequently a design principle but remains under-isolated as a causal factor.
Existing empathetic conversational agents for behavioral change employ rule- and template-based approaches [40],
often constrained to multiple-choice input, and have short study durations and limited personalization [39].
Early empathetic embodied agents are perceived positively but rarely tested for long-term effects on motivation
and self-efficacy. ODVIC is a seminal work in incorporating explicit verbal and facial empathy while delivering brief
MI-style interventions for excessive drinking [36]. Participants perceived the empathetic agent to be more human-like
and exhibited higher levels of acceptance, yet outcomes such as sustained motivation or self-efficacy were not evaluated
longitudinally. Relational-agent work similarly demonstrates that social-emotional dialog and empathic behaviors can
increase alliance and short-term PA (e.g., increased walking among older adults) [3, 4]. Rahmanti et al. [51] proposes
SlimeMe, a weight management chatbot equipped with artificial empathy through text-based emotion analysis coupled
with emojis and other emotional chatbot responses, implemented with the intention-based DialogFlow framework.
Participants perceived SlimeMe to be exciting and engaging after a 7-day simulation trial. Recent counseling systems
begin to integrate LLMs and to target empathy in a more flexible manner, but they are still in an early stage. An MI
chatbot with generative reflections produced larger short-term improvement in readiness and confidence for smoking
cessation compared to its non-generative counterpart [8]. Still, longer study durations and rigorous testing are still
required to examine the effectiveness of empathy in behavioral change coaching systems.
Closer to physical activity and empathy, Chauvin et al. [10] closely examines the effect of empathy on physical activity
among older French adults. The authors devise a set of three interventions, two with non-directive MI interventions
and one that directs the conversation using MI principles. The non-directive MI virtual agent is perceived as more

Design Principles

Prior work largely reports high-level guidelines and prompts but rarely specifies utterance-level behavior or provides
open-source implementations. As a result, our implementation of the physical coaching chatbot is constructed from
scratch, rather than extending an existing PA chatbot implementation. We derive our design principles based on the
behavioral change literature.
• Principle 1. Theory-Grounded Implementation: Identifying and implementing theory-grounded interventions strengthens the construct validity of our design. We will utilize theoretical frameworks such as motivational
interviewing when implementing the chatbot, similar to prior PA chatbot implementations [10, 24]. Additionally, current implementations of empathetic conversational systems for behavioral change are not sufficiently
grounded in operationalized empathy in the physical activity coaching context. We will bridge this gap by
employing a clinical empathy framework from Pounds [50].
• Principle 2. Design in accordance with Human PA Counseling: Human PA counseling and therapy have
been more extensively studied than their digital counterparts. Due to the increasing anthropomorphism toward
LLMs such as ChatGPT [21, 49], it is possible that transplanting successful human PA counseling strategies to
chatbot PA counseling scenarios would yield successful results. Using inspection results from Liang et al. [35],
we will place emphasis on addressing barriers and motivation in our initial session. In maintenance sessions, we
will aim to incorporate chatbot questions that elicit discussion of barriers and motivation.
• Principle 3. Better Personalization for Enhanced Long-Term Engagement: Prior work has indicated that
the rigidity of pre-LLM chatbots suffers from attrition issues. As our work aims to study longer-term effects,
sustained engagement from the users is desirable, which can be addressed through higher application flexibility
(already handled by LLM emergent capabilities) and through better personalization [19].
• Principle 4. Experimentally Controllable Empathy: We will hold the core content of the chatbot consistent
across the three conditions, such that the primary difference between them will be stylistic differences in exhibited
and perceived empathy. This can be achieved through consistent system prompts throughout different chatbot
versions.

Chatbot System Design

In this section, we detail the construction of our physical activity coach chatbot. All chatbot variants use the same
base model (GPT-4o). We chose a general-purpose LLM rather than specialized fine-tuned models because public
physical-activity coaching dialogues are limited, keeping the base model constant controls for capability differences
across conditions, and our study requires multiple variants. This also enables rapid iteration while reducing the risk
that fine-tuning would introduce confounds beyond the targeted empathic features. Our Empathetic chatbot features a
clinical empathy module; the other two versions do not have access to this module. Instead, we instruct GPT-4o not
to be empathetic when responding as the Non-Empathetic chatbot; for the Standard chatbot, we leverage the same

Since personalization has been shown to facilitate long-term engagement [19], the three chatbot versions also
incorporate personalization. Personalization is realized in multiple ways in our chatbot, after considering approaches to
benefiting PA plan adherence. First, our chatbot provides personalized action planning tailored to the user’s specific

to users using short micro-interventions [23, 45] for users who are experiencing excess stress, as stress reduction has
been shown to increase PA [58]. Thirdly, our chatbot retains past conversations in a condensed format and retrieves the
last three conversations to propose a pertinent discussion topic during maintenance sessions, to center the conversation
on the user.
We implement our chatbot as a WhatsApp bot with Twilio integrations. Reminder messages for engaging with the
WhatsApp chatbot are sent through SMS texts every 48 hours. We had to resort to this less integrated design choice
because Twilio no longer supports scheduled WhatsApp messages more than 24 hours in advance.
4.1

Designing the Initial Session

In the initial session, the chatbot follows the Motivational Interviewing procedur [41]. The Empathetic and Standard
chatbot versions share the prompts for this session, and we do not activate the clinical empathy modules in this session.
The Non-Empathetic chatbot uses a gently rephrased system prompt that removes the requirement for empathy in the
responses.
Following Principle 2, we address barriers and motivation directly in our initial session. The guiding principles for
the four stages of the initial session are as follows:
(1) Engaging: Getting to know the user, forming rapport.
(2) Focusing: Identifying barriers, exploring possible solutions, tailoring suggestions to the user’s personal situation
and preferences.
(3) Evoking: Reinforcing and enhancing motivation.
(4) Planning: Decide and discuss a feasible short-term action plan.

4.2

Designing the Maintenance Sessions

Figure 2 indicates the differences between the three chatbot versions when processing incoming user messages during
the maintenance session. The general maintenance workflow is held consistent among all chatbot versions. We include
example conversations for each of the chatbots in Figure 4.
After receiving the SMS reminder message, the user would first send a pre-defined message to trigger an LLM-driven
summarization process, which condenses the previous conversation into user-centric bullet points. Using these, GPT-4o
would generate a personalized ping message. A potential topic to discuss, as well as any additional relevant context,
would then be incorporated into the chatbot’s context window (Section 4.2.1). The chatbot would finally transition into
asking the user a set of check-in questions.
When designing these check-in questions, we aim to create questions that may elicit negative self-disclosure. This is
to create more naturalistic Empathy Opportunities for the Clinical Empathy modules (Section 4.2.2). We arrived at the
same three questions for each maintenance session:
(1) How have you been for the last two days?
(2) Were you able to perform the physical activity previously discussed?
(3) How were you feeling emotionally when you were doing the physical activities?
Finally, the chatbot would ask the user to rate their stress level on a scale of 0-5 and provide stress-relief microinterventions if the stress level is 2 or higher (Section 4.2.3).
4.2.1 Long-term Memory Module. The Long-term Memory module maintains all bullet-point summaries, including the
dates, of previous conversation sessions. Summaries are embedded using Sentence Transformers [53] and stored in a
FAISS index [15] for easy retrieval.
Prior to sending the initial ping message in every maintenance session, we would prompt GPT-4o to determine a
suitable discussion topic for the chatbot for this upcoming session, using the bullet point summaries of the previous
three sessions. Using this suggested topic, we would then retrieve the top 3 - 5 relevant summary bullet points.

4.2.2 Clinical Empathy Module. The definition of our chatbot’s empathy framework is informed by a digital health study
[54] investigating how healthcare professionals respond to messages from patients in a healthy lifestyle intervention
program. The authors first translate the definitions of Empathy Opportunities (EOs) and response strategies documented
in Pounds [50] into the context of behavioral change, then annotate sentences from patient messages for the EOs they
exhibit at a phrasal granularity. Afterwards, healthcare professionals responded to individual sentences presented in the
messages. Finally, the authors mapped the healthcare professionals’ response strategies back to the response strategies
from Pounds [50]. This mapping forms the backbone of how our Empathetic chatbot responds to participants.
To integrate this framework into our chatbot design, we created three submodules: Empathy Opportunity Classifier,
Strategy Sampler, and Instruction Generator. See an overview of this workflow in Figure 5. We discuss the specific
technical instantiation of our module in further detail in Appendix D.

Empathy Opportunity Classifier: Given a user message, we would first need to identify the Empathy Opportunities
(EOs) present. We optimized a GPT-4o-mini-based classifier that achieves a top-three classification accuracy of 85.3%.
Strategy Sampler: We created EO-specific strategy distributions using mappings between EOs and empathetic response
strategies using statistics from Rey Velasco et al. [54] (e.g., when the user explicitly exhibits negative feelings, the
healthcare professional responds with neutral support and explicit appreciation 37.5% of the time). These distributions
are then combined and sampled from, given the top-three EO predictions from the EO Classifier.
Instruction Generator: Finally, we create an instruction for GPT-4o to utilize descriptions of the selected empathetic
response strategies. The instruction contains descriptions of the chosen strategies (modified from Pounds [50]).
4.2.3 Stress Relief Module. The link between PA and stress is a reciprocal one: PA reduces stress, while more stress often
predicts decreased physical activity [58]. As a means to provide more varied content to keep users better engaged, and to
help them achieve their physical activity goals, we design the following stress relief module to provide in-conversation
stress-relief micro-interventions. These micro-interventions follow the format of Prompt + URL [45], and research
indicates that even brief interventions can be effective in significantly reducing perceived stress [65]. Methodologically,
just-in-time adaptive interventions and microrandomized trials provide frameworks for context-sensitive delivery and
causal optimization in mobile settings [29, 30, 42].
We manually curate 23 micro-interventions spanning four primary categories: relaxation, response modulation
(e.g., cognitive change), positive experiences, and resource buffers [23]. See high-level descriptions of the categories in
Appendix A. Similar to prior work [1], we employ a multi-armed bandit approach to select the optimal micro-intervention
for the current user, and then utilize retrieval-augmented generation to generate the response.
At the end of the maintenance sessions, the chatbot would prompt the user to provide a rating of their perceived
stress on a scale of 0 - 5. If the current perceived stress is higher than 2, a micro-intervention will be administered
through the multi-armed bandit. Afterwards, the user would again be asked to rate the effectiveness of the intervention
on a scale of 1 through 10, which would be used to update the weights for the bandit.

Pilot Study

We conducted a between-subject pilot study with 21 participants through Prolific to explore whether different versions
of the chatbot have short-term effects on self-efficacy in participants, while also ensuring the workflow functions as
intended. There were eight participants in the Non-Empathetic condition, four in the Standard condition, and nine in
the Empathetic condition. The distribution of participants was uneven because our primary interest was contrasting
between the Empathetic and Non-Empathetic conditions. See more details about this pilot study in Appendix E.
5.1

Study Design

The study simulates a user’s first two interactions with our coaching chatbot. After completing a pre-survey, participants
undergo an initial chatbot session, followed by a maintenance session a few minutes later, simulating that two days
have passed and they have not met their physical activity goals. The study concludes with a post-survey:
• System Usability Scale [7] with the word “Computer System” replaced with “Chatbot”.
• Chatbot Conversation Quality: These questions are used to assess whether the chatbot responses have
sufficiently high quality.
(1) Did the chatbot provide naturalistic responses? (1 - The chatbot did not provide any naturalistic response; 5 The chatbot always provided naturalistic responses)
(2) How can the chatbot be improved? Did anything feel odd or confusing during the conversation?
(3) What part of the conversation did you like the most?
(4) What part of the conversation did you dislike?
• Conversation Outcome: These Likert-style questions are reused for the check-in forms during our official user
study. The questions are on a scale from Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree.
(1) Intention to follow: I intend to follow the chatbot’s recommendation over the next 7 days.
(2) Self-efficacy: If I do intend to follow the chatbot’s recommendation, I am confident that I can follow the
recommendation over the next 7 days.
(3) Recommendation Helpfulness: I think the chatbot’s recommendation is useful for enhancing my physical
activity.
5.2

Results

Surprisingly, the Non-Empathetic chatbot was rated more favorably on several usability dimensions. However, due
to the small sample size, we present these results descriptively (means/medians) in Table 9 and do not consider them
conclusive. The Non-Empathetic chatbot was generally rated as more enjoyable and satisfying, while the Empathetic
chatbot was rated as easier to use.
Regarding the most enjoyable aspects of their sessions, multiple participants highlighted the stress-relief component.
Many also appreciated personalized advice, suggesting that these modules enhance engagement.
We notice that chatbot perception is influenced by significant individual variations. All three versions of the chatbot
can be perceived as friendly and even empathetic according to participant free-form feedback. However, participants
place emphasis differently when describing these versions. Both the Empathetic and the Standard chatbots received the
descriptor “like chatting with a friend", while the Empathetic chatbot is praised for its positive energy (“it genuinely
cares about your well-being”).

Pilot Study Takeaways

The pilot study challenges a common assumption that users always prefer empathetic coaching chatbots. Findings
suggest people vary in detecting empathy and have different preferences for empathy and personalization. Based on
this, we added pre-survey questions about desired empathy and personalization levels for the main study.
The study further highlights how individual differences can affect results. To address this, we chose a within-subject
design to better compare the impact of different empathetic conditions.

Longitudinal User Study

To examine our hypotheses about the effect of empathy on user engagement, self-efficacy, and physical activity when
interacting with a physical activity coach chatbot, we conducted a within-subject, six-week user study. Our study is
approved by our home institution’s IRB.

(1) Step Count: What has been your average step count for the past two days?
(2) Intention to follow: I intend to follow the chatbot’s recommendation over the next 7 days.
(3) Self-efficacy: If I do intend to follow the chatbot’s recommendation, I am confident that I can follow the
recommendation over the next 7 days.
(4) Chatbot Usefulness: I think the chatbot’s recommendation is useful for enhancing my physical activity.
For the check-in form, we aimed for a low-friction experience and therefore resorted to customized measures rather
than longer standardized ones. Future work reproducing this study should consider leveraging standardized measures
for better robustness.
We emphasized that participants have a means to track their steps in our instructional emails, aiming to improve the
accuracy of self-reported step count tracking.
Additionally, participants complete a weekly reflection form on their experience from the previous week:
(1) Multi-Dimensional Measure of Trust [61], shortened.
(2) User Engagement Scale - Short Form [44], shortened.
(3) System Usability Scale with the word “Computer System” replaced with “Chatbot”.
(4) Freeform text feedback (optional).
6.2

Participant Recruitment

We recruited participants through social media and through emailing computer science students at the authors’ home
institution. In this study, we focus on inactive but motivated participants (those answering B-C to Question 2 in the
pre-survey about their stage of behavior change). 106 users entered the pre-survey, and we narrowed it down to 33
after filtering. Participant compensation is $60; dropped-out participants are compensated proportional to the duration
they were enrolled.
Our study began with 33 users. 16 of them dropped out before interacting with all three versions of the chatbot,
3 did not fill out surveys consistently, and 1 was excluded due to incorrect initial setup. Our analysis focused on the
remaining 13 users (10 Females, 3 Males, aged 19-30) with high-quality data. More analysis about our attrition issue in
Section 6.3.4.
Our participants’ demographics are listed in Table 1. We include additional information about our participants’
familiarity with LLM chatbots and their attitudes toward Generative AI in Appendix F. We see that the majority of
our participants are women and young age groups, which we acknowledge as a limitation in our user study. While
participants have more variation in preferences for empathy, we observe that most participants share a positive attitude
toward personalization.

These findings suggest that while changes in the empathetic condition did not consistently shape participants’
perceptions, repeated interaction over time can be associated with modest improvements in chatbot perception. This
pattern is consistent with the idea that continued exposure builds rapport, even in the absence of strong effects from
explicit empathy manipulations. Combined with the outcomes from the check-in forms administered immediately
post-conversation, we hypothesize that participants may not consciously register the differences between empathetic
conditions, yet could still experience benefits from interacting with more empathetic versions of the chatbot.

6.3.4 Attrition Analysis. Our user study has experienced significant attrition, with 18 participants failing to complete
all six weeks of the study. This could be linked to the repetitive nature of our chatbot, which has been shown in prior
work to be associated with high attrition [32]. We review the empathetic condition of the final conversations conducted
by the dropped-out participants. The Non-Empathetic condition is associated with the highest percentage of
drop-out participants (50%), followed by the Standard condition (33%), and finally the Empathetic condition (17%).
Inspecting the pre-survey results from participants who dropped out, we do not observe any systematic differences.

adhering to the designed conversation flows, participants have attempted to discuss different topics with the chatbot.
This was especially prominent in the earlier stage of the user study, where the end-of-conversation Google Form had not
been enforced, and participants could freely continue the conversations beyond the predefined dialogue flow. During
the study, one of the researchers monitored conversations daily and recorded notable dialogues.
Participants have discussed meal-prepping advice given specific dietary restrictions, maintaining sleep hygiene and
routines, and using apps to aid their fitness journey. Participants have additionally sought health advice, such as seeing
a chiropractor. As a result of our chatbot containing a stress relief component, a few users talk with the chatbot about
mental health issues unprompted:
