Abstract
Parent–child tutoring is a collaborative learning setting with asymmetric roles, where parents guide children’s problem solving while
children engage in understanding and reasoning. However, most
LLM-based learning systems are designed for either single users
or symmetric collaboration, leaving parent–child tutoring with distinct instructional roles underexplored. Through a formative study,
we find that effective parent–child tutoring depends on preserving these distinct roles, with parents guiding the learning process
and children remaining actively engaged in reasoning. We also
identify recurring challenges when parents struggle to understand
problem structure, lack sufficient knowledge to provide support,
or encounter communication difficulties that disrupt shared understanding. To address these challenges, we present ParaTutor,
a scaffolding system that provides different forms of support to
parents and children. ParaTutor supports parents with guidance for
tutoring and provides children with visual grounding for problem
solving. We evaluate ParaTutor with 23 parent–child dyads (children aged 10–12) under four tutoring conditions that vary how LLM
assistance is delivered. Results show that generic LLM assistance
tends to reduce the parent’s role in tutoring, whereas ParaTutor
better preserves parent-led support and sustains children’s participation in reasoning. These findings suggest that in multi-user
learning, the value of LLM support depends not only on model

Introduction

Large language models’ (LLMs) outstanding reasoning ability may
transform the paradigm of parental tutoring in math. By delivering
explanations and feedback in a natural, conversational format, LLMs
make complex concepts more accessible, lowering the cognitive
barrier for learners [36, 45, 61]. In particular, LLMs can dynamically
adapt their responses with complexity and pedagogical style to meet
the needs of individual learners [17, 63]. Using LLMs to improve
collaboration between parents and children in home tutoring is still
developing. Studies on parental involvement have primarily focused
on child–machine interactions [14, 60], with only a limited number
exploring how LLMs can facilitate interactions between parents and

Conference acronym ’XX, June 03–05, 2018, Woodstock, NY

children in storytelling or time management [63, 64]. Even fewer
studies specifically address the parent-child interaction challenges
in the math tutoring process. This gap is particularly important
in the tutoring of math word problems, where parental support
for elementary-level math education is critical, and LLMs often
struggle with accuracy [25]. Therefore, it may not be appropriate to
develop an LLM system for children to interact with independently.
We need a system that can effectively facilitate the parent-child
interaction during the math word problem tutoring process.
Parental involvement in education is a critical factor in children’s
academic performance. Parental tutoring extends academic experiences beyond the classroom and provides essential support for
cognitive development and achievement with targeted feedback
[20]. Parental tutoring has largely been treated as a general construct, without attending to subject-specific differences [3, 13, 49].
Yet, the nature and impact of parental assistance may differ substantially based on the subject domain. In math education, for example,
math word problems represent one of the most cognitively complex components, as they require students to integrate linguistic
comprehension with mathematical reasoning [58]. Especially in the
upper elementary grades, solving word problems fosters conceptual
understanding, analytical and creative thinking, as well as the application of mathematics to real-life situations [2, 10, 52]. Parental
tutoring in this area, therefore, requires substantial alignment between school-based instruction and home-based practice, and many
parents find tutoring math word problems to be one of the most
challenging tasks. Significant gaps exist between adult perspectives
and children’s understanding of math word problems, leading to
tensions and conflicts that can further diminish children’s interest
and motivation in learning math [33, 41].
We propose an LLM system that aims to facilitate the interactions
between parents and their children. We hope to have a system that
can recognize the pedagogical value of parental involvement but
also streamlines parental tutoring strategies. Borrowing ideas from
the education field, a scaffolding framework is helpful to facilitate
the system design. Overall, we aim to address what an LLM system
can empower parental tutoring in math word problems. To reach
this goal, we ask three questions:
• What challenges often emerge in parent–child tutoring that
an LLM system can address?
• How can the LLM system be implemented to support parent–child tutoring?
• Can our LLM system facilitate parent–child tutoring interaction?
We conducted a formative study to first establish in the Chinese home tutoring context to identify locally common math word
problem-solving procedures, common challenges in parental tutoring, and seven identified strategies that were commonly adopted by
the parents. We then developed a system, named ParaTutor, using
multi-agent chatbots with those design goals and suggestions from
the formative study. This design supports a customized mode for
interactive problem-solving for the given parent-child dyad to address personalized parental tutoring styles and learning needs. We
then conducted a user study with 23 pairs of parents and children to
evaluate this prototype. This evaluation demonstrates that parental
tutoring is more effective when the tutoring strategies selected

2 RELATED WORK
2.1 Challenges in Parent–Child Math Tutoring
Parental tutoring refers to home-based instructional support in
which parents assist children with academic tasks such as homework [48]. In this setting, parents often help structure the learning process by setting the pace, providing feedback, and deciding
when to offer assistance. However, parent–child tutoring is not always straightforward. Although familiarity can enable personalized
support [34], tutoring can also become tense or frustrating when
parents and children differ in how they understand or approach
a problem. Parents and children often rely on different problemsolving strategies [11], and parents are not always subject-matter
experts. Compared with trained educators, they may lack up-todate knowledge or effective teaching strategies [35]. As a result,
misunderstandings, mismatched explanations, and difficulty maintaining shared focus can make parent-child tutoring emotionally
and interactionally demanding.
These challenges become especially salient in cognitively demanding tasks such as math word problems [24, 53]. Unlike routine
exercises, math word problems require learners to interpret narrative descriptions, map quantities to symbolic representations, and
maintain intermediate reasoning steps in working memory [47, 65].
They also unfold across multiple phases, such as understanding the
situation, planning a solution, executing calculations, and reflecting
on results [22, 40]. Because each phase places different cognitive
demands on learners, parents must continuously adjust how and
when they provide support. In practice, however, it can be difficult
to determine what kind of help is appropriate at a given moment,
how much guidance to provide, and how to keep children engaged
without taking over the task. This challenge motivates us to address
parent-child tutoring through the context of math word problems.

2.2

Scaffolding in Interactive Learning Systems

Scaffolding refers to guided support provided by a more experienced
partner to help a learner accomplish tasks that would otherwise
be difficult to complete independently [43, 54]. Rather than simply
giving answers, scaffolding involves regulating when and how support is introduced, adapting explanations to the learner’s current
understanding, and gradually withdrawing assistance as competence develops [50]. In this sense, scaffolding functions not only
as a pedagogical strategy but also as an interactional mechanism
that shapes turn-taking, pacing, attention, and the distribution of
responsibility during problem solving.
Prior HCI research has increasingly operationalized scaffolding
in interactive systems. Existing systems use structured prompts
[26, 28], decomposition [1, 23] and annotation [26] to support users
in tasks such as writing [8, 19], programming [32], literature review
[38], and healthcare communication [18]. These systems show that
scaffolding can help users externalize intermediate structure, stay
oriented in complex tasks, and receive context-sensitive support
without immediately giving final answers. However, most of this

ParaTutor: LLM-Mediated Parent–Child Tutoring through Role-Separated Scaffolding Interface in Real Time

work focuses on single-user learning or human-AI collaboration.
By contrast, parent-child tutoring is an asymmetric and relational
setting, where scaffolding must support parents in guiding children’s reasoning across different tutoring phases without taking
over the problem-solving process. In response, we examine how
scaffolding can be organized across tutoring phases in parent-child
learning, and present a system that supports parents in providing
timely, adaptive guidance while keeping children actively engaged
in problem solving.

2.3

LLM-Mediated Parental Tutoring

LLMs have increasingly been introduced into educational settings
as conversational tutors and learning companions [5, 6]. Their ability to generate step-by-step explanations, respond to follow-up
questions, and adapt content dynamically enables more flexible
support than earlier rule-based systems [30, 37, 51]. In mathematics education, LLM-based systems show promise in interpreting
problem statements, producing reasoning steps, and offering structured guidance [21, 27, 62]. These capabilities make AI-supported
tutoring increasingly feasible for everyday learning contexts.
Recent HCI research has also begun to explore AI-supported
learning in family settings. Prior systems have examined parentchild co-reading, storytelling, joint media engagement, and in-home
learning support, showing that AI can facilitate shared interaction, co-creation, and communication between parents and children
[12, 16, 29, 63]. This work establishes the home as an important
site of AI-mediated learning and positions AI as a third party in
family educational interactions. However, most of these systems
focus on child-agent dialogue [9, 56, 57], co-experiences [15, 55, 63],
or content engagement [4, 46], rather than on how instructional
support is coordinated within parent-child tutoring itself. A smaller
body of work has considered AI systems that support parents, for
example by helping them access child-related information, navigate
care or advocacy decisions, or participate in children’s learning activities [7, 39, 59]. While these studies highlight the importance
of parental involvement, they do not directly address how AI can
support parents in tutoring, such as interpreting a child’s understanding, deciding when to intervene, and adjusting support as
problem solving unfolds. Our work presents a phase-based LLMmediated tutoring system that supports parents in guiding children
through math word problems with timely scaffolding while keeping
children actively engaged in problem solving.

FORMATIVE STUDY

To address RQ1, we conducted a formative study to understand how
parent and child roles are organized during math word problem
tutoring, and what challenges disrupt this role structure.

3.1

Participants and Procedure

We recruited eleven parents (P1–P11) and two experienced primary
school teachers (T1, T2). Participants were recruited through online parent communities, including Xiaohongshu, a popular social
media platform in China, as well as local after-school institutions.
All participating parents were primary caregivers of children in
Grades 4–6 (ages 10–12) and regularly involved in home tutoring.
The two teachers had 26 and 38 years of teaching experience in

upper-elementary mathematics. Appendix A presents background
information about the parent participants and their children.
We conducted semi-structured interviews with teachers and parents to examine instructional practices in school and home settings.
Each interview lasted for 20 to 30 minutes. Interview questions included how tutoring was structured across problem-solving phases,
how problem-solving responsibilities were distributed between parents and children, and where difficulties or breakdowns occurred.
We also explored the strategies parents used to guide learning and
how interaction unfolded in practice, including how turns were
coordinated, how intervention timing was decided, and how artifacts (e.g., drawings, phone searches, scratch paper) were used to
maintain shared understanding. All interviews were audio-recorded
with consent and anonymized for analysis.

3.2

Data Analysis

We conducted a thematic analysis. Two researchers independently
reviewed the transcripts and generated initial codes capturing phenomena such as role responsibilities, intervention timing, phase
transitions, artifact use, and strategy mismatch. Through iterative
comparison and discussion, the researchers developed a shared
codebook with definitions and representative excerpts. Discrepancies were resolved in team meetings to ensure analytic consistency.
Our analysis aimed to catalog tutoring strategies and identify recurring breakdown patterns and coordination mechanisms in the
parental tutoring process. The notion of instructional asymmetry
emerged through iterative coding of role distribution and phase
transitions.

3.3

Findings

Across interviews, we identified recurring patterns in how parents
and children maintain distinct instructional roles during math tutoring, as well as situations where these roles become blurred or
disrupted. Overall, effective tutoring relied on parents guiding the
learning process while children remained responsible for reasoning
and solution generation.
3.3.1 Distinct Roles Are Maintained Through Staged Tutoring. Across
both teacher and parent interviews, word problem solving was described as a process that embodies three recurrent phases, namely
understanding, calculation, and summarization, and aligns
with classical problem solving models [40]. Each phase establishes
a temporary focus of attention and a shared expectation of what
counts as progress, thereby regulating when reasoning advances
and how instructional authority and responsibility are distributed
in interaction.
The understanding phase begins with the parent and child
working toward shared interpretive alignment. As T1 stressed, students are required to "read the question three times without adding
or omitting any words" before attempting computation. Parents
similarly reported authorizing phase transitions, deciding whether
comprehension was adequate to proceed (P5, P10, P11). Some described asking children to restate the problem in their own words
before any calculation (P4, P7). Calculation typically proceeds
after shared understanding is established. When errors occur, T2
reported that students are instructed to "return to the question

Conference acronym ’XX, June 03–05, 2018, Woodstock, NY

instead of correcting numbers blindly", thus going back to the understanding phase. Parents described similar strategies (P2). The
summarization phase extends beyond answer verification. Teachers and parents both described prompting children to articulate
underlying principles and consider how the method applies to other
similar problems (P3, P6).
Based on accounts from teachers and parents, math word problem solving is organized around these clearly defined stages. These
phase boundaries are actively enforced rather than implicitly assumed. Parents often require children to proceed through these
stages in sequence, using them as an interactional script to guide
instruction and regulate problem-solving behavior. This staged
progression enables parents to assert pedagogical authority by directing how the problem should be approached.
3.3.2 Distinct Roles Are Maintained Through Scaffolding Strategies.
We identified recurring scaffolding moves that parents use. Rather
than isolated techniques, these strategies shape turn-taking, pacing,
and the distribution of responsibility during problem solving.
Understanding Phase. Task simplification breaks down lengthy
or complex problem statements into smaller, more manageable
units. Parents noted that children often struggle with questions
containing dense wording (P2, P5, P7, P9). As P2 described, "For
example, use a pen to circle parts of the problem on paper, so they
can gradually understand it better and eventually grasp it fully".
He further explained, "Sometimes I would ask him to read out the
question part by part, according to the commas." These actions
make the structure of the problem more visible to the parent and
child, supporting shared understanding before moving forward.
Situational guidance refers to mapping abstract numerical relationships onto familiar real-life contexts. As P7 explained, "If a child
does not know much about the specific scenarios or applications, he
may not understand the meaning." Several parents also described
drawing diagrams to clarify relationships. As P3 noted, "If the question is too long, I will draw it so my child can see the relationships."
However, some parents found visualization challenging. P2 admitted, "I know drawing can really help, but I’m not good at it." Parents
sometimes replaced the original problem context with more common examples. As P8 illustrated, "For certain questions... we will use
real-life examples with food. . . to help him understand better."
Calculation Phase. The most common strategy was feedback
and correction. This strategy can effectively prevent children from
forming wrong cognitive patterns due to deviations in thinking
when solving problems for the first time (P4). Especially when
children have relatively weak foundations, this step-by-step explanation method is particularly important (P10). During these
interactions, parents monitored not only correctness but also their
child’s understanding, adjusting subsequent explanations based on
the child’s responses (P7). However, this approach was not universal. Two parents preferred allowing children to complete all
problems before conducting an overall review (P2, P6), believing
that a holistic assessment better revealed recurring reasoning patterns (P2).
A second strategy involved is demonstration and imitation. Four
parents (P2, P3, P8, P11) described modeling a solution process
when independent reasoning stalled. As P8 explained, he would
first demonstrate how to solve a similar problem and then modify

its values. Parents who adopted this strategy viewed imitation as
a means of stabilizing reasoning patterns (P11). However, P4 expressed concern that their problem-solving approach might differ
from the teachers’ approach. As a regulation strategy, demonstration allows parents to take greater control temporarily and then
return the task to the child when reasoning needs support.
Several parents adopted an independent-thinking strategy to redistribute problem-solving responsibility. Rather than immediately
identifying errors, they prompted children to re-examine their reasoning. As P5 stated firmly, "children must think first." Others described asking children to articulate their solution process before
offering help (P2, P6). Parents viewed this strategy as a way to
strengthen independent reasoning. However, they also acknowledged that its effectiveness depends on the child’s competence. As
P4 noted, even after prompting, some children struggle to progress,
requiring renewed intervention. This strategy shifts more responsibility to the child while maintaining parental supervision.
Summarization Phase. The first was review and consolidation,
in which children revisited incorrect problems and articulated underlying principles. As P5 explained, he emphasized the importance
of understanding "the reasons behind each type of mistake rather
than just memorizing the specific errors." Rather than treating mistakes as isolated events, parents asked their children to review the
question to stabilize reasoning patterns across similar problems (P3,
P4, P5, P6, P7, P9, P10, P11). However, several noted that systematic
review could be too time-consuming (P4, P9).
A second strategy involved practical application. Some parents
extended solutions into familiar real-life contexts, such as paper
cutting (P3) or buying fruit (P8), encouraging children to apply
learned methods beyond the original problem. By connecting abstract reasoning to lived experience, parents reinforced reasoning
coherence.
3.3.3 Distinct Roles Break Down Under Recurring Tutoring Challenges. Parents often encounter challenges when assisting their
children with math. They tend to forget important concepts, use
outdated problem-solving methods, and have difficulty communicating effectively. We summarize the key challenges below:
C1: Limited Visualization Proficiency. Although many parents acknowledged the importance of visual representations in
helping their children understand math word problems, several
noted difficulties in expressing mathematical relationships accurately through drawings. P2 admitted, “I know drawing can really help, but I’m not good at it.” Parents were generally aware of
whether their drawings were effective. Many could observe that
their children became even more confused with their visual explanations.
C2: Knowledge Gaps. Some parents (P2, P11) encounter knowledge gaps. They have to put in additional effort to align their understanding with the educational content being taught in schools.
P2 said, ” I also have some concepts that are not clear, and I have to
look them up because it has been so long. When I am tutoring my
child, I have to look up the book myself or search on my phone.” 6
out of 11 parents reported that when tutoring their children, they
tend to use the methods of solving equations that they are familiar
with, which are far beyond the methods taught at school (P1, P2, P4,
P5, P6, P7). “ My child still prefers the method taught by the teacher,

which sometimes I find difficult to accept. Sometimes, I can only use
my original method to explain it to him.” P2 added. This shows that
parents have certain difficulties in understanding and accepting
new problem-solving methods, which can confuse children. For
example, Parent P5 worries that exposing their child to various
methods could lead to confusion. This concern highlights parents’
frustration with the differences between their own methods and
what is taught in schools, as well as their fear that using a different
problem-solving method could confuse their child.
C3: Communication Conflict. When parents help their children with math, they often encounter significant communication
challenges (P3, P9, P10). Parent P10 observed that children often
think differently from their parents. This difference can make it hard
for children to understand their parents’ intentions and methods.
Many parents believed that they had explained things clearly, but
children remained confused and struggled to follow their reasoning.
These conflicts largely stem from parents’ difficulty in accurately gauging their children’s zone of knowledge, sometimes compounded by parents’ resistance to or distrust of unfamiliar problemsolving approaches. Although some parents, such as P3 and P10,
acknowledged that their own methods might not be very smart,
they still viewed them as more reliable than alternative approaches.
Although some parents claimed respect for their children’s choices,
their attitudes revealed an underlying superiority. Specifically, they
tended to honor their familiar approaches and remained cautious,
if not skeptical, toward potentially more efficient or innovative
strategies brought about by their children (P10).
Conflicts also arise from parental observation of learning habits
or attitudes. For example, Parent P9 said, “Sometimes he shows no
attitude, no patience, or carelessness, and starts answering questions
without fully reading them." When children display impatience or
carelessness in their studies, parents may respond harshly instead of
offering further guidance and corrections. This approach can lead to
resistance from the children and further strain the communication
between them.

PARATUTOR SYSTEM

Instead of directly delivering answers to the child, ParaTutor is
designed to support the parent’s tutoring role while keeping the
child actively engaged in reasoning. The system therefore provides
different forms of support to parents and children, helping parents
guide tutoring without bypassing parental involvement or taking
over the child’s reasoning process. This section answers RQ2.

4.1

Design Requirements

Our design is grounded in the formative finding that successful
parent–child tutoring depends on maintaining distinct roles during interaction, in which parents guide the tutoring process and
children remain engaged in reasoning. We therefore derive the
following requirements directly from the recurring challenges identified in the formative study.
R1: Provide Child-Facing Visual Grounding for Problem
Understanding. When parents struggle to clearly externalize mathematical relationships (C1), shared understanding can break down
during early problem solving. The system should therefore generate
child-facing visual representations that help make key entities and

relationships visible, reducing reliance on parents’ drawing skills
and supporting shared problem understanding.
R2: Provide Parent-Facing Tutoring Guidance That Adapts
to Different Solution Methods. When parents encounter knowledge gaps or differences in solution methods (C2), they may find it
difficult to explain the problem or guide the child effectively. The
system should therefore provide parent-facing, strategy-based guidance that helps parents support children’s reasoning and adapt to
different valid ways of solving the problem.
R3: Support Parent–Child Alignment During Tutoring Progression. When communication difficulties arise between parents
and children (C3), shared understanding can become difficult to
maintain. The system should therefore support staged progression with shared confirmation, helping parents and children stay
aligned, slowing down misunderstanding, and reducing premature
correction or emotional escalation.

4.2

Role-Separated Interfaces and Interaction
Flows

4.2.1 Role-Separated Interfaces. Guided by these requirements,
ParaTutor separates system support by user role. Rather than presenting explanations directly to the child, the system supports parents with tutoring guidance while providing children with visual
grounding for problem understanding. This design helps parents
guide tutoring, helps children remain engaged in reasoning, and
reduces the risk that system support bypasses parental involvement.
As shown in Fig. 1, the workspace is divided into two synchronized panels with different functions. The Parent Operation Panel
(C) provides strategy-based guidance that helps parents interpret
the problem, choose suitable scaffolding strategies, and support
the child across tutoring phases. The Child Operation Panel
(D) provides visual grounding of key entities and relationships in
the problem, helping children build understanding without revealing procedural solution steps or final answers. A shared phase
progress control (E) supports parent–child alignment by requiring confirmation before moving to the next stage, helping both
users remain synchronized during tutoring.
Through role-separated surfaces and staged progression, the
interface provides different forms of support to parents and children
while preserving parent-led tutoring. The system operates as a
tutoring scaffold rather than as a parallel instructor for the child.
4.2.2 Interaction Flows. ParaTutor organizes tutoring into three
phases: Understanding, Calculation, and Summarization. For
each problem, the parent begins by selecting scaffolding moves and
entering the word problem. The system then provides two forms
of support for the current phase: child-facing visual grounding and
parent-facing scaffolding prompts. The parent uses this support to
guide the interaction, while the child works through the problem
with visual support and dialogue. Within each phase, parent and
child interact iteratively, and the parent may query the system on
demand for additional help. Moving to the next phase requires joint
confirmation, which helps both users remain aligned and prevents
premature advancement. This design preserves parent-led tutoring
while keeping the child engaged in reasoning. Fig. 4 in Appendix B
provides an overview of this flow.

4.3

Phase-Gated Scaffolding Architecture

Although ParaTutor is implemented using multiple agents, the architectural novelty lies in how the system coordinates role-separated
support across tutoring phases rather than in the agent decomposition itself. As shown in Fig. 2, ParaTutor consists of a Main Agent,
a set of phase-specific scaffolding agents, and a visual grounding module. At each interaction turn, the Main Agent maintains
a shared internal state that stores the current word problem, the
active tutoring phase, recent interaction history, the selected tutoring mode, the child’s intermediate progress, and the current
visualization state. Based on this shared state, the Main Agent invokes only the scaffolding agents relevant to the current phase, and
does so sequentially rather than in parallel. For example, in the understanding phase, it prioritizes agents for task simplification and

situational guidance, whereas in the calculating phase it prioritizes
agents for mistake detection, feedback correction, and prompts that
encourage independent thinking. Each scaffolding agent takes the
updated shared state as input and returns a structured tutoring
move, including both its instructional intent and its parent-facing
surface realization. The Main Agent then integrates these sequential
outputs into a single response shown in the parent-facing interface.
The same shared state also drives child-facing support. When the
current problem state contains entities, quantities, or relations that
should be externalized, the Main Agent invokes a visual grounding
module implemented as a rule-based state-to-diagram generation
pipeline. Specifically, the module reads a structured problem state
consisting of extracted entities, numerical attributes, and relational
constraints, maps these elements to graphical primitives and layout
rules, and then renders the resulting diagram as an interactive visualization. Because the visualization is generated from the evolving
shared state rather than revealed as a worked solution, the childfacing interface supports interpretation and representation of the
problem while withholding procedural solution steps that remain
reserved for parent-facing scaffolding. Finally, phase transitions are
controlled by explicit rule-based conditions over the shared internal
state. Rather than allowing the LLM to decide when to move on,
ParaTutor advances between the understanding, calculating, and
summarizing phases only when predefined completion criteria are

USER STUDY

RQ3 examines how ParaTutor’s role-separated and phase-gated
LLM support reshapes parent-child tutoring interaction in practice. We evaluate ParaTutor not only as a tutoring aid, but as an
interaction guide for parent-child tutoring. In particular, we examine whether it changes how support is delivered, how parents
remain involved, and how children engage in reasoning across
problem-solving phases.

5.1

Experiment Design

Each parent-child dyad completed four tutoring Sessions at home.
In each Session, the parent tutored their child using one assigned
tutoring Mode to complete one math Test. To remove order effects,
we used a two-level Latin Square design [42] to counterbalance
tutoring Modes and math Tests. The final Modes and Tests combinations are reported in Appendix E.
We compared four tutoring modes: Conventional Tutoring (Mode
A), DeepSeek Tutoring (Mode B), ParaTutor Aligned Tutoring (Mode
C), and ParaTutor Complementary Tutoring (Mode D). Mode A
served as the no-AI baseline. Mode B provided support from a
general conversational LLM that returned explanations, hints, and
worked examples in response to queries from either the parent or
the child, rather than delivering role-structured support. Modes C
and D used ParaTutor’s role-separated scaffolding support, with
Mode C aligned to parents’ existing practices and Mode D introducing less familiar, complementary strategies. This design allowed us
to compare not only whether AI helps, but also how different ways
of delivering LLM support reshape parent-child tutoring interaction.
The four test sets (Test 1-4) were designed by the two school
teachers in the formative study to assess the same underlying mathematical logic through different real-world scenarios (Appendix C).

Participants and Procedure

We recruited parent-child dyads through Xiaohongshu and local
after-school institutions between August 2024 and May 2025. Eligible participants were primary caregivers of children aged 10-12
who were actively involved in home tutoring and had access to
an internet-connected computer for the remote study. The final
analysis included 23 parent-child dyads, labeled E1-E23, with each
family receiving 50 RMB for participation. Table 3 summarizes participant demographics, with parents averaging 39.1 years of age,
including 6 fathers and 17 mothers. Most held a Bachelor’s degree,
and families varied in perceived tutoring difficulty, children’s math
performance, and prior familiarity with LLMs.
Before the study, parents attended an online orientation session
covering system use and study procedures. Informed e-consent
was obtained. During the study, parents were asked to accompany
their child throughout all sessions and prepare basic materials such
as paper and pens. Each dyad completed four tutoring sessions.
Each session was limited to 30 minutes, with a 5-minute break
between sessions. After each session, parents submitted a brief
questionnaire reporting the tutoring strategy used, time spent, and
photographs of the child’s handwritten solutions (Appendix F).
Upon completion of all four sessions, each parent participated in
a semi-structured interview. The interview focused on (1) how
the system influenced their tutoring approach to math problemsolving, (2) the effectiveness of different tutoring strategies, and (3)
the overall user experience. The full interview guide is provided in
Appendix D.
The analysis of the parental interview scripts followed the same
procedure as in the formative study. Three researchers reviewed
the entire meeting transcript to gain a general understanding. Two
researchers then coded the transcript separately, categorizing the
content. Any discrepancies were discussed with a senior researcher.

diagram—he felt like he was figuring it out himself.” This interactive
format kept children focused and made learning feel like a shared
discovery.
LLM as a Mediator between parent and children. LLMs,
working as a “fair” third party in this process, also helped resolve

Even those who knew how to solve word problems found that
the system offered fresh perspectives. For instance, in a problem
about planting rose bushes along the edges of a square garden, the

different problem-solving approaches. As E11 reflected, “The Mode
D helped us realize that different paths could lead to the same solution.
It turns out we were both right—we just saw it differently.” These
experiences promoted mutual understanding and made learning a
more enjoyable process at home.

5.3.2 ParaTutor Keeps Parents in the Instructional Loop. Structured guidance helped parents lead the tutoring process. Compared to Deepseek, ParaTutor provided a more step-by-step, structured, and engaging tutoring experience (E7, E10, E12, E19). E19
compared the two systems, “With Deepseek, it just gives you the
whole solution at once. But with ParaTutor, it breaks things down step
by step, so that we can really work through it together. E5 echoed
this sentiment, “With ParaTutor, I finally understood how to guide
him step by step. It’s like I could see the logic unfolding with him.”
Similarly, E7 noted, “With ParaTutor, we could stop at each step and
talk about it. Deepseek just gave the whole answer.”
In addition, many (E10, E12, E20, E21) noted a shift in their role,
from passive observers to active participants. As E12 described, “I
felt like I was doing it with him (the child) using ParaTutor. With
Deepseek, I was just watching.” This greater involvement not only
helped parents better support their children but also strengthened
the sense of collaboration in this process. E10 echoed this point,
stating, “ParaTutor helped me ask better questions. Deepseek didn’t
really teach—it just showed the solution.” Taken together, this design
of a structured output not only improved comprehension, but also
promoted shared thinking and deeper engagement between the
parent and the child.
Parent-facing language reduced tension during correction.
In addition to providing practical assistance, the system also influences the emotional dynamics of parent-child interactions during
tutoring. Many parents noted that the system’s tone and language
helped to reduce tension and conflict, fostering a calmer and more
supportive learning environment. For example, E23 shared, “Now
that my child is in adolescence, she often finds me annoying. But with
the system, things feel less tense—it creates some distance so she can
think without us getting into arguments.”
To support emotional buffering, the system includes design features like gentle reminders for parents, suggestions for positive
reinforcement, and neutral phrasing. For example, sentence starters
such as “You’re on the right track,” “let’s think it through together”
or “That’s okay, let’s try another way”) help parents maintain a
constructive tone, especially when children struggle or make mistakes. Parents, like E15, noted that the system’s phrasing made a
significant difference: instead of saying “wrong,” it would suggest
“let’s try a different way,” which helped both parent and child stay
calm. This indicates that emotionally supportive language can enhance persistence and reduce frustration, fostering greater learner
confidence and encouraging problem-solving without anxiety.
Compared with general conversational LLM support, ParaTutor
kept parents centrally involved by making guidance something
they delivered and adapted in interaction, rather than something
they merely retrieved from the model.
