                                                                                              A BSTRACT
                                                 Educators face significant challenges in creating engaging, personalized assignments that accom-
                                                 modate students’ diverse interests and cognitive abilities. Traditional one-size-fits-all assignments
                                                 frequently lead to decreased student engagement and increased reliance on unethical practices such
                                                 as plagiarism. To address these challenges, we present Taklif.AI, a platform that leverages Large
                                                 Language Models (LLMs) to automatically generate personalized assignments tailored to individ-
                                                 ual student interests. Unlike existing AI-powered educational platforms that personalize based on
                                                 academic performance metrics alone, Taklif.AI incorporates students’ extracurricular interests and
                                                 cultural contexts into the assignment generation process through a structured prompt engineering
                                                 pipeline with input and output guardrails. The platform employs a serverless architecture on AWS
                                                 with Next.js, using Llama 3.3 70B as the primary LLM via LiteLLM for multi-provider load balancing
                                                 and LangChain for prompt orchestration. We describe the system architecture, the prompt design
                                                 methodology, and the guardrails framework that ensures output quality. Preliminary user acceptance
                                                 testing with 68 participants (65 students and 3 educators) indicates positive reception, with 84%
                                                 of participants rating the personalization feature as beneficial. We discuss the platform’s current
                                                 capabilities and limitations, and outline directions for rigorous empirical evaluation of learning
                                                 outcomes.

                                        1   Introduction

                                        The global expansion of higher education has brought unprecedented diversity into classrooms, with students varying
                                        widely in cultural backgrounds, cognitive abilities, and personal interests. Meeting the needs of such heterogeneous
                                        learners has become one of the central challenges facing educators worldwide, prompting growing interest in technology-
                                        enhanced approaches to teaching and learning.
                                        Among recent technological developments, Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as particularly transformative.
                                        These models demonstrate remarkable capabilities in understanding and generating human-like text, opening new
                                        possibilities for adapting educational content to individual learners at scale. Their potential to reshape how instructional
                                        materials are designed and delivered has attracted considerable attention from researchers and practitioners alike.
                                        Despite these advances, assignment practices in most educational institutions have remained largely static. Conventional
                                        approaches typically employ uniform tasks that offer little personal relevance to individual students. Research has
                                        shown that such one-size-fits-all assignments frequently result in low motivation, superficial learning, and diminished
                                        academic performance [1]. When students perceive tasks as monotonous or disconnected from their interests, they are
                                        also more likely to resort to unethical practices such as plagiarism [2], and they tend to achieve weaker comprehension
                                        of the underlying material [3].

Personalized learning offers a compelling response to these challenges. By aligning educational tasks with learners’
interests, abilities, and preferred styles, personalization has been shown to significantly enhance both motivation and
learning outcomes [4, 1]. However, manually crafting individualized assignments for every student is impractical at
scale, creating a clear opportunity for automation through AI.
In this paper, we present Taklif.AI, a platform that leverages LLMs to automatically generate personalized assignments
tailored to each student’s extracurricular interests. The system aims to bridge the gap between the established benefits
of personalized learning and the practical constraints educators face in delivering it.

1.1   Problem Statement

Instructors face challenges in creating engaging, personalized assignments catering to students’ diverse interests and
cognitive abilities. Traditional, one-size-fits-all tasks often fail to motivate students, leading to reduced engagement
and reliance on unethical practices like cheating [2]. While personalized assignments can improve comprehension and
performance [1, 4], designing them manually is labor-intensive and time-consuming, making it difficult for instructors
to balance learning objectives with engaging content at scale.

1.2   Scope and Limitations

Taklif.AI generates customized exercises tailored to students’ self-reported extracurricular interests. While the platform
has been primarily tested on programming and mathematics assignments, its LLM-based approach is not inherently
restricted to these domains. The platform does not evaluate or assess student submissions, nor does it alter the
fundamental structure or core learning objectives of assignments. Evaluation of actual learning outcomes (e.g., grades,
comprehension, plagiarism rates) is beyond the scope of this paper and is planned as future work.

2     Related Work
The integration of AI into education has significantly advanced the personalization of learning experiences. This section
reviews prior work across four areas and identifies gaps addressed by Taklif.AI.

2.1   AI-Driven Personalization in Education

Numerous studies highlight the transformative role of AI in fostering personalized learning environments. Kamalov et
al. [5] underscore the educational potential of intelligent tutoring systems, while Escueta et al. [6] demonstrate that
technology-based personalized platforms positively influence learning outcomes across diverse contexts. Casal-Otero
et al. [7] provide a systematic review of AI literacy in K-12, noting both the potential and the ethical risks of AI in
education.
In higher education, Alamri et al. [8] investigate personalization in blended learning environments, and Shemshack and
Spector [9] provide a comprehensive analysis of personalization terminology in digital learning. While these studies
establish a strong foundation, they primarily focus on broad curricular personalization, offering limited insights into
assignment-level customization or dynamic interest-based adaptation.

2.2   Interest-Based Personalization

A growing body of research supports the alignment of learning materials with students’ personal interests. Iterbeke
et al. [4] conducted a large-scale experiment in mathematics education, contextualizing problems based on students’
self-reported interests (e.g., sports, music), finding a 14% performance improvement among low-achieving students.
Walkington [1] demonstrated that embedding algebra problems within interest-based contexts led to higher test scores
and longer task persistence. However, both studies confined their personalization to subject-specific or classroom
interests, neglecting broader out-of-school interests such as hobbies and cultural practices.
Expanding the scope, Laine et al. [10] explored inquiry-based mobile learning activities rooted in students’ hobbies,
reporting increases in curiosity and long-term retention. The theoretical foundation of interest-based personalization is
grounded in Self-Determination Theory [11], which emphasizes autonomy, competence, and relatedness as key drivers
of intrinsic motivation, and the Four-Phase Model of Interest Development [12], which suggests that situational interest
can mature into sustained individual interest through repeated exposure to meaningful content.
Despite these insights, existing studies largely overlook the integration of diverse sociocultural interests into personalized
learning, and none leverage LLMs for automated assignment-level personalization.

2.3       Adaptive Learning Technologies and Assignment Personalization

Walkington [1] developed an algorithm mapping students’ survey-reported interests to mathematics problem contexts.
In a randomized trial, students receiving interest-aligned problems achieved higher accuracy and longer task persistence.
However, this system depended on static interest profiles, failing to account for evolving preferences. Zhou et al.
[13] introduced Deep Interest Networks for modeling evolving user preferences in e-commerce, a technique with
potential educational applications. Klašnja-Milicević et al. [14] showed that hybrid recommender systems improved
learning material relevance, though these systems operate at the content recommendation level rather than generating
personalized assignments.
In higher education, Alhazmi et al. [3] explored choice-based assignment models, allowing students to select projects
aligned with their interests. While effective in enhancing motivation, these models required significant instructor effort
and did not automate the personalization process.

2.4       Equity, Cultural Responsiveness, and Academic Integrity

Gay [15] advocates for culturally responsive pedagogy that acknowledges students’ cultural identities. Lee [16]
demonstrated that aligning science tasks with minority students’ cultural practices can reduce achievement gaps. Ngo
[2] found that customizing programming assignments based on students’ hobbies reduced plagiarism rates in a cohort
of 200 students, suggesting that personal relevance can foster authenticity and ethical behavior. Few existing systems,
however, explicitly integrate equity-driven personalization strategies at the assignment level.

          1. Web Layer: A Next.js [17] application providing the user interface for assignment submission, interest

             AWS S3 [21] for file storage.
          4. Third-Party AI Layer: LangChain [22] for prompt orchestration, LiteLLM [23] for multi-provider load
             balancing, and LangSmith [24] for evaluation and logging.

3.2   LLM Provider Selection

After comparative analysis of providers including OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Mistral AI, and Meta (via Groq &
Together AI), Llama 3.3 70B was selected as the primary model. Table 2 summarizes the key selection criteria, including
two widely used LLM benchmarks: MMLU [25] (Massive Multitask Language Understanding), which tests knowledge
across 57 academic subjects, and HumanEval [26], which measures code generation proficiency via Python function
synthesis. The choice was motivated by three factors: (1) its open-source license, enabling transparency and future
fine-tuning; (2) strong benchmark performance in multilingual tasks, code generation, and mathematical reasoning; and
(3) a 128K-token context window sufficient for processing full assignment documents.
LiteLLM provides vendor-agnostic routing across Groq (optimized for speed) and Together AI (offering a free evaluation
tier), with automatic failover if a provider becomes unavailable.

3.3   Processing Pipeline

The assignment personalization pipeline (Figure 3) proceeds through five stages:

      1. Event Parsing: Extracts the task type (personalization or simplification) and allocates threads dynamically
         based on available Lambda memory.
      2. Input Guardrails: Validates student interest relevance via LLM-powered checks (Figure 4) and verifies
         assignment content integrity for file-based submissions.
      3. LLM Processing: LangChain constructs structured prompts; LiteLLM routes API calls to the optimal provider
         based on availability, cost, and latency.
      4. Output Guardrails: Validates AI-generated content for safety, relevance, and educational alignment (Fig-
         ure 5).
      5. Storage: Saves the generated assignment and logs metadata (model name, token usage, response time) to
         DynamoDB.

3.4   Prompt Design

Prompt engineering is central to Taklif.AI’s personalization quality. We employ six specialized prompts, each assigned
a distinct LLM role and task (Table 3). All prompts follow a consistent four-part structure:

      1. Role Definition: Assigns a persona (e.g., “Learning Experience Designer”) to guide tone and style.
      2. Task Specification: Defines the objective precisely to minimize ambiguity.
      3. Context and Constraints: Provides rules such as formatting requirements, length limits, and exclusion
         criteria.
      4. Output Formatting: Enforces structured output (e.g., Markdown, JSON) for downstream integration.

We adopt zero-shot prompting (no in-context examples) to maximize generalizability across assignment types. Prompts
are iteratively refined using LangSmith’s evaluation tools to measure coherence, personalization depth, and safety
compliance.

4     Evaluation

4.1   System Performance

Performance testing was conducted to assess the platform’s scalability under concurrent load. A scenario simulating
100 concurrent users generating assignments simultaneously yielded an average end-to-end response time of 30 seconds
per request (including LLM inference via Groq’s optimized Llama 3.3 70B endpoint), with no significant resource
bottlenecks detected. The serverless architecture (AWS Lambda with API Gateway) auto-scales to handle demand
spikes, and LiteLLM’s multi-provider routing ensures that if one LLM provider experiences latency, requests are
rerouted automatically.

4.2   Guardrails Effectiveness

During development and testing with approximately 200 test inputs, the guardrails exhibited the following behavior.
The interest guardrails rejected approximately 12% of submitted interests, including single-character inputs, profanity,
non-interest terms (e.g., “nothing,” “I don’t know”), and attempted prompt injection attacks (e.g., “Ignore all previous
instructions”). The assignment guardrails rejected approximately 8% of input documents, primarily due to content not
recognized as a valid academic assignment (e.g., lecture notes, course syllabi, or incomplete fragments). The output
guardrails flagged approximately 3% of LLM-generated assignments for containing content outside the assignment
scope or exhibiting potential hallucinated facts. We note that these figures are based on development-phase testing and
have not been validated through a formal audit with labeled ground truth.

4.3   User Acceptance Testing

We conducted preliminary user acceptance testing (UAT) with 68 participants: 65 undergraduate students and 3
educators from the Faculty of Information Technology at the Islamic University of Gaza. Each participant received a
2-minute platform introduction, was asked to personalize at least one assignment of their choice using the platform, and
then completed a structured feedback questionnaire. Sessions lasted approximately 12 minutes each.
The questionnaire included four 5-point Likert scale items (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree) and one
open-ended question:

      1. “The personalized assignment felt more engaging than a standard assignment.”
      2. “The platform was easy to use.”
      3. “The personalized output preserved the original learning objectives.”
      4. “I would use this platform for future assignments.”
      5. “What improvements would you suggest?” (open-ended)

Key findings:

       • 84% of participants (57/68) rated the personalization feature as beneficial (items 1 and 4 scored ≥ 4).
       • 91% of participants (62/68) rated the user interface as intuitive (item 2 scored ≥ 4).
       • Common qualitative suggestions included improvements to interface responsiveness and support for additional
         assignment formats (e.g., multiple-choice, essay prompts).

4.4   Limitations and Threats to Validity

We acknowledge several important limitations of the current evaluation:
No controlled experiment (internal validity). The UAT provides preliminary user feedback but does not constitute a
rigorous evaluation of learning outcomes. We have not measured whether personalized assignments lead to improved
grades, deeper comprehension, or reduced plagiarism compared to standard assignments.
Small and non-representative sample (external validity). The 68 participants were drawn from a single institution
and department. Results may not generalize to other educational contexts, disciplines, or student populations.
Construct validity. The UAT measures perceived benefit (self-reported engagement), not actual learning gains.
Students who report finding an assignment “more engaging” may not achieve better learning outcomes.
No output quality assessment. We have not conducted systematic human evaluation of the generated assignments’
pedagogical soundness, difficulty preservation, or personalization depth across a diverse corpus.

5     Discussion

The examples in Section 3.5 suggest that LLM-powered personalization can produce contextually coherent assignments
across domains. However, several design tensions merit discussion.
When personalization may not help. Highly abstract mathematical content (e.g., proofs in real analysis) may resist
contextual framing without trivializing the material. In our Set Theory example, the “Basketball” personalization
reframes set intersections as teams sharing players, which enriches the narrative but does not alter the mathematical
substance. Whether such framing genuinely aids comprehension, or merely adds stylistic embellishment, requires
empirical investigation.
Risk of distraction. There is a tension between engagement and focus: overly elaborate interest-based narratives could
distract students from the core learning objectives. The prompt’s constraint that content length “does not exceed a 50%
increment over the original” partially mitigates this, but the optimal balance remains an open question.
Standardized assessment compatibility. Personalized assignments produce different problem statements for different
students, which complicates grading rubrics and cross-student comparisons. This is a known challenge in personalized
education that Taklif.AI does not currently address.
Interest authenticity. The system relies on students’ self-reported interests, which may not reflect genuine engagement
drivers. Longitudinal tracking of interest evolution could improve personalization quality over time.


6     Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presented Taklif.AI, a platform that leverages LLMs to generate personalized college assignments based on
students’ extracurricular interests. The system employs a serverless architecture with a structured prompt engineering
pipeline, multi-provider LLM routing, and input/output guardrails to produce contextually relevant assignments that
preserve original learning objectives.
Our primary contributions are the design and implementation of an end-to-end platform that bridges the gap between the
established benefits of interest-based personalization and the practical constraints of delivering it at scale. Preliminary
user feedback from 68 participants suggests that the approach is perceived as beneficial, though as discussed in
Sections 4.4 and 5, rigorous empirical validation of learning outcomes remains to be conducted.


6.1   Future Work

The most important direction for future work is a controlled empirical evaluation. We plan to conduct a randomized
study comparing learning outcomes (test scores, assignment completion rates, self-reported engagement, and plagiarism
indicators) between students receiving personalized assignments via Taklif.AI and a control group receiving standard
assignments. This study would address the central limitation of the current work.
Additional planned developments include:
Output Quality Evaluation: Systematic expert evaluation of generated assignments for pedagogical soundness,
difficulty calibration, and personalization depth, potentially complemented by LLM-as-judge automated evaluation.
Technical Enhancements: Bulk assignment generation for entire class rosters; multi-modal support (images alongside
text); OCR-based image-to-assignment input; and PDF export of personalized assignments.
Platform Integration: Development of a browser extension for integration with learning management systems (e.g.,
Moodle, Google Classroom), enabling instructors to deploy personalized assignments directly within existing workflows.
Longitudinal Interest Modeling: Analysis of students’ interest evolution over time to recommend new areas of
exploration and adapt personalization strategies accordingly.
